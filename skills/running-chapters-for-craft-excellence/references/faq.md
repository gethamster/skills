# FAQ: Running Chapters for Craft Excellence

## Should the chapter lead be the line manager?

In the original Spotify description, yes: the chapter lead handled people development and salaries and also worked in a squad. Critics who worked at Spotify argue this left nobody clearly accountable for a squad's delivery. Both designs are in use, so choose deliberately and write down who owns delivery under your choice.

## How large should a chapter be?

Large enough that members benefit from a shared practice, and small enough that the lead can support everyone while still working in a squad. If a chapter grows past what one lead can support, split it by specialty or add a second lead. If a skill has only one or two people in a tribe, a company-wide guild usually serves them better.

## What is the difference between a chapter and a guild?

A chapter is local to one tribe, groups people with the same skill, and has a lead with defined responsibilities. A guild spans the whole company, is open to anyone who is interested, and has a coordinator rather than a manager. Chapters carry formal responsibility for craft and growth; guilds are for sharing knowledge.

## What happens when a chapter standard conflicts with what a squad wants?

Settle the rule in the chapter charter before it happens. A common split is that the squad's product owner sets priorities, the chapter owns a small set of quality standards, and a named person decides when the two disagree. Keeping standards few and explaining their reasoning makes conflicts rarer.

## How often should a chapter meet?

Regularly enough that members see each other's work and can raise problems early. What matters more is that each meeting has a real topic, such as a design review or a new tool. Cancel meetings without a topic rather than holding them out of habit.
