# FAQ: How to Translate Features into Benefits

## What is the difference between features and benefits?

A feature is what the product has or does, such as an integration or a report. A benefit is what the customer gets because of it, such as not rebuilding a report by hand every week. Copy that lists features makes the reader do the translation, and many readers will not. Good copy states the benefit and keeps the feature as the reason to believe it.

## How many times should I ask "so what"?

Ask until you reach an outcome the customer would name without prompting, which is often two or three rounds. Stop before the answer becomes generic enough to describe any product. Keep the intermediate answers, because bullets and feature sections often need the middle level.

## Should I ever lead with features?

Yes, for readers who judge a product on its specifications, such as engineers or specialists buying in their own field. They want the precise capability first and trust copy less when it opens with a broad promise. Lead with the feature and follow it immediately with the benefit.

## How do I write benefits for a highly technical product?

Start from the problem the technical feature solves in the user's day, described in their vocabulary. Name the feature precisely, then state the concrete result, such as fewer manual steps or a failure mode that no longer happens. Avoid translating technical terms your audience already uses into vaguer words.

## How do I know a benefit is specific enough?

Put the benefit on a competitor's page in your head. If it would still be true there, it is probably too general. A specific benefit names the task, the situation or the result in a way tied to how your feature works.

## Can one feature have different benefits for different audiences?

Often it does. An approval workflow can mean speed to the person submitting a request and control to the person approving it. Translate for the audience of the page you are writing, and keep the other versions in the table for other pages.
