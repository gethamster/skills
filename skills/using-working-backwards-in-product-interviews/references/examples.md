# Examples: Using Working Backwards in PM Interviews

## A product sense question about older adults

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a candidate is asked to design a product that helps older adults stay connected with family.

**Walkthrough:**

The candidate narrows the customer out loud: adults in their late seventies who live alone, own a tablet a grandchild set up, and find video calls confusing to start. The problem, in their terms, is that they wait for family to call because starting a call means finding the right app and the right button. Their current workaround is the landline.

The candidate describes the experience: the tablet shows photos of family members, and touching one starts a call. Working back, the minimum features are a simple launcher, one-touch calling and remote setup by a family member. The candidate raises the main risk unprompted, that family members may not answer on their phones, and proposes measuring the number of calls the older adult starts each week.

## A prioritization question at a B2B company

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a candidate is given three possible initiatives for a project-management tool and asked which to fund.

**Walkthrough:**

Instead of scoring the initiatives on a grid right away, the candidate asks which customer segment the company most needs to keep and learns it is agencies. For each initiative, the candidate states the agency problem it would solve and what agencies do today. One initiative solves a problem agencies already handle well with another tool, so it drops out.

The candidate picks between the remaining two by asking which would produce the more meaningful improvement for agencies, and closes with the one-sentence summary and the outcome metric that would show it worked.

## A behavioral question in an Amazon-style loop

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: an interviewer asks a candidate to describe a time they started from the customer and changed a plan because of it.

**Walkthrough:**

The candidate uses a prepared story in STAR form. The situation: a team planned a new dashboard. The task: the candidate owned the proposal. The action: before design began, the candidate wrote a one-page press release and could not name a customer who would care, so they interviewed several users and found the real problem was exporting data to finance.

The result: the dashboard was dropped and a simple export shipped instead, with the candidate stating the measured change in time spent on exports. The whole answer takes about two minutes, and the interviewer's follow-up questions probe the interviews.
