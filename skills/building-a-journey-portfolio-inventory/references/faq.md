# FAQ: Building a Customer Journey Inventory and Portfolio

## What is the difference between a customer journey inventory and a journey map?

A journey map describes one experience in detail: steps, touchpoints, emotions, and pain points. An inventory lists every journey by name with a few facts about each, such as owner, level, and evidence. The inventory points to maps where they exist and records journeys that have no map yet. Think of it as the catalog and the maps as the books.

## How detailed should each inventory entry be?

Keep each entry to a single row of short facts. The name, description, segment, stage, level, owner, source, evidence date, status, and known pain points are usually enough. Anything more detailed belongs in the journey's own map. A thin inventory is faster to build and easier to keep current.

## Should the inventory include journeys we have never researched?

Yes. Unmapped and suspected journeys are some of the most useful rows, because they show where the organization has blind spots. Mark them clearly with a status so nobody mistakes them for researched journeys. Prioritization can then decide which gaps are worth studying.

## Who should own the customer journey inventory?

Usually the customer experience or journey management lead, or whoever coordinates journey work across teams. The owner adds rows, runs the periodic audit, and settles naming and level questions. Individual journeys still have their own owners, who are responsible for the content of those journeys.

## How often should the inventory be updated?

Add rows whenever a product, channel, or policy change creates a new journey, and run a fuller audit on a fixed rhythm. At the audit, check whether each journey is current, used, unique, and still real, and archive the ones that are not. The right rhythm depends on how fast the business changes. A fast-moving product needs more frequent audits than a stable service.

## Can we build the inventory in a spreadsheet?

Yes, for a first pass a spreadsheet works well because everyone can read and edit it. As the portfolio grows and maps need linking to levels, owners, and evidence, a dedicated journey management tool can make maintenance easier. The structure of the inventory matters more than the tool. Start simple and move only when the spreadsheet becomes the bottleneck.
