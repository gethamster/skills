# Examples: Building a Customer Journey Inventory and Portfolio

## A software company finds three versions of onboarding

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a mid-size software company has journey maps from marketing, product, and customer success, stored in three different tools. Leadership wants to know which customer experiences to improve next year.

**Walkthrough:**

The CX lead gathers every artifact and finds three separate onboarding maps: marketing's covers the trial, product's covers first use of the app, and success's covers the kickoff call for paying accounts. The team keeps one row, "Get started with the product," notes the three sources, and flags that the boundaries conflict.

Collection sessions with sales and billing add journeys nobody had mapped, including "Add a colleague to my account" and "Understand my invoice." Ticket categories reveal a cluster of requests about exporting data before cancelling, which becomes a suspected journey. The review meeting confirms owners for most rows and leaves the invoice journey as an open ownership question.

## A utility inventories journeys from call-center data

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a regional utility has almost no journey maps, but its contact center codes every call by reason. The customer team wants a starting portfolio without commissioning new research first.

**Walkthrough:**

The team lists the call reason codes and groups them by customer goal, producing rows such as "Move house," "Set up a payment plan," and "Report an outage." Each row gets the status "known but unmapped" and cites the call data as its evidence.

Interviews with field operations and billing add journeys that rarely reach the contact center, such as scheduled meter replacement. The inventory shows that the "Move house" journey touches four departments and has no owner, which becomes the first candidate for deeper research.

## A retailer merges store and online journeys

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a retailer's store team and e-commerce team each keep their own list of customer journeys. Both lists include returns, but they describe different experiences.

**Walkthrough:**

The inventory owner renames both rows as "Return an item" and tags them by channel instead of keeping two separate journeys. The merged row records both teams as contributors and notes that customers who buy online and return in store fall between the two maps.

That cross-channel case becomes its own row with the status "suspected," supported by store staff reports. At the review, the two teams agree to share ownership of the research while one person is named as the accountable owner.
