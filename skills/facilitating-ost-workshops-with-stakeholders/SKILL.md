# OST Workshop Facilitation: Build the Tree Together

> OST workshop facilitation: run an opportunity solution tree workshop that builds a first tree with your trio and brings stakeholders into discovery.

## Overview

OST workshop facilitation is the skill of running a working session in which a team builds or reviews an [Opportunity Solution Tree](../../methods/opportunity-solution-tree/METHOD.md) together. An opportunity solution tree workshop turns individual knowledge into a shared picture: the outcome, the customer opportunities the team has heard, the target opportunity it chooses, and the solutions it will test. Teresa Torres designed the tree as a team activity for a product trio, and one of its main benefits is helping the team "externalize and visualize" its thinking so it can align on what to do next ([Torres, Opportunity Solution Trees](https://www.producttalk.org/opportunity-solution-trees/)).

Torres's advice on workshops starts with a warning: "please start with the prerequisites." Before a first tree, the team needs a theory of its target customer and value proposition, a clearly defined outcome, and three to four story-based customer interviews ([Torres, Opportunity Solution Trees](https://www.producttalk.org/opportunity-solution-trees/)). A workshop run without interview evidence ends up with invented opportunities, and Torres says making up opportunities from scratch misses the point of the tree. Product Talk offers workshop templates for Miro, Mural, and FigJam, and has published stories of teams running their own OST workshops.

This skill covers two formats of collaborative product discovery. The first is a working session where the trio, sometimes with a few close collaborators, builds or rebuilds the tree. The second is a stakeholder session where the team walks stakeholders through the tree, invites their knowledge, and builds buy-in. Torres describes the second as showing your work: bringing stakeholders along the path from outcome to evidence instead of presenting conclusions ([Torres, Stakeholder Management](https://www.producttalk.org/stakeholder-management/)).

Both formats share a facilitation discipline. Individual thinking comes before group discussion, the outcome frames every conversation, and opinions are redirected to evidence. The detailed techniques for each layer of the tree live in the sibling skills; this page is about running the room.

## How It Works

Stories from teams who have run these workshops show the shape that works. Tali Melchior, profiled on Product Talk as a director of product management at Texthelp, first ran a series of workshops with her development team at a previous company, explaining the basic concepts and then filling out the tree together. She later facilitated a three-hour OST workshop at the UXDX conference using a fictional case study with interview snapshots and analytics dashboards she had prepared ([Product Talk, Introducing Opportunity Solution Trees at Texthelp](https://www.producttalk.org/introducing-opportunity-solution-trees/)). At BBC Maestro, Lily Smith ran practice sessions where small groups interviewed, then mapped opportunities, solutions, and experiments from a single interview; she found her first session's agenda ambitious for the time available, and a couple of participants said they needed some training at the start ([Product Talk, Deliberate Practice at BBC Maestro](https://www.producttalk.org/deliberate-practice-at-bbc-maestro/)).

Three lessons follow from those stories. Real or realistic interview material has to be ready before the session. A short introduction to the concepts pays off, especially with people new to the tree. And the agenda should cover less than seems possible, because mapping takes longer than expected.

Inside the session, the facilitator separates individual work from group work. Nielsen Norman Group's guidance on affinity diagramming in workshops recommends that each participant first write ideas independently "to avoid being biased by other team members," and warns about dominating personalities skewing the group ([NN/g, Affinity Diagramming](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/affinity-diagram/)). The same applies to placing opportunities and generating solutions on the tree.

Stakeholder sessions follow the tree from top to bottom. Torres's sequence is to start with the outcome, share the opportunity space and ask what the team missed, walk through each prioritization decision, show the target opportunity in depth with interview snapshots, share solutions and invite stakeholders' ideas, and share assumption tests and results ([Torres, Stakeholder Management](https://www.producttalk.org/stakeholder-management/)). She argues that presenting conclusions invites an opinion battle, and "You can't win opinion battles." Bringing new information, from interviews and tests, is what changes minds.

Agreeing decision criteria in advance helps both formats. Hope Gurion recommends working with stakeholders to define lines in the sand for when an opportunity or solution counts as promising, so later decisions do not become arguments about favorites ([Torres and Gurion, Engaging Stakeholders](https://www.producttalk.org/engaging-stakeholders/)).

## Step-by-Step Guide

### Step 1: Confirm the prerequisites before booking the workshop

Check that the outcome is agreed and that the team has three or four story-based interviews with snapshots. If not, postpone the tree workshop and run interviews first, or run a practice session on a fictional case as Tali Melchior did. List the materials you have: snapshots, an experience map draft, any relevant data.

### Step 2: Choose the format and the invite list

Decide whether this is a trio working session or a stakeholder session. For a working session, invite the trio and at most a few people with direct customer knowledge. For a stakeholder session, invite the people who influence or are affected by the team's decisions, and plan the level of detail each needs. Write one sentence stating what the session should produce.

### Step 3: Prepare the board and the pre-read

Set up the tree on a shared whiteboard using a template, with the outcome already in the root node. Add the interview snapshots and the experience map where everyone can see them. Send a short pre-read that explains outcome, opportunity, solution, and assumption test in a few lines each, since first-time participants often want that grounding.

### Step 4: Open the workshop with the outcome

Start by restating the outcome and why it matters to the business. Ask whether anything has changed since it was agreed. This sets the scope of the session: any idea or complaint that does not relate to the outcome gets parked. Keep this opening short.

### Step 5: Map the opportunity space together

Have participants read the snapshots, then silently write opportunities in the customer's voice and place them under the moments of the experience map. Discuss as a group, merge duplicates, reframe solutions in disguise, and structure one branch at a time. Aim for a rough first draft that later sessions will refine.

### Step 6: Choose the target opportunity and agree criteria

Compare the top-level opportunities, choose one, and descend its branch to a small target opportunity, discussing sizing and customer, market, and company factors. Agree in the room what evidence would make the team change its mind. Record the reasoning on the board.

### Step 7: Generate solutions and pick three

Give everyone quiet time to write solution ideas for the target opportunity only. Share, build on each other's ideas, cluster, and dot-vote. Choose three distinct solutions and place them under the target. Invite stakeholders' ideas into this step rather than debating them later.

### Step 8: Close the workshop with next steps

Identify the riskiest assumption for each of the three solutions and assign an owner to design each test. Set the date of the next tree update. Summarize what was decided and what the team will learn next, and share the board with everyone who attended.

## Best Practices

- Start with the prerequisites. [Torres](https://www.producttalk.org/opportunity-solution-trees/) repeats this advice for workshops because a tree built without interviews fills up with invented opportunities.
- Plan less than you think. Lily Smith found the agenda for her first BBC Maestro session ambitious, so leave slack for the mapping discussion.
- Teach the concepts briefly. A couple of participants in the BBC Maestro sessions said they needed training at the start; a short explanation of each layer avoids confusion later.
- Work silently, then together. Individual writing before discussion keeps dominant voices from shaping the whole tree, as [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/affinity-diagram/) advises for workshop clustering.
- Show your work to stakeholders. Walk from outcome to evidence, and ask stakeholders what you missed, instead of presenting a finished roadmap.
- Welcome stakeholder ideas at the solution step. Torres notes that "Solution ideas are cheap. The opportunity is what matters," so an idea that adds diversity can join the set.

## Common Mistakes

- **Running the workshop before any interviews**: The group fills the tree with assumptions presented as opportunities. Do the interviews first or use a clearly fictional practice case.
- **Inviting too many people to a working session**: Large groups slow decisions and let the loudest voices dominate. Keep building sessions small and hold a separate stakeholder walkthrough.
- **Letting the session become a solution debate**: When someone pitches a feature, ask which opportunity it addresses and park it until the solution step.
- **Presenting conclusions to stakeholders**: A finished answer invites stakeholders to compare their preferences with yours. Walk them through the tree and invite contributions at each level.
- **Ending without owners**: A tree with no next steps rarely gets updated. Close with assumption tests, owners, and a date for the next update.
