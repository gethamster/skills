# Examples: Evaluating Market Penetration Strategies

## A subscription software company picks retention over discounts

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a project-tracking tool sells monthly and annual plans to small agencies in one country. The sales team proposes a discount to win agencies from a larger competitor.

**Walkthrough:**

The baseline shows the company already serves a large share of the agencies it can reach, and that a meaningful number of customers cancel in their first few months. The headroom analysis points to retention and usage as the largest source of growth, with switching from the competitor second.

The team lists tactics across all levers and scores them. The discount scores high on speed but low on reversibility and competitor response, since the rival can match it within a week. An onboarding rework and an annual-plan offer to active monthly customers score better overall. The team pilots the onboarding rework with half of new signups for one quarter, sets early retention as the measure, and parks the discount unless the pilot fails.

## A regional food producer tests distribution before promotion

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a producer of packaged sauces sells through supermarkets in one region. Marketing wants a bigger advertising budget to grow share.

**Walkthrough:**

Store data shows the sauces sell well where stocked, but many stores in the region carry only one or two varieties. The team tags tactics by source of growth: advertising targets non-buyers, while wider shelf presence reaches buyers who already look for the brand and cannot find it.

The distribution tactic scores higher on expected impact and lower on cost. The team runs a pilot with a group of stores that agree to carry the full range and compares their sales with similar stores that do not. The advertising increase is deferred to the next planning cycle, when the pilot result is known.

## A price cut rejected on Dean's conditions

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a maker of professional cleaning equipment considers cutting prices on its main product line to take share from two established rivals.

**Walkthrough:**

The team checks the move against the conditions Joel Dean described for penetration pricing. Buyers are facility managers with fixed suppliers and multi-year service contracts, so short-run price sensitivity is low. Production costs would barely fall with more volume, and both rivals are large enough to match any cut.

The price cut fails the check. The team instead evaluates a bundled service plan for existing customers and a trade-in program for competitors' machines, scores both, and pilots the trade-in program with the sales team in one territory.
