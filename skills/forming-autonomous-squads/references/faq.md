# FAQ: Forming Autonomous Cross-Functional Squads

## How many people should be in a squad?

There is no single correct number. Published guidance ranges from fewer than eight people to 6-12, and practitioner sources caution against reading any figure as a universal rule. Size from the competencies your scope requires, then check whether the team can still coordinate in one short daily conversation. If it cannot, split the scope.

## Which roles does a squad need?

A squad needs whatever skills its scope requires to design, build, test, release and operate without routine handoffs. Commonly that includes developers, a designer, testing skills and a product owner who represents priorities. Many organizations add technical guidance, often from a chapter lead, and an agile coach as collaborating support roles rather than a management layer.

## Does every squad have to use Scrum?

No. The model lets each squad choose its own delivery process, such as Scrum, Kanban or a hybrid like Scrumban. The organization should set shared constraints and expected outcomes, then let the squad decide how to work within them. Mandating one process for reporting convenience tends to fit some squads poorly.

## How is a squad different from a project team?

A squad is a long-lived team that keeps owning a product area, while a project team is assembled for a specific delivery and then disbanded. That difference changes staffing, because a squad must cover ongoing operation and support, not just the first release. It also lets the squad accumulate the domain knowledge that makes its autonomy safe.

## What if we cannot staff every skill in every squad?

Scarce specialists are a real constraint, and some dependencies will remain. Decide deliberately which gaps to close by hiring, training or moving system ownership, and which to accept as known dependencies. Track the accepted ones through a dependency management practice so they stay visible rather than turning into hidden queues.
