# Examples: Conducting Discovery Research in the Double Diamond

## Specialist appointment booking at a regional health service

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a regional health service asks a small design team to "fix the online booking form" for specialist appointments. The team has about three weeks for Discover and access to patients, clinic reception staff and the booking system's logs.

**Walkthrough:**

The team rewrites the brief so the form is one possible cause, not the assumed problem. Its open questions become: how do patients get from a referral letter to a booked slot today, who helps them, and where do they give up?

Desk research comes first. The support log shows that most booking complaints are about referral letters patients cannot interpret, and far fewer are about the form itself. That changes the interview guide before the first session.

The team interviews patients in three groups (people who book for themselves, older patients who rely on a relative, and people who live far from the clinic) and two receptionists. Two team members then sit at a clinic reception desk for a morning and watch how phone bookings are handled.

Observation shows receptionists re-keying referral details from paper into the system while patients wait on the phone. Nobody mentioned this in interviews. The team logs it as a finding with its source and moves on without deciding what it means.

At handover, the team presents raw findings and flags one gap: it did not reach any patients who never booked at all. The Define team starts with that caveat on the wall.

## Internal tooling request at a mid-sized company

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: leadership at a company of a few hundred people says teams "collaborate badly" and asks for a new collaboration tool. A design lead is given a month to explore the problem before any purchase is considered.

**Walkthrough:**

The brief asks what collaboration looks like today, where work stalls between teams, and what each role means by "good collaboration." Tool choice is explicitly out of scope.

Desk research uses what already exists: an engagement survey, a list of tools in use, and project retrospectives. The retrospectives mention delays waiting for decisions far more often than problems finding documents.

Interviews cover several roles in several departments, including two people who joined in the last few months, because newcomers notice friction that long-serving staff have stopped seeing. The team also sits in on a few cross-team planning meetings.

The pattern that emerges is unclear decision ownership. People can find information but do not know who is allowed to decide. The researchers record this as a set of observations and quotes, and hand them to Define without proposing a tool.

## Small council service with little budget

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a small local council wants to understand why few residents apply for a garden waste permit online. There is no research budget and the team is two people for two weeks.

**Walkthrough:**

The team uses what it has. It reads the call centre's notes on permit calls, walks the online journey itself, and reads guidance from neighbouring councils.

It then spends two mornings at the council's front desk and one afternoon with call centre staff, asking permission to talk briefly with residents who come in about permits. These short conversations replace formal recruited interviews.

The findings are thinner than a funded study would produce, and the team says so. The handover lists what it heard, what it saw, and which resident groups it did not reach, so Define can scope its problem statement to match the evidence.
