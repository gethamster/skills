# 7 Ps Marketing Mix Product Strategy: Defining the Offer

> Build a 7 Ps marketing mix product strategy: define the core service, the elements around it and the differentiation the other six Ps must support.

## Overview

Product is the first P in the [7 P's Marketing Mix](../../methods/7-p-s-marketing-mix/METHOD.md) and the one the others depend on. Price is set against what the product delivers, Place and Promotion carry it to customers, and People, Process and Physical Evidence deliver and prove it. A 7 Ps marketing mix product strategy decides what exactly is being offered, to whom, and why they should choose it over the alternatives.

The product element covers more than features. Wikipedia's summary of product decisions in the mix lists "quality, features, benefits, style, design, branding, packaging, services, warranties, guarantees, life cycles, investments and returns" ([Wikipedia: Marketing mix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marketing_mix)). Borden's original list put "Product Planning" first, covering the product lines to offer, the markets to sell to and new product policy ([Borden, The Concept of the Marketing Mix](https://www.guillaumenicaise.com/wp-content/uploads/2013/10/Borden-1984_The-concept-of-marketing-mix.pdf)).

For a service, the product is harder to pin down because the customer cannot hold it. The services marketing literature describes a service product as a bundle: a core service that solves the customer's problem, plus supplementary, facilitating and supporting services around it ([Wikipedia: Services marketing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Services_marketing)). Much of the differentiation in a service lives in those surrounding elements, such as advice, onboarding, billing and follow-up.

Use this skill when launching a new offer, when a product has started to look like its competitors, or when a 7 Ps audit shows that the other Ps are pulling in different directions because nobody agreed what the product is.

## How It Works

The skill works in four layers. It starts with the customer's job, moves to the structure of the offer, then to differentiation, and ends with the product's lifecycle stage.

The customer's job comes first because it anchors every later choice. Christensen and his coauthors argue in [Know Your Customers' "Jobs to Be Done"](https://hbr.org/2016/09/know-your-customers-jobs-to-be-done) that product developers focus too much on building customer profiles and looking for correlations in data, and that firms should instead home in on the job the customer is trying to get done. Their condo example is a service story: sales rose when developers added help with the move itself instead of more features. Writing the job in the customer's words keeps the product strategy from turning into a feature list.

The structure of the offer follows the core and supplementary split. The core service is the basic reason the customer buys. Facilitating services are needed to deliver it, such as order taking, information and billing. Supporting services add value and could be removed without destabilizing the core ([Wikipedia: Services marketing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Services_marketing)). The same element can play different roles in different businesses, so the classification is a judgement the team makes and writes down.

Differentiation is where the product decisions meet the competition. Theodore Levitt opened [Marketing Success Through Differentiation of Anything](https://hbr.org/1980/01/marketing-success-through-differentiation-of-anything) with the claim that there is no such thing as a commodity and that all goods and services are differentiable. In a service, the core is often similar across providers, so the difference usually comes from the supplementary elements and from the three service Ps. The strategy names the one or two dimensions where the offer will be clearly better and accepts parity elsewhere.

Lifecycle stage sets the kind of investment the product needs. Levitt's [Exploit the Product Life Cycle](https://hbr.org/1965/11/exploit-the-product-life-cycle) complained that senior executives knew the concept but that none he surveyed used it in any strategic way. Placing the product in introduction, growth, maturity or decline turns the concept into choices: build awareness, scale, defend and extend, or harvest.

The output is one page. It should let anyone working on price, channels, promotion or delivery check their decisions against what the product is meant to be.

## Step-by-Step Guide

### Step 1: Write the customer's job

Interview recent customers and read support and sales notes to learn what they were trying to get done when they chose you. Write the job in one sentence, in their words. Note what they used before and why it fell short. Every later decision should trace back to this sentence.

### Step 2: Map the core and supplementary elements

List everything the customer receives. Mark the core service, then sort the rest into facilitating and supporting elements using the split from the [services marketing literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Services_marketing). For each element, write the benefit the customer gets from it. Elements with no clear benefit are candidates for removal.

### Step 3: Compare the offer with the alternatives

Pick the few alternatives customers actually consider, including doing nothing or doing it in-house. Compare them element by element. Mark where you are better, equal and worse. Look for gaps no alternative fills well.

### Step 4: Choose the differentiation

Choose one or two dimensions where the offer will be clearly better, following Levitt's view that any offer can be differentiated ([Levitt](https://hbr.org/1980/01/marketing-success-through-differentiation-of-anything)). Write a short positioning statement: for this customer, this offer is the one that does this, because of this reason to believe. Check that the reason to believe is something the People, Process and Physical Evidence can show.

### Step 5: Place the offer in its lifecycle

Decide whether the product is in introduction, growth, maturity or decline, using sales trends, competitor entry and how customers talk about the category. Different parts of a portfolio can sit in different stages, so assess each offer on its own. Write the implication for investment: build, scale, defend and extend, or harvest.

### Step 6: Decide the line and extensions

Based on the stage, decide what to add, improve or remove. For each proposed variant or tier, ask whether it serves a real job, whether it strengthens or blurs the positioning, and what it adds to delivery complexity. Removing a weak variant is a valid product decision.

### Step 7: Check the product against the other six Ps

Share the one-page strategy with whoever owns price, channels, promotion, people, process and physical evidence. Ask each whether their current decisions support the positioning. Record any contradictions and fix them, or change the product decision if the rest of the mix cannot support it.

## Best Practices

- Start from the customer's job. A product defined by its features tends to compete on features; one defined by the job can compete on the whole experience.
- Put effort into the supplementary elements. In many services the core is similar across providers, and the [services marketing literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Services_marketing) places much of the added value in the elements around it.
- Pick one or two differentiators and accept parity elsewhere. Trying to win on everything spreads investment thin and blurs the message.
- Keep the strategy to one page. If it needs a long deck to explain, the rest of the team will not use it to make decisions.
- Revisit the lifecycle stage when evidence changes. Levitt's point was that the concept is only useful when it drives decisions ([Levitt](https://hbr.org/1965/11/exploit-the-product-life-cycle)).

## Common Mistakes

- **Defining the product as a feature list**: Features without a job behind them give the other Ps nothing to aim at. Write the job first, then show how each element serves it.
- **Ignoring the service around the product**: Teams often plan the core and leave onboarding, billing and support to chance. Map every element the customer receives, because the customer judges all of them.
- **Differentiating on something the delivery cannot show**: A claim of expertise with no visible evidence or trained people behind it will not be believed. Check the reason to believe against the service Ps.
- **Assuming growth when the market is maturing**: Optimism about the lifecycle stage leads to the wrong investments. Use evidence such as competitor entry and slowing category growth.
- **Adding variants without removing any**: Each new tier or variant adds delivery and support complexity. Review the line as a whole and prune when a variant no longer serves a clear job.
