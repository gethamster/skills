# FAQ: Writing Desired Outcome Statements

## How many desired outcome statements should I write per job step?

Aim for 5-15 outcomes per step. Fewer than 5 usually means you are writing at too high a level of abstraction, and your downstream opportunity analysis will lack the resolution needed to make specific product decisions. More than 15 per step is unusual and may indicate you are splitting hairs or including sub-step-level detail that could be consolidated. The total across all steps typically lands between 50 and 150 for a well-scoped functional job.

## Should I write desired outcome statements before or after creating the job map?

Always after. The job map gives you the step structure that organizes your outcomes. Without it, you will write outcomes in a free-form list with no organizing principle, and you will almost certainly miss entire areas of the job. Start with [creating job maps](https://tryhamster.com/skills/creating-job-maps), validate the map, and then write outcomes step by step.

If you discover outcomes that do not fit any step during writing, that is a signal your map may be missing a step.

## Can I use outcome statements written by one customer segment for a different segment?

The statements themselves, the descriptions of what success looks like, are typically universal across segments that share the same core functional job. A financial auditor at a Big Four firm and an auditor at a regional firm both want to 'minimize the likelihood that outdated documentation is used as supporting evidence.' What differs across segments is the importance and satisfaction scores. So yes, use the same outcome statements in surveys across segments, and let the scoring data reveal where segments diverge. That divergence is what feeds [segmenting customers by unmet needs](https://tryhamster.com/skills/segmenting-customers-by-unmet-needs).

## How do I handle outcome statements that feel redundant across steps?

If two statements share the same metric and object, they must differ in context to earn separate spots on your list. 'Minimize the time it takes to locate relevant information when defining project scope' and 'Minimize the time it takes to locate relevant information when troubleshooting mid-project issues' are both valid because the context changes the scenario meaningfully. If you cannot differentiate by context, merge the statements and assign the merged version to the step where it is most relevant. During the cross-step review, flag any statement pair with more than 80% word overlap for a merge-or-differentiate decision.

## How long should writing desired outcome statements take for a typical project?

For a first attempt with 8-12 job steps and 10-15 interviews as source material, plan for 2-4 hours of writing time spread across 2-3 sessions. The first session is the slowest because you are learning the formula. By the third or fourth step, you will develop pattern recognition and move faster. Add another 1-2 hours for the cross-step review and deduplication pass, and 1-2 hours for validation with customers or SMEs.

Total elapsed time is typically one week if you are fitting this around other work.

## Why does my outcome list keep growing every time I review it?

This is normal during the first two iterations and is actually a sign of increasing rigor, not scope creep. Each review pass reveals implicit outcomes you missed and exposes bundled statements that should be split. The growth typically plateaus after 2-3 review cycles. If your list keeps growing beyond that, check whether you are writing at too fine a grain.

A useful threshold: if two outcomes would receive nearly identical importance and satisfaction scores from most respondents, they should be merged. Also ensure you are not accidentally introducing solution-specific outcomes during revision, which inflates the list without adding analytical value.

## What is the difference between a desired outcome statement and a user story?

A desired outcome statement describes what the customer wants to achieve, measured along a specific performance dimension, independent of any solution. ' Outcome statements are solution-free and persistent across product generations. User stories are solution-specific and change with every redesign. Outcome statements feed into opportunity analysis to determine which needs are underserved.

User stories translate a chosen solution into buildable work. In a JTBD workflow, outcome statements come first and inform which user stories to write.
