> Website copywriting by page type: write homepage, landing page, pricing page and feature page copy that fits each page's visitor and single job.

## Overview

Page-specific copy means writing each page of a website for the visitor who lands on it and the one job it has to do. A homepage, a landing page, a pricing page and a feature page meet different readers at different points in a decision, so the same copy cannot serve all of them. This skill is the page-by-page layer of the [copywriting framework](../../methods/copywriting-framework/METHOD.md) and covers the website copy structure for each common page type.

Corey Haines's [copywriting skill](https://github.com/coreyhaines31/marketingskills/blob/main/skills/copywriting/SKILL.md) gives each page type its own brief. The homepage should serve multiple audiences without being generic, lead with the broadest value proposition and provide clear paths for different visitor intents. The landing page gets a single message and a single call to action, matched to its traffic source. The pricing page should help visitors choose the right plan and address the question of which one is right for them. The feature page should connect feature to benefit to outcome and show use cases.

Usability research backs several of those briefs. For homepage copywriting, NN/g's [homepage guidelines](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/top-ten-guidelines-for-homepage-usability/) recommend starting the page with a one-sentence tagline that summarizes what the company does, and warn that a bland marketing slogan fails to tell users what they will gain. For pricing page copy, NN/g's research on [showing prices](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/show-price/) found that prospective customers want to know the price as their top information need, including on B2B sites, and that study participants went to competitors' sites when prices were hidden.

The homepage also carries a specific risk. NN/g's study of [Get Started buttons](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/get-started/) found that visitors looking for basic information about a company were pushed into sign-up and onboarding flows instead, and lost trust. A homepage that assumes every visitor is ready to sign up fails the visitors who came to find out what the company does.

The output of this skill is a short brief for each page, stating visitor, job and primary action, plus copy written to that brief, so the site works as a set of pages that each do their part.

## How It Works

Start with a page audit. For each page, write who arrives, from where, what they already know, and the one thing the page must get them to do. That audit usually shows pages doing the wrong job, such as a homepage written as a landing page or a pricing page with no help choosing.

The homepage orients and routes. Its first screen should say what the company does and for whom, in a tagline a stranger could repeat, following NN/g's [homepage guidelines](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/top-ten-guidelines-for-homepage-usability/). Below that, give each major visitor type a clear path: by role, by use case or by stage. Calls to action should be specific enough that visitors know where each one leads. Proof on the homepage should be broad, such as recognizable customers or overall usage, because the audience is broad.

The landing page persuades one audience to take one action. Its copy continues the promise of the ad, email or search result that brought the reader, and it follows a section order built for that single argument. The companion skill on [structuring landing page copy](../structuring-landing-page-copy-for-conversion/SKILL.md) covers that order in detail.

The pricing page helps a reader who is already interested make a choice. Show prices where you can. When exact prices are not possible, NN/g suggests showing sample prices, prices for typical scenarios or a price range ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/show-price/)). Name each plan by who it is for, list differences in terms of what the customer can do, mark the plan most customers should start with, and answer billing and cancellation questions right on the page.

The feature page explains one capability to a reader evaluating it. Lead with what the feature lets the customer do, show it working through a use case, then give the specifics an evaluator needs. Haines's brief for feature pages, connecting feature to benefit to outcome, keeps the benefit and its mechanism together. Feature pages may be read by more technical evaluators, so keep precise terms and detail.

Across all page types, align the calls to action with each page's job, and check that the pages link to each other in the order a buyer would move through them.

## Step-by-Step Guide

### Step 1: Audit each page's visitor and job

List every important page with its main traffic sources, what arriving visitors already know, and the single action it should lead to. Flag pages with no clear job and pages trying to do several. Decide the primary action for each page. This audit becomes the brief for everything that follows.

### Step 2: Write homepage copy that orients and routes

Open with a one-sentence tagline that says what the company does and for whom, as NN/g's [homepage guidelines](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/top-ten-guidelines-for-homepage-usability/) recommend. Add a subhead with the broadest benefit. Below the hero, give each main visitor type a clearly labeled path. Use calls to action that say where they lead, and include a path for visitors who want to learn more before signing up.

### Step 3: Write landing page copy for one argument

For each landing page, confirm one audience, one traffic source and one action. Write the headline to continue the promise that brought the reader. Follow a section order built for a single argument, from hero to proof to objections to the final call to action. Remove navigation and options that pull the reader away from the one action.

### Step 4: Write pricing page copy that helps visitors choose

Show prices, or sample prices and ranges where exact prices are not possible, following NN/g's research on [pricing information](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/show-price/). Name each plan by who it is for and describe differences as things the customer can do. Mark a recommended plan. Answer billing, upgrade and cancellation questions next to the plans, and label each plan's button with what happens next.

### Step 5: Write feature page copy that explains and differentiates

Lead with what the feature lets the customer do, in their words. Show it through one concrete use case. Then give the specifics an evaluator needs, such as limits, integrations and requirements. Pair each benefit with the capability behind it, following Haines's feature-to-benefit-to-outcome brief in his [copywriting skill](https://github.com/coreyhaines31/marketingskills/blob/main/skills/copywriting/SKILL.md).

### Step 6: Align calls to action across pages

Check that each page's primary action matches its job: routing on the homepage, one action on landing pages, a plan choice on the pricing page, a trial or demo on feature pages. Make sure labels describe what happens next. Link pages in the order a buyer would move through them, such as from feature pages to pricing.

### Step 7: Review each page against its brief

Read each page as its intended visitor and check that it does its one job. Run a quick comprehension check on the homepage and pricing page with people who have not seen them. Compare each page's metric with its baseline after launch, and fix the page furthest from its job first.

## Best Practices

- Write a brief for every page before writing copy: visitor, source, prior knowledge, job and primary action.
- Open the homepage with a plain tagline. NN/g's [homepage guidelines](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/top-ten-guidelines-for-homepage-usability/) call for one sentence that says what the company does.
- Show prices, or give ranges or sample prices. NN/g found price is the top information need, including for [B2B buyers](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/show-price/).
- Give homepage visitors a path to learn before they commit. A homepage that funnels everyone into sign-up loses the visitors who came for information.
- Keep feature pages specific. Evaluators reading them want the capability and its limits as well as the benefit.
- Keep one primary action per page and make its label match its destination.

## Common Mistakes

- **Writing every page from one template**: A homepage structured like a landing page ignores most of its visitors. Write each page for its own visitor and job.
- **Hiding prices**: Sending every visitor to "contact sales" pushes early researchers to competitors who show prices. Show what you can.
- **Using a slogan as the homepage headline**: A clever slogan that does not say what the company does leaves new visitors guessing. Use a plain tagline first.
- **Burying feature specifics**: A feature page that only states benefits frustrates evaluators who need details. Pair benefits with capabilities and limits.
- **Leaving pages disconnected**: Pages that do not link in the order buyers move through them strand visitors. Connect feature pages to pricing and pricing to sign-up.
