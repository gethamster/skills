# Examples: How to Create a Customer Journey Map That Drives Action

## A trial-to-paid map for a self-serve product

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a small project-management tool sees plenty of trial signups but few conversions to paid. The growth lead wants to know what the onboarding team should change next quarter. The team has a few months of support tickets, product analytics and no recent interviews.

**Walkthrough:**

The growth lead writes the business question first: why do trial users who create a project still not upgrade? The actor is a team lead at a small agency who signs up alone and needs to bring colleagues in. Before the session, a product manager runs six short interviews with recent trial users and sorts support tickets by stage.

In the session, the group places actions stage by stage. The evidence shows that users who never invite a teammate treat the tool as a personal to-do list and see no reason to pay. The emotional low point sits in the second week, when the lead tries to invite colleagues and the invitation lands in spam. That becomes the top opportunity, owned by the onboarding engineer and tied to the trial-to-paid conversion metric. Two cells about pricing perception stay flagged as assumptions until the next round of interviews.

## A future-state map for a new service line

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: an accounting firm is launching a monthly bookkeeping subscription for existing tax clients. Nobody has bought it yet, so there is no current-state journey to observe.

**Walkthrough:**

The team maps the current tax-client journey first, because that is where the new service will be sold. It shows that clients hear from the firm only at filing time, which leaves retention and advocacy almost empty. The future-state map then designs the subscription journey against that baseline: a mention during the tax meeting for awareness, a short comparison of doing books in-house versus the subscription for consideration, and a monthly summary email as the main retention touchpoint.

Each future-state cell is marked as a design choice rather than an observation. The team agrees to replace those cells with evidence after the first cohort of clients has been through onboarding.

## Rescuing a map nobody used

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a retail brand built a large journey map in a workshop last year. It covered every segment at once, hung on a wall and was never referenced in planning.

**Walkthrough:**

The new customer experience manager starts over with one segment, repeat online buyers, because that is where the planning question sits: why repeat purchase rates have flattened. She keeps the old map's touchpoint list as input but rebuilds the emotions and pain points from recent reviews and support chats. The new map fits on one screen and has four opportunities, each with an owner.

The change that sticks is procedural. The map is reviewed at the start of each quarterly planning meeting, and each owner reports on their opportunity. The old wall map is taken down so there is only one version.
