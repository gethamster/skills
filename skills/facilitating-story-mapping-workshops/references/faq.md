# FAQ: Story Mapping Facilitation: How to Run a Workshop

## How many people should attend a story mapping workshop?

Jeff Patton's original article on the method says four to eight people is a good number. The group should include people who know the users, people who understand the business, and a developer or two. Larger groups tend to split into spectators and participants, so if more people need to be involved, walk them through the map afterward.

## What should the workshop agenda look like?

Patton's quick reference gives a sequence you can use as an agenda: frame the problem, map the big picture, explore the details, slice out viable releases, and slice out a development strategy. Add an opening to agree the user and goal and a close to capture questions and owners. How much of the sequence fits in one session depends on the scope.

## Should the product manager build a draft map before the workshop?

Bring a brief and a user sketch, but avoid a pre-built map. A finished map turns the session into a review and reduces the discussion that makes mapping useful. Patton notes that the model could be built by one person but recommends against it, because building it together spreads understanding across the team.

## How do I keep the workshop from turning into a specification session?

Keep the first pass wide and shallow until the story reaches the user's goal. When the group starts writing acceptance criteria or debating edge cases in one column, write those points on a card, park them lower in the column, and move on. Detailed work belongs to the stories closest to being built.

## Can a story mapping workshop run fully remote?

Yes. The Nielsen Norman Group notes that remote teams can use video conferencing along with collaborative spreadsheets, slides or web-based whiteboards. Prepare the board in advance, give everyone edit access, and assign columns so people are not all editing the same spot. Walk the map aloud more often than you would in a room.
