# FAQ: Identifying Your Product's North Star Metric

## How is a North Star Metric different from a KPI or OKR?

KPIs are any key performance indicators—you might have dozens across the company. OKRs are time-bound objectives with measurable key results, typically set quarterly. Your North Star Metric is a single, enduring metric that represents the core value your product delivers. It sits above KPIs and OKRs in the hierarchy: your OKRs might define quarterly targets for improving the NSM, and your KPIs are the input metrics that drive it. Think of it this way: OKRs change every quarter, KPIs vary by team, but your North Star Metric stays constant for 6-12+ months and applies to the entire product organization.

## Can a company have more than one North Star Metric?

The short answer is no—the entire value of the framework comes from forcing alignment around a single metric. If you have two, you effectively have zero because teams will optimize for whichever is more convenient. The exception is companies with genuinely distinct product lines serving different customers (e.g., a company running both a consumer app and a B2B API). Each product can have its own NSM, but each product team should have exactly one. If you're struggling to choose between two candidates, it usually means one is actually an input metric that drives the other—use the [mapping input metrics](https://tryhamster.com/skills/mapping-input-metrics-to-north-star) skill to determine the hierarchy.

## What is a product manager's role in identifying the North Star Metric versus leadership's role?

The product manager typically drives the identification process—conducting customer research, analyzing data, generating candidates, and making a recommendation. Leadership's role is to provide strategic context (where is the company headed in 2-3 years?), validate that the proposed NSM aligns with the business model, and commit to the metric organizationally. Think of it as: the PM does the analytical work and proposes, leadership challenges and ratifies. The worst pattern is when a CEO announces the NSM top-down without the analytical process—it rarely reflects genuine customer value and teams won't feel ownership.

## How do I identify a North Star Metric when my product is pre-product-market fit?

Before product-market fit, your understanding of customer value is still forming, so your NSM will be more of a hypothesis than a validated metric. That's okay—pick the best candidate you can based on early customer interviews and usage data, and expect to revise it. Focus on the value moment: what action indicates a user got real value? Even with 50 users, you can often identify the behavioral difference between users who come back and those who don't. Use that as your starting NSM, commit for 3-4 months instead of the usual 6-12, and iterate. Having an imperfect NSM is still better than having no alignment metric at all.

## What if our North Star Metric conflicts with short-term revenue goals?

This tension is common and actually healthy—it surfaces strategic misalignment that was always there but hidden. If your NSM is 'weekly active teams completing projects' but leadership wants to maximize this quarter's upsell revenue, the conflict isn't between metrics, it's between short-term extraction and long-term value creation. Present the correlation data showing your NSM predicts future revenue. Then negotiate: perhaps the team allocates 80% of effort to NSM-driving work and 20% to immediate revenue needs. If leadership consistently overrides the NSM for short-term revenue, the framework won't work—and that's valuable information about whether the organization is truly ready for it.

## How do I know if I've chosen the wrong North Star Metric?

Three warning signs: (1) The metric is going up but customer satisfaction or retention is flat or declining—this means your metric doesn't actually capture value. (2) Teams are gaming it in ways that don't help customers—for example, inflating 'active users' through aggressive notification spam. (3) After 3-6 months, product teams can't articulate how their current work connects to the NSM—this means it's too abstract or disconnected from daily product decisions. If you see any of these, don't panic-switch. Instead, revisit the process described here and use the [iterating and evolving your North Star Metric](https://tryhamster.com/skills/iterating-and-evolving-north-star-metric) skill to systematically reassess.
