# FAQ: Identifying Your North Star Metric

## Why write a statement before choosing a metric?

Because a metric chosen first tends to reflect whatever data is easy to get or whatever another company uses. The statement forces the team to agree on how the product creates value and grows, in plain words. Once that sentence holds up, the right measure is much easier to recognize and defend.

## What if our beliefs about customers conflict?

Write them all down and treat each as an assumption to test. Conflicting beliefs usually point to the research the team most needs, such as interviews with customers who stayed and customers who left. The metric can wait until the most important conflict is settled or explicitly accepted as a risk.

## Can our North Star be something we cannot measure yet?

It can start that way. The playbook warns against dropping promising ideas just because nobody yet knows how to measure them, and notes that light instrumentation often solves the problem. Plan the tracking work early so the metric does not stay on a slide.

## How precise does the first definition need to be?

Precise enough that two analysts would calculate the same number, including the unit, the threshold and the time window. The thresholds themselves can be provisional. Say openly which parts you are unsure about and when you will revisit them.

## How is this different from choosing a metric from a list of examples?

Example lists show what other products measure, and each reflects that company's game and beliefs. Starting from your own beliefs, vision and value exchanges produces a metric that expresses your strategy, which is what lets the team use it to make decisions.
