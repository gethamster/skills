# Examples: Identifying Your North Star Metric

## A design tool that stops arguing about daily users

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a browser-based design tool has two camps. Growth wants daily active users as the North Star, and the collaboration team wants comments per file. Neither side will move.

**Walkthrough:**

The product lead asks everyone to fill in the belief templates alone. The answers show the real split: growth believes people choose the tool for speed, and the collaboration team believes they choose it because reviews happen in one place. Customer interviews from the last quarter support the second belief for paying teams.

The team writes the statement: growth depends on its ability to help small teams get a design from draft to approved without leaving the tool. Candidate measures follow from the sentence, and the group picks "designs approved with at least one reviewer" as its hypothesis. Daily active users stays on the dashboard as a health metric rather than the North Star.

## A marketplace that finds its value exchange outside the product

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a marketplace for local home repairs tracks bookings made in the app. Repeat use is low even though bookings grow each month.

**Walkthrough:**

Mapping the key value exchanges takes the team outside the app. The moment customers care about is the repair being finished properly on the first visit, which happens in the customer's home. Bookings capture intent before that moment happens.

The statement becomes: growth depends on the team's ability to get jobs done right the first time. The team has no direct measure of that yet, so it keeps the concept and adds a short follow-up question after each job. Its candidate North Star is "jobs completed with no follow-up visit," defined using the new question plus rebooking data.

## A learning app with a threshold debate

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a language-learning app agrees on a statement about helping learners build a daily practice habit. The meeting then stalls on whether a habit means practice on five days a week or six.

**Walkthrough:**

The facilitator points out that the words already agree and the argument is about a number. The team picks five days as an aspirational but achievable threshold, writes down that it is a guess, and names the metric "Weekly Habit Learners."

It defines the metric precisely, looks at how the count would have moved over the past year, and sets a date to revisit the threshold once input work begins. The disagreement becomes a question for data instead of a blocker.
