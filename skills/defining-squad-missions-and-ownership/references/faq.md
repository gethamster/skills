# FAQ: Defining Squad Missions and Product Ownership Areas

## How specific should a squad mission be?

Specific enough to rule work out, broad enough to leave the solution open. A useful check is whether squad members can name work the mission excludes. A mission such as "improve the search experience" names a clear outcome without prescribing features. If the mission could describe every team in the company, it is too broad.

## Who writes the squad mission?

The product manager or product owner usually drafts it, because they hold the product strategy and stakeholder context. The draft should then be tested with the squad and with neighboring squads before it is final. The squad needs to believe the mission is achievable within its boundary. Neighbors need to confirm it does not overlap theirs.

## What if an area cannot be owned end to end by one squad?

First try widening or reslicing the area along a user outcome instead of a technology layer. If a shared piece remains, such as a platform service several squads use, give it a single owner and define the interfaces other squads use. Some remaining dependencies are normal and can be handled through coordination. The goal is to remove routine blocking, not all collaboration.

## How does the product owner handle requests from outside the squad's area?

The product owner weighs outside requests against the ranked priorities tied to the mission. Requests that serve the mission get ranked alongside everything else. Requests that do not serve it get redirected to the owning squad or declined with a reason. A written boundary makes these decisions quick and less personal.

## How often should ownership boundaries change?

Rarely, and only for a clear reason such as a shift in product strategy or repeated strain at a boundary. Squads are meant to be long-lived, and that value comes from deep knowledge of a stable area. A periodic review, for example quarterly, catches boundaries that need adjustment without turning ownership into a moving target.
