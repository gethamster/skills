# FAQ: Making Pivot-or-Persevere Decisions

## How many experiments should I run before making a pivot-or-persevere decision?

Two experiments is the minimum, three to four is ideal. A single experiment can fail due to execution problems rather than assumption problems, so you need multiple data points to separate signal from noise. However, do not use "more data" as an excuse to postpone the decision indefinitely. If three experiments all point in the same direction, you have enough evidence.

The exception is if each experiment tested a fundamentally different approach, in which case you need at least two experiments testing the same refined approach to see if iteration is producing improvement.

## How long should a pivot-or-persevere meeting take?

Plan for 90 to 120 minutes. The first 45 minutes should be spent reviewing the evidence package without debating conclusions. The next 15 minutes are for individual silent assessments. The remaining time is for group discussion and decision.

Teams that try to rush this into 30 minutes almost always defer the decision because they run out of time before reaching alignment. If you consistently go over 120 minutes, it usually means the evidence package was not circulated in advance and people are processing the data for the first time in the meeting.

## Should I make the pivot-or-persevere decision before or after conducting customer discovery interviews?

Customer discovery data should feed into the decision, not replace it. Ideally, you run [customer discovery interviews](https://tryhamster.com/skills/conducting-customer-discovery-interviews) as part of your experiment cycles, so the qualitative insights are already in your evidence package when the pivot-or-persevere meeting happens. If you find yourself wanting to do a fresh round of customer interviews to "get more data" right before the decision, check whether that impulse is genuine curiosity or a delay tactic. If your quantitative metrics clearly fail your thresholds, interviews will not change the math.

## What if my team cannot agree on whether to pivot or persevere?

First, check whether the disagreement is about the data or about risk tolerance. If team members interpret the same metrics differently, spend more time on the evidence review and ask each person to state what specific data point would change their mind. If the disagreement is about risk tolerance (one person wants to give it more time, another wants to pivot now), the tie-breaker is the pre-set success threshold. If the metric is below threshold after the agreed number of experiments, the framework says pivot.

Overriding the framework because it feels premature is the most common way teams persevere too long.

## How do I know which type of pivot to choose?

The pivot type should be determined by which assumption was invalidated. If the problem is validated but the solution is not, consider a zoom-in pivot (focus on the one feature that works) or a technology pivot (different technical approach to the same problem). If the solution works but the customer segment is wrong, try a customer segment pivot. If the product works for the audience but you cannot reach them affordably, try a channel pivot.

Map each invalidated assumption to the corresponding pivot type, and the answer usually becomes clear. If multiple assumptions are invalidated simultaneously, focus on the one with the most evidence against it and address the others in subsequent cycles.

## Can I do a partial pivot instead of a full pivot?

Every pivot is partial by design. The entire point of naming the pivot type is to specify which one assumption changes while the rest stay the same. There is no such thing as a "full pivot" in the Lean Startup framework. Changing everything at once is not a pivot, it is starting a new company.

If you feel the urge to change multiple assumptions simultaneously, force yourself to sequence them: change one, test it, then re-evaluate whether the other assumptions still need changing. Often, fixing one broken assumption resolves issues that appeared to affect others.

## Why does my pivot-or-persevere decision keep getting deferred?

Deferral almost always comes from one of three causes. First, the team did not set clear success thresholds before running experiments, so there is no objective basis for the decision. Third, the emotional cost of admitting failure is too high, and the team unconsciously avoids the decision by finding reasons to delay.
