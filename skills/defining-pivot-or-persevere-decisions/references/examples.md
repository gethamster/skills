# Examples: Making Pivot-or-Persevere Decisions

## Example: B2C Meal Planning App (3-Person Team)

**Scenario:**

A three-person team has spent eight weeks building and testing a meal planning app for busy parents. They ran three experiments: an onboarding flow test, a recipe recommendation A/B test, and a push notification retention experiment. Their key metric is 7-day retention (percentage of users who return after their first week). Their pre-set threshold was 20%. The team has $80K in runway remaining.

**Walkthrough:**

The team compiled their evidence package the night before the meeting. Experiment 1 showed 7-day retention at 11%. They iterated on onboarding and ran Experiment 2, which brought retention to 14%. Experiment 3 (push notifications) moved it to 15%.

The trend is upward but the trajectory, if extrapolated, would take another six to eight months to reach 20%. With $80K in runway, that timeline does not work. In the assumption review, the team rated the problem assumption (parents need help planning meals) as validated based on customer discovery data. The solution assumption (an app with AI recipes) was rated inconclusive.

The channel assumption (Instagram ads targeting parents) was rated invalidated because customer acquisition cost was $12 with a target of $3. The team decided to pivot. They named it a channel pivot: keep the product and audience but switch from paid Instagram to a partnership distribution model with pediatrician offices. The new hypothesis was: "We believe pediatrician offices will distribute our app to new parents.

We will test by approaching 10 offices and measuring signup rate. " They documented the decision and scheduled the next review in six weeks.

## Example: B2B SaaS Expense Reporting Tool (Seed Stage)

**Scenario:**

A seed-stage startup has built an expense reporting tool targeting mid-market companies (200-2000 employees). They have run four experiments over 12 weeks, targeting trial-to-paid conversion rate. Their threshold is 8%. They have $400K in runway and a team of six. Two enterprise pilot customers are using the product but have not converted to paid.

**Walkthrough:**

2%. The metric was essentially flat despite improvements to the onboarding flow, pricing page, and feature set. During the assumption review, the team discovered something surprising. Their two pilot customers loved one specific feature: automated receipt matching using phone camera photos.

Both pilots reported that their employees used the receipt matching daily even though they barely touched the rest of the product. The team rated the customer segment assumption (mid-market companies) as inconclusive and the solution assumption (comprehensive expense reporting) as partially invalidated. But the receipt matching sub-feature was strongly validated. They decided on a zoom-in pivot: make automated receipt matching the entire product, strip away the full expense reporting suite, and target small businesses and freelancers who need a simple receipt capture tool, not a full expense system.

The new hypothesis was: "Freelancers will pay $9 per month for a standalone receipt matching app. We will test with a landing page MVP targeting freelancer communities and measure signup-to-trial conversion. " They preserved their validated learning about the receipt matching technology and the insight that simplicity beat comprehensiveness.

## Example: Developer Tool Startup (Pre-Revenue, Large Team)

**Scenario:**

A 15-person startup backed by $2M in seed funding has built a code review automation tool targeting engineering teams at growth-stage startups. They have run five experiments over 16 weeks. Their north star metric is weekly active teams (teams that use the tool at least three times per week). Their threshold is 50 weekly active teams by week 16. They are at 47.

**Walkthrough:**

The team assembled a thorough evidence package. The weekly active teams metric had grown from 12 to 47 across five experiments, a clear upward trajectory. Retention among active teams was strong at 78% monthly. The team reviewed each assumption: problem validated (code review bottlenecks are painful), solution validated (automated suggestions save time), customer segment validated (engineering teams at Series A-C companies).

The channel assumption was rated as partially validated because organic growth through developer word-of-mouth was working but slowly. The revenue assumption was untested because they had not started charging. The decision was to persevere, but with an important caveat. The team acknowledged that being at 47 versus a threshold of 50 was functionally at threshold, and the next experiment needed to focus on the untested revenue assumption rather than continuing to optimize for free adoption.

The next experiment was a pricing test: introduce a paid tier for teams larger than five developers and measure willingness to pay. The threshold was 15% of existing active teams converting to paid within 30 days of the pricing introduction. They scheduled the next pivot-or-persevere meeting for eight weeks out, with the explicit note that if the revenue assumption was invalidated, a revenue model pivot would be the primary option to evaluate.

## Example: Marketplace Startup (Two-Sided, Early Stage)

**Scenario:**

A four-person team is building a marketplace connecting freelance graphic designers with small business owners who need branding work. After 10 weeks, they have 120 designers on the supply side and 45 business owners on the demand side. They have facilitated 8 completed transactions. Their key metric is transactions per week, with a threshold of 5 per week by week 10.

6 per week.

**Walkthrough:**

The evidence package told a story of a classic cold-start problem. Supply (designers) was growing steadily through outreach, but demand (business owners) was not converting to actual transactions. Three experiments had focused on demand-side acquisition: Google Ads, a content marketing launch, and a referral program. All three brought in business owners who signed up but did not post projects.

The team dug into qualitative data from customer discovery calls and found that business owners did not trust the platform enough to post a real project without seeing portfolios and reviews, but there were not enough completed transactions to generate reviews. In the assumption review, the problem was validated on both sides. The solution assumption (a self-service marketplace) was rated as invalidated for the current customer segment. Business owners with serious branding needs wanted a concierge experience, not a marketplace browse.

The team chose a customer need pivot: instead of connecting buyers and sellers on a self-service marketplace, they would offer a concierge matching service where the team personally matches each business owner with three designers based on style and budget. The hypothesis was: "Business owners will complete a branding project when matched by our team rather than browsing a marketplace. We will test by concierge-matching 20 incoming business owners and measuring project completion rate. " They preserved their designer supply base and their insight about the trust barrier.
