# Examples: Creating Job Maps to Visualize Customer Processes

## Example: B2C Financial App, Small Team

**Scenario:**

A fintech startup with a four-person product team is building a personal budgeting app. They have conducted eight JTBD interviews with target customers and defined the core functional job as 'manage personal finances over time.' They need a job map to identify where their product should focus first, given limited engineering resources and a six-month runway.

**Walkthrough:**

The team pulls interview transcripts into a shared doc and highlights every verb phrase customers used when describing how they manage their money. They cluster these into the eight universal steps. Define: customers determine their financial goals and decide what 'managing finances' means to them (saving for a house, reducing debt, building an emergency fund). Locate: customers gather financial information from bank accounts, credit cards, pay stubs, and bills.

Prepare: customers organize this information so it can be analyzed, often by categorizing transactions. Confirm: customers verify that the information is complete and accurate before making decisions. Execute: customers allocate money to categories, make payments, transfer to savings. Monitor: customers track spending against their plan throughout the month.

Modify: customers adjust allocations when unexpected expenses arise or income changes. Conclude: customers review their financial position at the end of a period and evaluate progress toward goals. The team annotates that monitor and modify are the highest-weight steps based on customer frustration data: seven of eight interviewees described difficulty tracking whether they were on plan mid-month. This insight directly shaped their product manager roadmap, prioritizing real-time spend tracking and smart alerts over the onboarding and setup features they had previously planned to build first.

## Example: B2B SaaS, Enterprise Team

**Scenario:**

A mid-size SaaS company selling project management software has a 12-person product organization. They have 30 interview transcripts from construction project managers and have defined the core job as 'ensure a construction project is completed on time and within budget.' They want to use the job map to align three product squads around customer-centric goals rather than feature areas.

**Walkthrough:**

The research team codes all 30 transcripts against the eight steps, producing a dense matrix of customer actions mapped to universal phases. Define: project managers establish the project scope, timeline, milestones, and budget parameters before any physical work begins. Locate: they identify and secure the required subcontractors, materials, permits, and equipment. Prepare: they sequence work activities, stage materials on-site, and brief subcontractor teams.

Confirm: they verify permit approvals, material delivery, and subcontractor readiness before each phase begins. Execute: they oversee the performance of each construction phase. Monitor: they track progress against the schedule and budget, flag variances, and report status. Modify: they re-sequence work, negotiate change orders, and reallocate budget when delays or cost overruns occur.

Conclude: they complete punch lists, close out permits, hand over documentation, and conduct a post-project review. The map revealed that confirm and modify were the most complex and frustrating steps: permit verification and change order management consumed disproportionate time. The product leadership used this to restructure squad assignments. One squad now owns the confirm step (permit tracking and readiness workflows), one owns monitor and modify (variance detection and change order management), and one owns define and conclude (project setup and closeout).

Each squad generates outcome statements for its steps, and the combined scores form a unified product manager roadmap.

## Example: B2C Healthcare, Solo Product Manager

**Scenario:**

A solo product manager at a digital health startup is building a medication adherence app. She has conducted five interviews with patients managing chronic conditions and defined the job as 'take the right medication at the right time consistently.' She needs a job map to guide her first product release with a two-person engineering team.

**Walkthrough:**

Working alone, the PM creates a simple spreadsheet with eight columns and starts coding interview notes. Define: patients understand their medication regimen, including which medications to take, dosages, and timing. Locate: they obtain prescriptions, refill medications, and ensure they have an adequate supply. Prepare: they organize medications by time of day, set up pill organizers, and position medications where they will see them.

Confirm: they verify they are about to take the correct medication and correct dosage (especially important when regimens change). Execute: they take the medication. Monitor: they track whether they have taken each dose and watch for side effects or interactions. Modify: they adjust timing when schedules change (travel, shift work) or when a doctor changes the regimen.

Conclude: they evaluate whether the medication is working by assessing symptoms and discussing with their doctor at follow-ups. The execute step turns out to be almost trivial, it is physically swallowing a pill, yet the PM's initial feature ideas had focused entirely on making that moment pleasant. The map shows the real complexity is in define (understanding a multi-drug regimen), confirm (avoiding errors with similar-looking pills), and modify (handling changes). She restructures her product manager roadmap to prioritize a regimen clarity feature and a change-management workflow over the reminder notification system she had originally planned.

## Example: B2B Professional Services, Large Organization

**Scenario:**

A global consulting firm's internal product team is building a knowledge management platform for 5,000 consultants. They have conducted 15 interviews across four offices and defined the core job as 'find and apply relevant expertise to solve a client problem.' They need the job map to justify a significant platform investment to the executive committee.

**Walkthrough:**

The team assembles a cross-functional mapping group: two product managers, one UX researcher, one senior consultant, and one knowledge management librarian. They code interview data collaboratively in a two-day workshop. Define: consultants frame the client problem, identify what type of expertise is needed, and determine whether they can solve it themselves or need external knowledge. Locate: they search for relevant prior work, identify colleagues with relevant experience, and find external research or data.

Prepare: they synthesize and adapt found knowledge to the specific client context, translating general frameworks into industry-specific recommendations. Confirm: they validate that their approach is sound by checking with senior colleagues, comparing to precedent engagements, and verifying data accuracy. Execute: they develop and deliver the client solution. Monitor: they track client reactions and engagement outcomes to gauge whether the expertise was correctly applied.

Modify: they refine the solution based on client feedback and evolving project requirements. Conclude: they document lessons learned and make their new expertise available for future consultants. The map reveals that locate and prepare account for an estimated 60% of consultant frustration. Consultants described spending hours searching fragmented systems and then manually adapting what they found.

The executive committee had been skeptical of the platform investment, but seeing the job map with customer quotes attached to each step, particularly the locate and prepare pain, made the case concrete. The resulting product manager roadmap prioritized intelligent search and contextual adaptation features, with a clear line of sight from each feature to the specific job step it addresses.
