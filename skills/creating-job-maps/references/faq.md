# FAQ: Creating Job Maps to Visualize Customer Processes

## How long does a job map stay valid before it needs updating?

Job maps are remarkably stable because they describe functional needs, not solutions or behaviors. A well-built map for a core functional job typically remains valid for 3-5 years or longer. What changes more frequently are the desired outcome statements and satisfaction scores that attach to each step. Plan to refresh outcome data annually and re-validate the map structure every 2-3 years or when a major technology shift fundamentally changes how the job is performed (e.g., the introduction of smartphones changed the locate and monitor steps for many jobs).

## How many customer interviews do I need before building a job map?

Five to eight well-conducted JTBD interviews typically provide enough data to draft a complete map. You will reach diminishing returns around 12-15 interviews for the map structure itself, though you may need more interviews to generate comprehensive desired outcome statements for each step. If you have fewer than five interviews, you can draft a provisional map, but flag it clearly as unvalidated and plan targeted follow-up interviews to fill gaps. The risk of building from too little data is not that the map will be wrong, but that it will be incomplete, missing steps that a broader sample would have revealed.

## Should I create a job map before or after writing desired outcome statements?

Always create the job map first. Desired outcome statements are organized by job step, so you need the map as the structural scaffold before you can generate outcomes systematically. Attempting to write outcomes without a map leads to an unstructured list that is hard to prioritize and often has gaps because you have no framework for ensuring coverage across all phases of the job. The map-first sequence also ensures that your outcomes are attached to the right step, which matters when you later score importance and satisfaction to identify underserved opportunities.

## Can one customer have multiple job maps?

Yes, but be precise about what you mean. A customer typically has one core functional job in a given context, and that job gets one map. However, a customer may have several distinct functional jobs across their role or life. A marketing manager might have 'generate qualified leads,' 'measure campaign effectiveness,' and 'manage vendor relationships' as separate jobs, each with its own map.

The key is to avoid combining multiple jobs into a single map. If you find yourself with a map that has 15 or more distinct activities, you likely have two or more jobs blended together. Split them.

## How do I use a job map to build a product manager roadmap?

The job map becomes the demand-side structure for your roadmap. First, generate desired outcome statements for each step. Then survey customers to score each outcome on importance and satisfaction. Steps with high-importance, low-satisfaction outcomes are your biggest opportunities.

Map your current and planned features against these opportunities to see where you are investing and where you have gaps. Your product manager roadmap then becomes a sequence of bets organized by which job steps you are improving, with clear evidence for why each investment matters. This approach is detailed in the sibling skill [applying JTBD insights to product strategy](https://tryhamster.com/skills/applying-jtbd-to-product-strategy).

## What if my team disagrees about which universal step an activity belongs to?

Disagreements about step assignment are common and usually productive. , an activity that is partly locate and partly prepare), or team members have different mental models of the customer's process. Resolve by returning to customer data. Read the original quote in context and ask which phase of the job the customer was in when they described this activity.

If the activity genuinely spans two steps, note it in both with an annotation. Do not spend more than five minutes debating a single assignment. If it is ambiguous, it will not materially affect your prioritization decisions.

## Why does my job map keep drifting toward our product's feature set?

This drift happens because your team's mental model of the customer's job is shaped by years of working on your product. Every day you think about features, user flows, and product metrics, so that vocabulary colonizes your descriptions. The antidote is a deliberate review pass where you read each step description and ask: would this description make sense to a customer who has never heard of our product or any competitor's product? Schedule this as a specific review step, not an afterthought.

Having a team member from outside product (a customer success manager or a researcher) do this review is especially effective because they are less anchored to the product vocabulary.
