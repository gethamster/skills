# Examples: Planning the Shape Up Cooldown Period

## A cool-down after a noisy launch

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a team ships a redesigned project dashboard at the end of a cycle. Within two days, support has a stack of messages, some asking for the old layout back and several requesting new widgets.

**Walkthrough:**

The product lead does not promise anything in the first days. Support answers with thanks and a note that the feedback is being collected. After a week, the complaints about the layout have mostly faded, and two requests keep coming up: a way to pin projects and a compact view.

Neither is bet on during this cool-down. The product lead adds both to their own list of ideas to shape. One of them, pinning, gets shaped on the shaping track during the next cycle and competes at the following betting table. Meanwhile, the builders spend the cool-down on bugs from their own lists and a cleanup of the dashboard's queries.

## A team that keeps spilling into cool-down

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: over three cycles, a big batch team has used the first few days of every cool-down to finish must-haves that did not fit.

**Walkthrough:**

The head of product treats the pattern as a signal about shaping. Reviewing the last three pitches, they find each had an unresolved rabbit hole the team hit mid-cycle: a data migration, a third-party API limit and a permissions question.

The shapers add a step to their process: before a pitch goes to the table, a senior engineer walks through it and asks whether each part is possible within the appetite. The next two projects ship inside the cycle, and cool-down goes back to being unscheduled time.

## A small team skips the formal cool-down

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a startup of three people, two builders and a founder who also handles sales, tries to run six-week cycles with a formal cool-down and betting table.

**Walkthrough:**

The structure feels heavy. The founder is shaping, selling and answering support at once, and a separate betting meeting between three people adds little. Following the book's advice for very small teams, they drop the formal cycles and cool-down.

They keep the core habits: set an appetite, shape the next piece of work, build it, then shape the next thing. Between bets, they take a few days for bugs and cleanup whenever a project ships. When the team grows and coordination starts to slip, they plan to return to fixed cycles and a proper cool-down.
