# Examples: Building a North Star Metric Dashboard

## A product team replaces a wall of charts

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a B2B software company has a product metrics dashboard with dozens of charts. Its North Star, weekly active accounts with a completed workflow, sits halfway down the page. Leadership reviews it monthly and rarely makes a decision in the meeting.

**Walkthrough:**

The analytics lead rebuilds the main view from the input map. The North Star moves to the top, its four inputs sit directly below in the order they flow, and three health metrics sit at the bottom. Every other chart moves to team drill-downs. Each chart shows the last six weeks beside the last year by month, with the definition and owner in the chart title.

The monthly review becomes a weekly half-hour leadership review. Owners send notes on unusual movements the day before. In the first meeting, one input shows a sharp drop; the owner explains it as a tracking change after a release, and the team adds a release annotation so the next reader sees it immediately.

## A consumer app learns to separate campaigns from product

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a consumer app's North Star jumps in one week and the product team celebrates a new onboarding flow. Two weeks later the number falls back.

**Walkthrough:**

The team discovers that a large email campaign went out the same week as the onboarding release. Nobody had recorded it where the product team could see it. They create a shared event log that marketing, product and engineering all write to, and they show it as markers on every chart.

They also add a cohort split to the activation input so new users from the campaign can be compared with users from other channels. The next release is judged on the cohort view, and the team can tell how much of any movement came from the product change.

## A leadership dashboard that stops at the North Star

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a marketplace shows its board and leadership a single North Star chart, completed bookings per week. When it dips, nobody in the room can say why until analysts investigate for several days.

**Walkthrough:**

The team adds the inputs beneath the North Star: new buyers, repeat buyer rate, supply in high-demand areas, and booking completion rate. Each input gets an owner who reviews it weekly and brings an explanation for anything unusual.

At the next dip, the dashboard shows that supply in high-demand areas fell while the other inputs held. The supply owner already has a note: two large providers paused their listings. The discussion moves straight to what to do, and the analysts are no longer the bottleneck for every question.
