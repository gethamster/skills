# Examples: Assessing Transformational Leadership Effectiveness

## Evaluating a leadership coaching program

**Scenario:**

An HR team has funded a coaching program for first-line managers and wants to know whether it shifted how teams experience their leaders.

**Walkthrough:**

Illustrative scenario: the team assesses 20 managers, each rated by 5 direct reports, once before coaching and again six months later. They also collect a comparison group of 20 managers who have not yet started the program. The coached group's average overall score rises from 3.0 to 3.5 on the survey's scale, while the comparison group moves from 3.0 to 3.1. Because both groups were measured twice with the same rater rules, the team can attribute more of the change to coaching than a single snapshot would allow.

Their report still notes that only 40 managers were involved and that dimension-level shifts are too small to interpret separately. The recommendation is to extend the program to a second cohort and repeat the measurement.

## Catching same-source inflation

**Scenario:**

An analyst finds an unusually strong link between leader scores and follower satisfaction in an internal survey and is asked to present it to leadership.

**Walkthrough:**

Illustrative scenario: the first analysis shows a correlation of 0.70 between transformational scores and satisfaction, both taken from the same questionnaire completed by the same 100 followers. Before presenting, the analyst reruns the analysis against performance ratings supplied separately by each leader's own manager. That correlation comes out at 0.30. The analyst presents both numbers, explains that the higher one shares a single rater source and is likely inflated by general impressions, and leads with the lower, independent figure.

Leadership gets a smaller but more credible story, and the next survey cycle is redesigned to pull outcomes from separate systems.

## Interpreting a single leader's dimension profile

**Scenario:**

A department head receives a feedback report and wants to know whether a lower score on one of the Four I's means they should overhaul how they lead.

**Walkthrough:**

Illustrative scenario: the leader scores 4.0 on three dimensions and 3.0 on intellectual stimulation, based on ratings from 8 direct reports and 3 peers. The coach first checks whether the gap holds across both rater groups; it does for reports but not for peers. Because dimension scores overlap and are measured imprecisely, the coach frames the result as a prompt rather than a verdict. Together they read the written comments, which mention that team meetings rarely invite alternative approaches.

The leader agrees to one concrete change, asking for at least one alternative before each major decision, and the pair plan to review it at the next assessment cycle.
