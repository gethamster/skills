# Examples: Prioritizing Outcomes Across Product Teams

## Three teams, seven candidate outcomes

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a product area with three squads gathers candidate outcomes for the next quarter. Seven come in, from activation to reporting accuracy to a partner integration that sales wants. Each squad has room for one or two outcomes.

**Walkthrough:**

The head of product publishes the criteria first: fit with this year's retention objective, expected value, confidence from evidence, and effort. Five scorers rate each outcome alone. The scores agree on the top two, activation for new admins and fewer failed data imports, and diverge sharply on the partner integration.

In the calibration session, the sales lead shares signed-deal notes showing two prospects waiting on the integration, and the engineering lead shares an estimate that it would take a full squad for the quarter. The group ranks it third. Capacity allows two squads on the top two outcomes and the third squad on discovery for the integration. The published rationale explains what evidence would move the integration up next quarter.

## A mature product and cost of delay

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a long-established invoicing product has four unrelated goals competing for one team: a bug backlog driving churn complaints, a tax-rule change due in a few months, a request for multi-currency support, and a dated reporting screen.

**Walkthrough:**

Because the product is mature and the goals do not form a sequence, the product manager uses cost of delay, following Roman Pichler's advice for this stage. The tax-rule change has a fixed deadline and a large cost if missed, so it goes first. The bug backlog is next, since churn complaints are rising each month. Multi-currency support has a modest delay cost because few customers need it yet. The reporting screen can wait.

The ranking is shared with the stakeholders who asked for multi-currency and reporting, along with the cost of delay reasoning and a date to revisit.

## When the formula and the strategy disagree

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: two teams use a RICE-style score to rank outcomes. A small conversion tweak scores highest because it is cheap and well evidenced. A new-segment outcome scores lower because confidence is low.

**Walkthrough:**

Reading the ranked list against the strategy, the head of product sees that entering the new segment is the company's main objective for the year. The score reflects low confidence. That is a reason to learn quickly before committing a full team.

The group keeps the conversion tweak as a small task for one team and gives the new-segment outcome a discovery allocation with a clear learning goal. The rationale records that the ranking was adjusted for strategic fit and why, so the decision can be checked at the next review.
