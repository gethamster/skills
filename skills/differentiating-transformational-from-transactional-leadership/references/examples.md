# Examples: Differentiating Transformational from Transactional Leadership

## The target-hitting team that stopped improving

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a support team of 8 people consistently meets its weekly ticket target, earns its recognition, and offers no suggestions for reducing ticket volume.

**Walkthrough:**

The lead runs the inventory and finds almost every interaction is exchange: targets, a leaderboard, corrections when response times slip. Nothing in the week asks why tickets arrive in the first place. She keeps the target and the recognition exactly as they are, so the exchange stays reliable. She adds a short monthly session where the team examines the top recurring ticket types and proposes one process change.

Each proposal gets an owner and a date before the session ends. Within two cycles the team is contributing ideas without any loss in target performance.

## The inspired project with slipping dates

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a product lead has energized a team of 5 around a new offering, but three of the last four milestones slipped and nobody is sure who owns integration testing.

**Walkthrough:**

The inventory shows the opposite imbalance: lots of purpose and brainstorming, almost no explicit terms. The lead writes one-sentence expectations for each person with a date and an owner. He agrees two management-by-exception triggers: any milestone at risk is flagged two days ahead, and any unowned task is raised at standup. He keeps the vision conversations but ends each one with a round of stated next actions.

The team keeps its energy and the ownership gap on integration testing closes in the first week.

## Choosing the mix for a routine versus a novel task

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: an operations manager oversees a monthly reconciliation that must be identical every time and a new vendor-selection effort with no precedent.

**Walkthrough:**

For the reconciliation, she leans on contingent reward and monitoring: a checklist, a due date, a clear standard and a trigger for review if totals do not match. She does not ask the team to reinvent it, because variation is the risk. For vendor selection, she leans on engagement: why the choice matters, which assumptions about current vendors might be wrong, and what each person wants to learn from the process. Even there, she sets an owner and a decision date.

The same manager, in the same week, applies different proportions of each style to different work.
