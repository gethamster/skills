# Examples: Segmenting Customers by Unmet Needs

## Accounts payable: two very different struggles

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a team has survey data from 400 office managers on outcomes for paying supplier invoices, plus profiling questions on company size, invoice volume and current tool.

**Walkthrough:**

Factor analysis groups the outcomes into a few factors that line up with job steps: confirming details, matching invoices to orders and tracking payments after sending. Clustering on those factors gives a clear three-segment solution that holds up when re-run on half the data.

One segment struggles mostly with confirming payee details and is named "Worried about paying the wrong party". Another struggles with matching invoices to orders and is named "Drowning in reconciliation". The third is fairly well served. Profiling shows the reconciliation segment skews toward firms with high invoice volume, but the worried segment cuts across every company size, so the team knows it cannot target it by size alone.

## Recipe app: households with a planning problem

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a recipe app team has survey data from 300 home cooks and suspects that families with children have different needs.

**Walkthrough:**

The team clusters on outcome ratings without using household type. One segment stands out for struggling with the define step: deciding what to cook for people with different preferences. When profiled, it contains many households with children but also shared adult households with mixed diets.

The team names it "Cooking for mixed tastes" rather than "Families", because the need, not the household type, defines it. That changes its targeting: messages address mixed preferences, which reach both groups.

## A segmentation that did not hold up

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a small team clusters survey data from 80 respondents and gets one large segment and several very small ones.

**Walkthrough:**

The small segments change every time the team re-runs the analysis, and none of them matches anything in the interview notes. The team concludes the sample is too small to split reliably. It reports market-wide opportunity scores instead, notes the one subgroup difference that matched interviews, and plans a larger survey before trying segmentation again.
