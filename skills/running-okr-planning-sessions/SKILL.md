# How to Run an OKR Planning Session

> How to run an OKR planning session: prepare context, draft and test objectives and key results together, calibrate ambition, and leave with an agreed set.

## Overview

An OKR planning session is the working meeting where a team turns company direction and its own knowledge into the OKRs it will commit to for the next cycle. In the [OKR method](../../methods/objectives-and-key-results-okrs/METHOD.md), this is where objectives are chosen, key results are drafted, ambition is set, and the set is labelled committed or aspirational. Quarterly OKR planning is the most common rhythm, so for most teams this is a quarterly meeting.

The session sits at a specific point in the cycle. In the [typical OKR cycle](https://www.whatmatters.com/resources/a-typical-okr-cycle) John Doerr describes, senior leaders start brainstorming company OKRs 4 to 6 weeks before the quarter and communicate them 2 weeks before it; teams then develop their own OKRs based on the company's and share them at the start of the quarter. The team planning session belongs in the gap between the company OKRs arriving and the team OKRs being shared.

Google's re:Work guide describes the balance the session has to strike: successful OKRs often come from a mix of top-down and bottom-up suggestions, so people across the organization can say what they believe is worth their time ([re:Work](https://rework.withgoogle.com/intl/en/guides/set-goals-with-okrs)). A session where the leader reads out pre-written OKRs gets compliance. A session with no company context gets OKRs that point anywhere. The facilitator's job is to hold both.

The session also needs time. Google's playbook warns that OKRs written in five minutes probably are not good ([Google's OKR playbook](https://www.whatmatters.com/resources/google-okr-playbook)). Doerr told Betterworks that getting the OKR process right is trial and error and usually takes a company 1-2 quarters ([Betterworks interview with Doerr](https://www.betterworks.com/keys-okr-success-qa-john-doerr/)), so the first sessions will be rougher than later ones.

The output is a draft OKR set for the team: a few objectives, each with key results, baselines, owners and a committed or aspirational label, plus a list of cross-team dependencies to take into the alignment step.

## How It Works

Preparation decides most of the outcome. Before the meeting, the facilitator gathers the company OKRs, last cycle's grades and reflections, the team's key metrics, and any known capacity constraints. Participants read this in advance so the meeting is spent on decisions.

The meeting itself moves from wide to narrow. First the team reviews context: what the company is trying to achieve, what the team learned last cycle, and what has changed. Then people generate candidate priorities individually before discussing them, which keeps the loudest voice from setting the agenda. The group clusters the candidates and narrows them to the few that would make the biggest difference, using [What Matters' questions](https://www.whatmatters.com/faqs/okr-examples-and-how-to-write-them) about what would get better and why it matters now.

Once the priorities are chosen, the group drafts objectives and key results. Small groups can draft in parallel, one objective each, and then bring drafts back to the whole team for testing against the objective and key result rules. The facilitator keeps the checks visible: outcome or activity, sufficient or not, measurable or not.

Ambition is calibrated explicitly. Google's playbook frames it in capacity terms: committed OKRs should credibly consume most, but not all, of the team's resources, and committed plus aspirational should consume somewhat more than it has ([Google's OKR playbook](https://www.whatmatters.com/resources/google-okr-playbook)). Christina Wodtke offers a quick gut check for stretch goals: rate confidence from 1 to 10, and treat a roughly 50/50 chance of making it as probably the right level of stretch ([Wodtke, The Art of the OKR, Redux](https://eleganthack.com/the-art-of-the-okr-redux/)). The team applies whichever check it has agreed on and labels each OKR.

The session closes with ownership and next steps. Every key result gets an owner and a baseline or a plan to establish one. Dependencies on other teams are listed with a named person to raise each one. The draft goes to the alignment review rather than straight to publication.

## Step-by-Step Guide

### Step 1: Prepare and send the pre-read

A week before the session, send the company OKRs, last cycle's grades and reflections, key metrics with current values, and known constraints such as holidays or fixed commitments. Ask each participant to bring a short list of candidate priorities. Book enough time; a first session usually needs most of a day, and later ones less.

### Step 2: Open with context

Start with the company OKRs and what they ask of this team. Review last cycle briefly: which OKRs were met, which were missed, and what the team learned. Name anything that has changed since. Keep this part short and factual so the rest of the meeting has a shared starting point.

### Step 3: Generate and narrow priorities

Have each person write candidates silently, then share and cluster them. Ask of each cluster what would get better if it were done and why now. Narrow to the few priorities the team will write objectives for, and explicitly park the rest as business as usual or future candidates.

### Step 4: Draft objectives and key results

Split into small groups, one priority each, and draft an objective with about three key results including baselines. Bring drafts back and test them together: is the objective an end state, are the key results outcomes, and would meeting them prove the objective? Rewrite on the spot where the tests fail.

### Step 5: Calibrate ambition and label each OKR

Walk through the set against the team's capacity using the [playbook's resource test](https://www.whatmatters.com/resources/google-okr-playbook), and ask for a confidence rating on each aspirational key result. Adjust targets that everyone is sure of or that nobody believes. Label each OKR committed or aspirational and write the label down.

### Step 6: Assign owners and list dependencies

Give every key result one owner. List every dependency on another team, with the person who will raise it and by when. These go into the cross-team alignment review, as described in [re:Work](https://rework.withgoogle.com/intl/en/guides/set-goals-with-okrs), before the OKRs are final.

### Step 7: Close and follow up

Read the draft set aloud and confirm the team can commit to it. Record open questions and who will resolve them. Send the draft within a day, with a date for the alignment review and for publication.

## Best Practices

- Separate the facilitator from the decision-maker. A neutral facilitator can challenge a leader's pet objective and keep time without seeming to take sides.
- Generate before discussing. Silent individual drafting surfaces priorities from quieter people and from those closest to customers, which is the point of bottom-up input.
- Start small when new to OKRs. [What Matters](https://www.whatmatters.com/faqs/rolling-out-okrs-objectives-and-key-results) suggests a pilot of no more than 2-3 objectives, and a team that tries to cover everything in its first session tends to finish with a task list.
- Keep last cycle's lessons on the wall. The reflections from grading are the best evidence of what the team tends to over- or under-estimate.
- Name an OKR shepherd. Doerr recommends one person who educates the team, tracks progress and tweaks the process ([Betterworks interview with Doerr](https://www.betterworks.com/keys-okr-success-qa-john-doerr/)).
- Keep stretch believable. What Matters notes that [stretch goals are not meant to be set so high](https://www.whatmatters.com/resources/okrs-stretch-goals-difference-between) that the OKR is unrealistic, so reset any target nobody in the room believes.
- Timebox each phase. Drafting expands to fill the day; fixed slots keep time for calibration and ownership, which are the parts most often skipped.

## Common Mistakes

- **Presenting finished OKRs for approval**: The team learns that the session is theatre and stops contributing. Bring company context and let the team write the answers.
- **No pre-read**: The first hour goes to explaining numbers. Send context in advance and start from decisions.
- **Drafting key results before choosing priorities**: The team argues about metrics for objectives it has not agreed on. Narrow priorities first.
- **Skipping the ambition check**: Without it, sets are either all safe or all impossible. Run the capacity and confidence checks before labelling.
- **Leaving without owners**: Unowned key results go unreported at check-ins. Assign one owner per key result in the room.
