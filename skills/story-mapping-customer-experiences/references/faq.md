# FAQ: Story Mapping Customer Experiences

## How many customer interviews do I need before creating my first experience map?

As a rule of thumb, aim for at least 5 interviews focused on the specific journey you're mapping before looking for patterns. With fewer, you're essentially mapping one or two individual experiences rather than a generalizable journey. Somewhere around 8-12 interviews tends to work well for a first map, enough to see repeating patterns and conflicting experiences while still being manageable for extraction and synthesis. Don't wait for perfection though; start small and update the map as you complete more interviews through your weekly rhythm.

The map improves incrementally, so the first version doesn't need to be comprehensive.

## Should I create the experience map before or after building my opportunity solution tree?

The experience map feeds the opportunity solution tree, so it should come first, or at least concurrently. The map is where you identify and name opportunities; the tree is where you organize those opportunities under outcomes and explore solutions. That said, you don't need a complete map before starting your tree. Many teams build a rough map, extract the first few opportunities, add them to the tree, and then continue enriching the map as new interview data arrives.

See [building opportunity solution trees](https://tryhamster.com/skills/building-opportunity-solution-trees) for how to structure the tree once you have opportunities.

## How is customer experience story mapping different from a standard user journey map or service blueprint?

Standard user journey maps typically chart the steps a user takes within your product interface, so they're product-centric. Service blueprints add the backstage operations (support processes, systems, policies) behind the customer-facing experience. Customer experience story mapping, as practiced in continuous discovery, is data-centric rather than design-centric: every element on the map must trace to a real customer story from an interview, not to a team's projection of how things should work. It also maps the customer's full context, including what happens before and after your product, and explicitly includes an emotional layer.

The key differentiator is the source of truth: interview data, not workshops or process documentation.

## What tool should I use to build the experience map?

The tool matters far less than the data quality. Physical whiteboards with sticky notes work well for co-located teams because the tactile nature encourages participation and makes rearranging easy. For remote or hybrid teams, any shared whiteboard that supports sticky notes, color coding, and simultaneous editors works well. Some teams use simple spreadsheets with phases as columns and lanes as rows, which is perfectly adequate and easy to update asynchronously.

Start with whatever your team already uses for collaboration and migrate to specialized tools only if you hit a genuine limitation.

## How do I handle conflicting data from different customers on the same map?

Conflicting data is a feature, not a bug, because it reveals segment differences, edge cases, or evolving experiences. When two customers describe contradictory experiences in the same phase, place both notes on the map and tag them with the customer identifiers, then mark these conflicts explicitly during your annotation pass. Common causes include different customer segments (power users vs. novices), different use cases (solo users vs. team users), or different time periods (customers who onboarded long ago vs. last week). If conflicts cluster heavily in one phase, it may indicate you need to split your map into two segment-specific maps.

## Why does my experience map keep producing obvious opportunities that everyone already knew about?

This usually means the map is too shallow: you're capturing surface-level actions but missing the emotional, cognitive, and contextual layers that reveal non-obvious opportunities. Common causes are interview questions that are too product-focused ("How do you use Feature X?") rather than journey-focused ("Walk me through the last time you tried to accomplish Y"), swim lanes that omit emotions and thoughts, and a journey scope that starts and ends at your product's boundaries. The fix is to expand your interview protocol (see [conducting weekly customer interviews](https://tryhamster.com/skills/conducting-weekly-customer-interviews)) and add the emotional and contextual lanes. The most valuable opportunities are often hiding in the spaces between tools and in the emotional valleys that actions alone don't reveal.
