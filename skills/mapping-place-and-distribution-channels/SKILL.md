# Place in the Marketing Mix: Mapping Distribution Channels

> Plan place in the marketing mix: map the distribution channels and access points where customers find, buy and receive a product or service.

## Overview

Place is the P that decides where and how customers get the offer. Wikipedia's summary of the marketing mix defines it as the "direct or indirect channels to market, geographical distribution, territorial coverage, retail outlet, market location, catalogues, inventory, logistics, and order fulfillment" ([Wikipedia: Marketing mix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marketing_mix)). In the [7 P's Marketing Mix](../../methods/7-p-s-marketing-mix/METHOD.md), Place has to deliver the experience the price and promotion promise.

For services, place is mostly about access. The general account of distribution puts it directly: in the case of services, distribution is principally concerned with access ([Wikipedia: Distribution (marketing)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_%28marketing%29)). Access includes physical locations, opening hours, booking systems, response times, websites and apps, and the partners who sell or deliver on your behalf. A service nobody can reach at the moment they need it does not get bought.

Borden's original list of marketing mix elements already treated channels as a set of policy decisions: which channels to use between the producer and the customer, how selective to be among resellers, and how to gain the cooperation of the trade ([Borden, The Concept of the Marketing Mix](https://www.guillaumenicaise.com/wp-content/uploads/2013/10/Borden-1984_The-concept-of-marketing-mix.pdf)). Those questions still frame a marketing mix distribution strategy, with digital channels added to the list.

The skill shows how to choose distribution channels and set a 7 P's place strategy. Use it when launching an offer, entering a new region or segment, adding a partner or reseller, or when a 7 Ps audit shows customers struggle to find, buy or reach the service.

## How It Works

The skill maps channels across the customer's path and then decides where to invest. It rests on three decisions: direct or indirect, how much coverage, and how the channels work together.

Direct distribution means the business sells and delivers itself. Indirect distribution uses intermediaries such as wholesalers, retailers, agents or brokers ([Wikipedia: Distribution (marketing)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_%28marketing%29)). Direct channels give control over the experience and the customer relationship. Indirect channels give reach but hand part of the experience to someone else. Services have their own intermediaries, including agents, brokers and surrogate consumers who choose on the customer's behalf ([Wikipedia: Services marketing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Services_marketing)).

Coverage sets how widely the offer is available. Mass (or intensive) distribution uses as many outlets as possible, selective distribution restricts outlets to those that can support the offer, and exclusive distribution uses one intermediary or one type of intermediary to keep control ([Wikipedia: Distribution (marketing)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_%28marketing%29)). Coverage is also a positioning signal: selective and exclusive coverage suit premium offers that need expert support, while mass coverage suits simple, low-priced ones.

The channels also have to work together. Customers often discover an offer in one channel, evaluate it in another and buy in a third. Omnichannel strategy aims at "seamless and effortless, high-quality customer experiences that occur within and between contact channels," in the definition from Frost and Sullivan quoted on [Wikipedia: Omnichannel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Omnichannel). In practice that means consistent prices, information and service standards across channels, and handoffs that do not make the customer start again.

Every channel must also be able to deliver the service Ps. A reseller who cannot explain the offer weakens People. A booking site that loses details breaks Process. Shostack noted that a service blueprint gives distribution "a map to be duplicated" ([Shostack, Designing Services That Deliver](https://strategicdesignthinking.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2012/11/hbr-shostackpdf.pdf)), which is the practical tool for copying a service to a new location or partner without losing quality.

## Step-by-Step Guide

### Step 1: Map how customers find, buy and receive the offer

Talk to recent customers and check your sales and web data to learn where they first heard of you, where they evaluated the offer, how they bought and how they receive the service. Draw these as stages across the page. Note where customers drop out or ask for help. This is the current channel map.

### Step 2: List the channel options at each stage

For each stage, list the channels you use and the realistic alternatives: your own website or app, your own sales team, locations, marketplaces, resellers, agents and referral partners. Include the access points that matter for a service, such as booking, opening hours and response times. Mark each as direct or indirect.

### Step 3: Choose the coverage level

Decide whether the offer should be widely available, available through a selected few outlets, or exclusive, using the mass, selective and exclusive options described in the [distribution literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_%28marketing%29). Match the choice to the price and positioning. A premium, advice-heavy service usually needs selective coverage with trained partners.

### Step 4: Score channels and choose distribution channels

Score the candidate channels on reach in the target segment, cost to acquire and serve a customer, control over the experience, and fit with how customers want to buy. Use your own data where you have it and small tests where you do not. Drop channels that cannot deliver the experience the price implies, even if they bring volume.

### Step 5: Design the handoffs between channels

Decide how a customer moves between channels, for example from a partner referral to your onboarding, or from a website enquiry to a sales call. Make sure prices, information and service standards match across channels. Assign an owner for each handoff.

### Step 6: Equip partners and locations to deliver the service

Give each indirect channel or new location what it needs to deliver the service Ps: training, scripts, materials and a documented process. Use the service blueprint as the map to copy. Agree how you will check quality and handle complaints that arise through the partner.

### Step 7: Measure and review the channel mix

Track results per channel: acquisition, conversion, cost, satisfaction and complaints. Review the channel mix when a channel's costs rise, a partner's quality slips, or customers start buying in new places. Record why each channel was added or dropped.

## Best Practices

- Map channels from the customer's side first. Internal channel reports show where sales are booked, which is often a different place from where customers made up their minds.
- Treat access as part of the service. Opening hours, response times and booking steps are Place decisions for a service and belong on the channel map.
- Match coverage to positioning. Selling a premium service through every available outlet signals that it is ordinary.
- Keep prices and information consistent across channels. Customers who find different prices or promises in different channels lose trust.
- Give indirect channels the process and materials they need. A partner who delivers a weaker version of the service damages your brand as well as its own.

## Common Mistakes

- **Adding channels for reach alone**: More channels add cost and complexity and can dilute the experience. Add a channel when it reaches the target segment in a way that fits the offer.
- **Ignoring the service experience in partner channels**: Resellers and agents shape how customers experience the service. Train them and check quality, or keep the channel direct.
- **Breaking the handoff between channels**: Customers who must repeat themselves when moving from one channel to another drop out. Design and own each handoff.
- **Measuring channels on volume only**: A channel that brings many customers who churn quickly may cost more than it earns. Measure cost to serve and retention as well.
- **Forgetting access for services**: A good location with hours that do not suit customers, or a website with no way to book, is a Place problem. Include access points in the map.
