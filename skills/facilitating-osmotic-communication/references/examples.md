# Examples: Facilitating Osmotic Communication in Agile Teams

## Cutting question wait time after a reorganisation

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a team of six developers and one tester sits across two rows separated by tall partitions, and the product specialist works on another floor. Developers report waiting most of a day for answers to simple business-rule questions.

**Walkthrough:**

The team lead maps the longest waits and finds most involve the product specialist and the tester. She moves the team into one area with low partitions, seats the tester beside the two developers whose code changes most often, and arranges for the product specialist to sit with the team three afternoons a week. The team agrees to ask quick questions aloud. After a few weeks, developers say most rule questions get answered in the room, often by someone who overheard a similar exchange earlier.

The tester starts spotting risky changes during conversations instead of at test time.

## Restoring concentration in an over-noisy room

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a team adopted an open layout and pair programming, but after a month several members wear headphones all day and two regularly book meeting rooms just to write code.

**Walkthrough:**

The team lead recognises that the awareness layer is working but the counterweight layer is missing. At the reflection session the team agrees on a quiet block every morning from 9 to 11, where questions go to a written channel unless something is blocking. They also claim a small room next to the team area for pairing sessions that get loud and for personal calls. Headphone use drops outside quiet hours, and people stop leaving the area to work.

Background conversation in the afternoon still carries the context everyone relies on.

## Testing a remote substitute honestly

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a five-person team moves to a hybrid arrangement where two members work remotely three days a week. The lead worries that remote members will miss the context the room provides.

**Walkthrough:**

Rather than assuming a group call replaces the shared room, the lead picks three signals to watch: how long remote members wait for answers, how often they learn about conflicting changes late, and how many written handoffs appear. The team tries an always-open audio room during core hours for a few weeks. Remote members report hearing some useful talk but missing much of it, and late surprises continue. The team shifts in-office days so the whole group overlaps on the days with the most integration work.

The lead keeps reviewing the signals at each reflection session.
