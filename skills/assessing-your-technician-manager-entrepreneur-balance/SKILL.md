# E-Myth Self-Assessment: Find Your Dominant Role

> An E-Myth self-assessment that scores your time, energy and comfort in the Technician, Manager and Entrepreneur roles and names your biggest gap.

## Overview

This E-Myth self-assessment measures which of Michael Gerber's three business personalities runs your working life. The [technician manager entrepreneur framework](../../methods/technician-manager-entrepreneur-framework/METHOD.md) explains why owners need to balance technician, manager and entrepreneur work; this skill tells you where you stand today, so the changes you make are aimed at the real gap.

Gerber's starting observation is that most owners are Technicians first. EMyth's [about page](https://www.emyth.com/about-us) puts it as "most businesses are started by Technicians." A StoryShots summary of The E-Myth Revisited states that the typical small business owner is "only 10 percent Entrepreneur, 20 percent Manager, and 70 percent technician" ([StoryShots](https://www.getstoryshots.com/books/the-e-myth-revisited-summary/)). That is a generalization about typical owners. Your own numbers may differ a lot, which is why you measure them.

The assessment looks at three things for each role. Time is where your hours actually go. Energy is whether the work drains or recharges you. Comfort is where you retreat by default when things get hard. The three together are more useful than time alone. An owner who spends little time on Entrepreneur work but finds it energizing needs protected time. An owner who avoids it because it feels uncomfortable needs practice and structure.

The output is a one-page scorecard and a single named gap. One gap is deliberate: trying to rebalance all three roles at once usually changes nothing. The gap you name becomes the input for the sibling skills, such as [role-based time allocation](../designing-role-based-time-allocation/SKILL.md) or [building business systems](../building-systems-as-the-manager-role/SKILL.md).

Treat the result as a snapshot of habits. People are quick to call themselves "a Technician" as if it were a fixed trait. Gerber's framing, as [Shortform summarizes it](https://www.shortform.com/blog/startup-roles/), is that an owner possesses all three "to varying degrees", which means the mix can change with practice.

## How It Works

Each role has recognizable behaviors, and the assessment maps your activities and preferences onto them. [Shortform's guide](https://www.shortform.com/blog/startup-roles/) describes the three clearly. The Entrepreneur "is always thinking of the future" and "has a strong need for control." The Manager "uses planning and systems to create order and predictability" and "sees problems rather than opportunities." The Technician "prefers to do things himself to ensure that they're done right" and "lives in the present."

The time score comes from a log. Every activity from a recent working period gets tagged by purpose. The key rule is to tag each activity by what it accomplishes. Designing a new feature in the design tool yourself is Technician work even though it feels creative. Writing the checklist that lets someone else design it is Manager work. Deciding whether the feature should exist at all is Entrepreneur work.

The energy and comfort scores come from short self-ratings. For each role, rate on a simple scale how much its typical tasks drain or restore you, and how naturally you drift toward them under pressure. Keep the scale small so the rating is quick and honest.

The short technician manager entrepreneur quiz below adds a check on the log. Answer each statement below with agree, neutral or disagree. These statements are this skill's own prompts, written from the role descriptions above. They are not an official E-Myth instrument.

- Technician: When a job matters, I would rather do it myself. I feel most productive when I finish something tangible.
- Manager: I notice when work is done differently each time and want to fix it. I like having a checklist or process to follow.
- Entrepreneur: I often think about where the business should be in a few years. I get restless when nothing is changing.

The gap is the difference between where you are and where your stage needs you. Gerber describes three phases of business growth, infancy, adolescence and maturity ([StoryShots](https://www.getstoryshots.com/books/the-e-myth-revisited-summary/)). In the infancy stage, the summary says, the owner is "running all parts of the business." Growing out of that stage takes more Manager and Entrepreneur time. Pick the role with the largest shortfall for your phase, and check it against the energy and comfort scores to decide whether the fix is more time, more practice, or both.

## Step-by-Step Guide

### Step 1: Gather your activity data

Pull your calendar, task list and sent email for a recent normal working period. List every recurring activity and estimate the hours it takes each week. Break broad items like "meetings" into their real types, such as client delivery calls, team check-ins and planning sessions. If your records are thin, track the coming week instead.

### Step 2: Tag each activity by purpose

Mark each activity Technician, Manager or Entrepreneur based on what it accomplishes. When an activity mixes roles, split it, for example a client call that is half delivery and half scoping the next project. Be strict with anything you do yourself that someone else could do; it is Technician time.

### Step 3: Total the time by role

Add up the hours for each role and turn them into shares of your week. Compare the result with what you would have guessed before tracking. The difference between the guess and the log is often the most useful finding of the whole exercise.

### Step 4: Score energy and comfort

For each role, rate how its tasks affect your energy and how strongly you default to it under pressure. Then answer the quiz statements. Look for mismatches, such as low time and high energy in the Entrepreneur role, which points to a scheduling problem rather than a skills problem.

### Step 5: Place yourself in a phase

Decide whether the business is in Gerber's infancy, adolescence or maturity. Note what the phase demands: an infant business needs a lot of hands-on work, while a business trying to grow past the founder needs systems and direction. Write down the balance you think your phase needs for the next quarter.

### Step 6: Name the single biggest gap

Compare your time shares with the balance you wrote down and pick the role with the largest shortfall. Write one sentence describing the cost of that gap in concrete terms, such as "no one else can onboard a client" or "we have not reviewed pricing this year". Resist naming a second gap; it can wait for the next assessment.

### Step 7: Record the scorecard and set a review date

Put the time shares, energy and comfort scores, quiz results, phase and chosen gap on one page. Share it with a co-founder, coach or advisor if you have one. Set a date to repeat the assessment, usually next quarter, and hand the gap to the skill that addresses it.

## Best Practices

- Use records rather than memory. Calendars and sent email are more honest than recollection.
- Tag by purpose. The same meeting can be Technician or Manager work depending on what you do in it.
- Keep the energy and comfort ratings quick. First reactions are more honest than long deliberation.
- Assess co-founders separately, then together. Each person's scorecard matters, and so does whether the team as a whole covers all three roles.
- Repeat on a schedule. A single assessment is a snapshot, and the value comes from comparing snapshots as the business changes.

## Common Mistakes

- **Tagging by appearance**: Calling every creative task Entrepreneur work inflates that score. Ask whether you are deciding direction or doing the work.
- **Treating the result as identity**: "I am a Technician" becomes a reason not to change. Read the scorecard as habits you can shift.
- **Copying a ratio from a book**: Gerber's typical-owner split is a description, and adopting it as your target ignores your stage and team. Set your own target.
- **Fixing everything at once**: Three simultaneous changes rarely hold. Work on the single largest gap until it closes.
- **Skipping the follow-up**: Without a review date the assessment becomes a one-time insight. Put the next one on the calendar before you finish.
