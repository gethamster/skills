# Examples: Sketching Competing Solutions Individually

## Onboarding for a budgeting app

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a six-person sprint team at a budgeting app wants new users to connect a bank account in their first session, and the map shows most drop-off at the account-linking screen.

**Walkthrough:**

During Notes, the product designer copies the long-term goal and circles two Lightning Demo patterns: a progress bar from a tax tool and a preview of insights from a fitness app. In Ideas she jots five concepts, from a skip option to a sample dashboard filled with fake data. Crazy 8s goes to the sample dashboard, and her panels vary what the preview shows and where the link prompt appears. Her Solution Sketch, titled 'Show Me First', has three panels: a demo dashboard with real headline copy, a prompt reading 'See this with your own spending', and the linked account view.

An engineer on the team independently sketches a completely different concept about trust badges, so the wall holds genuinely competing directions for the decision step.

## A support lead who cannot draw

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a customer support lead joins a sprint about reducing refund requests and warns the facilitator that she cannot draw at all.

**Walkthrough:**

The facilitator reminds the room that boxes, arrows and words are enough. The support lead's Notes page is dense with quotes from the expert interviews, and her Ideas page is almost entirely text. In Crazy 8s she draws eight rectangles with different headline wording for a pre-refund message. Her Solution Sketch uses three sticky notes with a short sentence under each box describing what the customer reads and clicks.

It is the most readable sketch on the wall because every panel is labelled, and it earns several dots in the later review.

## Fixing a sketch that needed narration

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: halfway through the Solution Sketch step, a founder realises his storyboard is a single dense screen full of arrows that only he understands.

**Walkthrough:**

He applies the stranger test and admits nobody could follow the flow without him explaining it. He splits the screen into three panels: what the customer sees on arrival, the choice they make, and what happens after. He replaces arrow shorthand with short captions and writes the actual button text. He gives the sketch a title and leaves his name off before posting.

During the silent read, teammates understand the concept without asking him anything, which is the signal the sketch is ready.
