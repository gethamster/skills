# How design sprint solution sketching works, step by step

> Turn the sprint's shared research into one clear, self-explanatory solution storyboard by sketching alone, in silence, through four timed steps.

## At a Glance

| Field | Value |
|-------|-------|
| Difficulty | Beginner |
| Time to Learn | One focused afternoon session |
| Outcome | One anonymous, titled storyboard per participant that a teammate can read and understand without the author explaining it. |
| Prerequisites | A framed sprint challenge with a long-term goal and sprint questions, A problem map and How Might We notes from the mapping day, Lightning Demo notes on relevant competitor or precedent ideas, Paper, markers and a room (or call) where everyone can work silently |
| Part of | [GV Design Sprint](../../methods/gv-design-sprint/METHOD.md) |

## Overview

Sketching competing solutions individually is the part of a design sprint where every participant turns the shared research into one concrete proposal of their own. In GV's format this happens on [Tuesday afternoon, on paper, with each person sketching individually](https://designelite.co/en/blog/design-sprint) rather than brainstorming as a group. For the history of the sprint and how the other days fit around this one, see the [GV Design Sprint method page](https://tryhamster.com/methods/gv-design-sprint). This page covers only how to do the sketch well.

The reason for working alone is structural, not stylistic. [GV's method gives each team member responsibility for developing an independent solution](https://gv.com/sprint), which is why practitioner guides insist on no group brainstorming and no whiteboard consensus. When a room ideates out loud, the first confident idea anchors everyone else, and the group drifts toward a compromise nobody would have drawn alone. When people sketch separately, the output is [one proposed solution per participant](https://designelite.co/en/blog/design-sprint), so the team can compare genuinely competing concepts before anyone argues for one.

GV frames the activity as [a four-step process that emphasizes critical thinking over artistic ability](https://gv.com/sprint). The steps move from broad to narrow: Notes collects what the team already knows, Ideas gets rough concepts on paper, Crazy 8s stretches the most promising concept into variations, and the Solution Sketch commits to one end-to-end proposal. Nobody needs drawing skill. The standard, as one research review puts it, is that [a sketch can be rough or ugly as long as the idea and how it works are clear](https://geyfman.org/dossiers/gv-design-sprint-deep-research-report).

The inputs are the artifacts the sprint has already produced: [the long-term goal, the map, the How Might We notes and the Lightning Demos](https://paulminors.com/blog/sprint-jake-knapp-book-summary-pdf). Lightning Demos matter most here because they supply precedent; teams [evaluate ideas from competitors and record what they like](https://ideacenter.nd.edu/news-and-events/news/the-power-of-the-google-ventures-design-sprint) so it can inform new concepts. If those artifacts are thin, the sketches will be thin too, because the Notes step has nothing to draw from.

You know the session worked when the wall holds several distinct, self-explanatory storyboards that a stranger could read without the author in the room. You know it failed when sketches are vague single frames, when several of them are the same idea because people talked during Ideas, or when a sketch only makes sense after its author explains it. Those failures push problems into the decision step, where the team ends up critiquing the explanation instead of the idea.

## How It Works

The four steps run back to back in one sitting, with the whole team working silently in the same room. A workshop recap with the method's author calls this [sketching alone together](https://voltagecontrol.com/blog/austin-workshop-w-jake-knapp): a shared space and a shared timer, but private pages. The facilitator keeps time and keeps the room quiet, and nobody shares work until the Solution Sketches are finished.

| Step | Time | Activity | Output |
|---|---|---|---|
| Notes | [20 min](https://pulserevops.com/sales-book-summaries/bs0194) | Review goal, map, HMW notes, demos | Notes with key items circled |
| Ideas | [20 min](https://bookey.app/book/sprint) | Private rough concepts in words and doodles | Page of ideas, best ones marked |
| Crazy 8s | [8 min](https://pulserevops.com/sales-book-summaries/bs0194) | [Eight variations of one idea](https://paulminors.com/blog/sprint-jake-knapp-book-summary-pdf) | Folded sheet of variants |
| Solution Sketch | [30-90 min](https://pulserevops.com/sales-book-summaries/bs0194) | Detailed storyboard with labels | One self-explanatory concept |

Each step narrows the funnel. Notes is a harvest, not a creative step: you [walk the room, copy the long-term goal, note anything useful from the map, the How Might We questions and the Lightning Demos](https://medium.com/v%C3%ADrgula-mas/sprint-jake-knapp-fa9a9328e9c0), and circle what matters most. Ideas turns those notes into [rough concepts in words, doodles, stick figures, diagrams or headlines](https://wind4change.com/design-sprint-google-venture-jake-knapp). You capture everything plausible first and only then [mark the most promising ones](https://paulminors.com/blog/sprint-jake-knapp-book-summary-pdf).

Crazy 8s is where people most often misread the exercise. You fold a sheet into sections and [take 60 seconds per section](https://shortform.com/pdf/sprint-pdf-jake-knapp-john-zeratsky-and-braden-kowitz) to sketch a variation of one promising idea. Practitioner guides describe it as [a rapid way to stretch and improve a concept](https://wind4change.com/design-sprint-google-venture-jake-knapp), not a demand for unrelated wild ideas. The time pressure stops you polishing the first version and forces you to consider layouts, sequences and wording you would otherwise skip.

The Solution Sketch is the only artifact the rest of the team will see. It is commonly drawn as [a three-panel storyboard showing the solution from beginning to end](https://bookey.app/book/sprint), and it has to be [detailed and self-contained, explaining how the problem gets solved and why the approach could work](https://paulminors.com/blog/sprint-jake-knapp-book-summary-pdf). Words carry as much weight as pictures: labels, headlines and annotations make the storyboard readable, which matters because the team will [walk the line of sketches and read each one](https://voltagecontrol.com/blog/austin-workshop-w-jake-knapp) before any discussion.

Guides disagree on how long the final step needs. One summary allows [30-90 minutes for the Solution Sketch](https://pulserevops.com/sales-book-summaries/bs0194), while a university team that ran a GV sprint describes [producing its detailed end-to-end solution in a much shorter window](https://ideacenter.nd.edu/news-and-events/news/the-power-of-the-google-ventures-design-sprint). Another guide puts the whole four-step sequence at [about an hour and a half](https://wind4change.com/design-sprint-google-venture-jake-knapp). Pick a time box before starting and announce it, so people pace their panels rather than stopping mid-storyboard.

## Step-by-Step Guide

### Step 1: Set up the room and the rules

Before the timer starts, give every participant paper, a marker and a hard surface, and check that the goal, map, How Might We notes and Lightning Demo notes are still visible. Explain that the next stretch is silent and individual: [everyone sketches on paper, with no group brainstorming](https://designelite.co/en/blog/design-sprint). Tell people drawing skill does not matter, since GV frames the exercise around [critical thinking over artistic ability](https://gv.com/sprint). Announce the time box for each step and for the final sketch.

Participants who know the finish line pace themselves and do not end with half a storyboard.

> **Pro tip:** Say out loud that stick figures and boxes are fine. The people most worried about drawing often hold the most domain knowledge.

### Step 2: Harvest notes from the room

Spend [about 20 minutes on Notes](https://pulserevops.com/sales-book-summaries/bs0194), walking the walls and copying anything that could shape a solution. Start by writing down the long-term goal, then pull [details from the map, the sprint questions and the inspirational examples](https://shortform.com/pdf/sprint-pdf-jake-knapp-john-zeratsky-and-braden-kowitz). Include the Lightning Demo patterns, which are the team's record of what other products already do well. Finish by circling the handful of notes that feel most important.

The output is a private page of raw material, not ideas yet.

> **Pro tip:** Copy the long-term goal at the top of your notes page so every later idea can be checked against it.

### Step 3: Get rough ideas down privately

Spend [roughly 20 minutes on Ideas](https://bookey.app/book/sprint), turning your notes into concepts. Use whatever form is fastest: [words, doodles, stick figures, diagrams or headlines](https://wind4change.com/design-sprint-google-venture-jake-knapp). Capture every plausible concept before judging any of them, then circle the most promising. Keep the page to yourself, because sharing now collapses the team's options into whichever idea is described first.

If you end with only one idea, return to your notes and ask what a product from the Lightning Demos would do instead.

### Step 4: Run Crazy 8s on your strongest idea

Fold a sheet into sections, choose one promising idea, and [sketch eight variations of it in eight minutes](https://paulminors.com/blog/sprint-jake-knapp-book-summary-pdf). Each panel gets [about 60 seconds](https://shortform.com/pdf/sprint-pdf-jake-knapp-john-zeratsky-and-braden-kowitz), so draw only what differs: a new layout, a different first screen, another way of wording the offer. Treat it as [iteration on a concept you already like](https://wind4change.com/design-sprint-google-venture-jake-knapp), not a hunt for unrelated ideas. When the timer ends, look across the panels and mark the version or combination that works best.

> **Pro tip:** If you run dry partway through, change one variable per panel: the headline, the order of steps, or the moment the customer first sees value.

### Step 5: Draw the three-panel Solution Sketch

Commit to one concept and develop it into [a three-panel storyboard that shows the solution from beginning to end](https://bookey.app/book/sprint). Use sticky notes or boxes for panels so you can rearrange them, and [write real labels, headlines and annotations](https://paulminors.com/blog/sprint-jake-knapp-book-summary-pdf) instead of squiggles standing in for text. Each panel should show what the customer sees and what they do next. The finished sketch must explain how the problem is solved and why the approach could work.

Stay inside the time box you announced, since guides range from [30-90 minutes](https://pulserevops.com/sales-book-summaries/bs0194) to [considerably shorter sessions](https://ideacenter.nd.edu/news-and-events/news/the-power-of-the-google-ventures-design-sprint).

> **Pro tip:** Imagine handing the sketch to a stranger. Wherever they would need you to explain a panel, add a label to that panel.

### Step 6: Title it, anonymize it and post it

Give the finished sketch [a catchy title and keep your name off it](https://geyfman.org/dossiers/gv-design-sprint-deep-research-report), so review focuses on the idea and the team has a handle to refer to it by. Post all sketches side by side on the wall. The team then [walks the line of sketches and reads each one](https://voltagecontrol.com/blog/austin-workshop-w-jake-knapp) before anyone judges or discusses them. Do not pitch your sketch or stand beside it during this read.

Evaluating the wall belongs to the decision step, covered in [making structured decisions under time constraints](https://tryhamster.com/skills/making-structured-decisions-under-time-constraints).

> **Pro tip:** Collect sketches face down and shuffle them before posting, so wall position gives no clue to authorship.

## Best Practices

- Copy the long-term goal onto your notes page before anything else. It keeps your solution tethered to what the sprint is trying to achieve, and it gives you a quick test to apply to every idea on your page.
- Write real words in the storyboard. Labels, headlines and annotations [make the storyboard understandable](https://paulminors.com/blog/sprint-jake-knapp-book-summary-pdf) instead of relying on pictures, and actual copy exposes a weak value proposition that a squiggle would hide.
- Borrow openly from the Lightning Demos. They exist so the team can [take ideas from competitors and other products](https://ideacenter.nd.edu/news-and-events/news/the-power-of-the-google-ventures-design-sprint) and build on them, and a remixed precedent is often more testable than a blank-page invention.
- Keep the room silent until every sketch is posted. Silence protects the independence that makes the concepts compete, which is the point of [assigning each person their own solution](https://gv.com/sprint).
- Announce time boxes up front and hold them. Because guides differ on how long the final sketch takes, choosing and stating one prevents some people from finishing early while others stop mid-panel.
- Tell one end-to-end story rather than listing features. A storyboard forces you to show the sequence the customer experiences, which is exactly what the prototype will need to reproduce later.
- Proofread for readability before posting. Read your sketch as if you had never seen it, and fix any panel whose meaning depends on something only you know.

## Common Mistakes

- **Treating the sketch as an art test and either freezing or spending the time on polish.** — Guides recommend [rough drawings, diagrams, stick figures and words](https://wind4change.com/design-sprint-google-venture-jake-knapp) over artistic skill. Judge your sketch on whether the idea is clear, not whether it looks good.
- **Stopping at a vague idea, such as one box labelled with a feature name.** — A label alone gives the team nothing to evaluate. The Solution Sketch should show [enough detail to communicate the solution clearly](https://bookey.app/book/sprint) and explain how it addresses the problem.
- **Drawing a sketch that only makes sense while its author narrates it.** — Sketches are [read silently on the wall before discussion](https://voltagecontrol.com/blog/austin-workshop-w-jake-knapp), so anything left to a spoken explanation is lost. Add labels and annotations until the storyboard stands on its own.
- **Talking through ideas during Notes or Ideas, so the wall fills with versions of one concept.** — Keep ideation [individual, with no group brainstorming](https://designelite.co/en/blog/design-sprint), until the Solution Sketches are done. Early conversation converges the team before real alternatives exist.
- **Using Crazy 8s to generate a set of unrelated wild ideas.** — The practitioner guide frames Crazy 8s as [a rapid way to iterate on and improve a promising idea](https://wind4change.com/design-sprint-google-venture-jake-knapp). Pick one concept and vary it panel by panel.
