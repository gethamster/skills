# FAQ: The OKR Scoring System

## How do you score an OKR from 0 to 1?

Score each key result as the share of its target achieved, or as 0 or 1 if it is binary. Then average the key result scores to get the objective's score, using equal weights or weights agreed at planning. Google's re:Work guide describes exactly this, calling the average "rough" because some key results are weighted.

## What is a good OKR score?

It depends on the type. For aspirational OKRs, Google expects an average around 0.7, and its re:Work guide calls 60-70% the sweet spot. For committed OKRs, the expected score is 1.0, and anything less needs an explanation. Read every score against the label it was given.

## What is the difference between committed and aspirational OKRs when grading?

Committed OKRs are promises, graded effectively as pass or fail against 1.0. Aspirational OKRs are stretch goals where partial success is expected; What Matters reports Google's bands for them as green from 0.7, yellow from 0.4 to 0.6, and red below that. A committed miss gets a postmortem, and an aspirational shortfall usually carries forward.

## Should scores be adjusted for circumstances?

They can be, openly. What Matters recommends a self-assessment step where the team can raise or lower a grade for context it can explain, such as a result with more impact than the number suggests. Keep the original score beside the adjusted one, so the record shows both.

## Should OKR scores affect bonuses or performance reviews?

No. Google's re:Work guide says OKRs are not synonymous with performance evaluation, and John Doerr advises against tying OKRs to bonus payments except for sales quotas. Grades used for pay push people toward safe targets, which undermines the stretch that aspirational OKRs rely on.
