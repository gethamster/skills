# FAQ: Scoring and Grading OKRs at End of Cycle

## How do I score a key result that was abandoned mid-cycle due to a strategic pivot?

0. 0 due to poor execution. 0 would distort your historical data and make it look like the team failed when they actually made a smart strategic call. In your interpretation notes, document why it was abandoned, when the decision was made, and what replaced it.

Track the percentage of N/A key results per cycle. If more than 15-20% are regularly abandoned, your planning process needs to improve, or your cycle length may be too long for your rate of strategic change.

## Should I score OKRs before or after the team retrospective?

Score before the retrospective. The numeric scoring should happen first, ideally with OKR owners submitting their proposed scores and interpretations individually before any group discussion. This prevents groupthink and anchoring bias. The retrospective then uses the scores as the input for a discussion about what happened and why.

If you score during the retrospective, the conversation will influence the numbers, and you will lose the diagnostic value of seeing where people's independent assessments diverge. Think of it as: scoring is data collection, the retrospective is data analysis.

## How long should the OKR scoring session take for a team of 8-10 people?

Plan for 45-60 minutes for a team with two to three objectives and six to nine total key results. Allocate 2-3 minutes per green item, 5-7 minutes per yellow item, and 10-12 minutes per red item, plus 10 minutes at the end for identifying cross-cutting patterns. If the session consistently runs over 60 minutes, you may have too many OKRs, your key results may be poorly defined (causing measurement debates), or you may be letting discussion drift into problem-solving mode. The scoring session should diagnose issues, not solve them.

Park solutions for follow-up meetings.

## Why does my team's OKR score keep coming out at exactly 0.7 every quarter?

A score that is suspiciously consistent quarter after quarter usually indicates one of two problems. Either the team is calibrating their targets backward from the score they want (setting targets they know they can 70% complete), or scores are being subtly inflated or deflated during the group discussion to converge on the "right" answer. Both undermine the system. To diagnose which, look at the variance across individual key results.

7, you have a mathematical coincidence that will likely resolve on its own. 7, you have a sandbagging or anchoring problem. Fix it by having OKR owners score independently before the group session and by explicitly challenging targets that feel too comfortable during planning.

## Can I use a different scale instead of 0.0 to 1.0, like percentage or letter grades?

0 scale is strongly recommended because it is the industry standard established by Intel and Google, and it makes cross-team and historical comparisons consistent. Percentages (0-100%) are mathematically equivalent and work fine. Letter grades (A/B/C/D) lose the granularity that makes the system useful for calibration. A five-point scale (1-5) also works but changes the interpretation benchmarks.

Whatever scale you choose, commit to it across the entire organization and do not mix scales between teams. 0, another use percentages, and a third use letter grades, because then cross-team analysis becomes impossible.

## What should I do when a key result scored 1.0 but the objective clearly was not achieved?

This is a strong signal that the key results were not properly aligned with the objective. Key results are supposed to be the measurable evidence that the objective was achieved. 0 but the objective feels unmet, the key results were measuring the wrong things (activity metrics rather than outcome metrics) or were too narrow to capture the full scope of the objective. 0 on both while the team is nowhere near achieving the objective.

Document this mismatch explicitly in the scorecard and use it as a case study during the next [key result writing session](https://tryhamster.com/skills/defining-measurable-key-results). Better key results might have measured developer community engagement, repeat usage, or inbound requests.
