# Examples: The OKR Scoring System

## The same score, two meanings

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: an infrastructure team ends the quarter with two objectives that both score 0.6. One is committed: keep a set of internal services within their agreed reliability targets. The other is aspirational: cut the cost of running the build system sharply.

**Walkthrough:**

The team reads the two scores against different bars. The aspirational objective at 0.6 sits in the yellow band Google uses for stretch goals: real progress, short of the full target. The team records what worked and carries the objective forward.

The committed objective at 0.6 is a miss. The team holds a short postmortem and finds that two outages came from a dependency it had not load-tested. It adds that testing to next quarter's plan. Neither result is averaged with the other in the team's summary, so the missed commitment stays visible.

## Agreeing the scoring rule in advance

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a key result aims to raise weekly active accounts from a baseline of 100 to a target of 200. At the end of the cycle the result is 150.

**Walkthrough:**

Scored as the share of the target reached, the result is 150 out of 200, a 0.75. Scored as the share of the gap closed, it is 50 out of 100, a 0.5. The team had agreed at planning to use the gap-closed rule for metrics with a baseline, because it reflects the change the team actually made.

The grade is recorded as 0.5, with the raw numbers beside it. Because the rule was fixed in advance, nobody argues for the more flattering number, and the grade can be compared with other cycles.

## Spotting sandbagging across cycles

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a marketing team has scored 1.0 on every aspirational key result for three quarters running. Its leader presents this as a strong track record.

**Walkthrough:**

The OKR lead looks at the pattern rather than any single grade. Consistently full scores on aspirational OKRs are what What Matters describes as a sign of sandbagging. In the reflection, the team admits it set targets it knew it could reach, partly because it worried the grades would come up in performance reviews.

Leadership restates that grades are not used for reviews. For the next cycle, the team sets one aspirational key result where confidence is closer to even odds. It scores 0.6, which the team now reads as a healthy stretch.
