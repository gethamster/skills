# FAQ: Iterating Design Solutions with Users

## How many iteration cycles does a design need?

There is no fixed number; you stop when the design meets the user need you defined at the start. IDEO describes refining until the design [addresses a genuine human need](https://ideo.com/methodologies), which is a condition, not a count. In practice, watch for new cycles producing only minor findings from varied participants. If you are still finding blocking problems, you are not done.

## How many users should I test with in each cycle?

Use enough to see patterns rather than individual quirks, for example five to eight participants per round, and vary who they are between rounds. Human-centered design [tends to study small samples in depth, which makes it prone to sampling bias](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9582917). Rotating in new participants and deliberately recruiting underrepresented groups offsets that. Treat the number as a starting point you adjust to how consistent the findings are.

## When should I pivot instead of refining?

Pivot when the same fundamental problem survives several revisions, because that signals the concept is wrong rather than the details. Refining is right when users grasp and want the core idea but stumble on specifics. Set a pivot trigger before testing begins so the decision is not made under sunk-cost pressure. A cheap, low-fidelity approach makes pivoting far less painful.

## How do I keep users engaged across many cycles?

Show returning participants what changed because of their input, keep sessions short, and schedule them well in advance. Sustained stakeholder engagement was identified as an important practice in a [2025 public-health review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12352946). Clearing consent and approval requirements early also helps, since those hurdles can otherwise delay involvement until late in development.

## What is the difference between iterating and just testing at the end?

End-stage testing can only confirm or reject a finished design, and by then changes are costly. Iteration uses repeated tests to shape the design as it forms, so each round of feedback changes what gets built next. The ISO standard treats this as a requirement, calling for an [iterative process driven and refined by user-centred evaluation](https://standards.iteh.ai/catalog/standards/iso/f476aadb-0ef4-4d4d-8049-139bd94b5a05/iso-9241-210-2010). Late-only testing is the pattern that leads to superficial involvement.
