# Examples: Iterating Design Solutions with Users

## Refining a benefits application form

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: A city digital team is redesigning an online application for a household assistance benefit. Their user need is that applicants can finish the form in one sitting without calling for help.

**Walkthrough:**

The team sets a two-week cycle and books five applicants per round at a community centre, mixing returning and new people. Cycle one uses paper screens and shows applicants stalling on a question about household income, so the team rewrites it in plain language and splits it into two questions. Cycle two, on a clickable mockup, shows the income fix working but reveals that people abandon the form when asked to upload documents they do not have to hand. The team adds a save-and-return option and logs both changes against the observations.

Cycle three meets the one-sitting criterion for most participants except those on older phones, so the team runs a fourth cycle focused on that group before stopping.

## Pivoting a clinic check-in concept

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: A clinic wants to cut waiting-room confusion with a self-service check-in kiosk. The stated need is that patients know where to go and how long they will wait.

**Walkthrough:**

The first cycle tests a cardboard kiosk with printed screens, and patients struggle to find it and hesitate to use it. The team refines the placement and wording in cycle two, but the same reluctance appears, especially among older patients who prefer to speak to someone. After a third cycle shows no improvement, the team applies the pivot trigger it set at the start. The new concept is a text message sent on arrival with directions and an estimated wait, prototyped by a staff member sending messages manually.

Cycle four shows patients understand it immediately, and the team iterates on message wording from there.

## Keeping stakeholders honest with observed sessions

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: A product team building an internal scheduling tool is split over whether shift managers need a calendar view or a list view. Both camps are confident.

**Walkthrough:**

Instead of debating, the facilitator builds two rough versions and invites both camps to watch the next round of sessions. Managers using the calendar view repeatedly miss unfilled shifts, while list-view users spot gaps quickly but ask to see the week at a glance. The team logs both findings and builds a list view with a compact weekly strip for the next cycle. Returning managers are shown the change and confirm it addresses what they struggled with.

The disagreement ends because everyone saw the same behaviour, and the decision is recorded in the revision log for the implementation team.
