# Analyzing Active Evaluation Behavior

> Analyze active evaluation behavior: how buyers research, which brands they add or drop, and which touchpoints cause each change.

## Overview

Active evaluation is the phase between the moment a buyer starts researching and the moment they decide. In the [McKinsey Consumer Decision Journey](https://tryhamster.com/methods/mckinsey-consumer-decision-journey), it is the phase that most clearly breaks the funnel model: buyers do not only narrow their options here, they also add new ones. [The original research](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/growth-marketing-and-sales/our-insights/the-consumer-decision-journey) found that car shoppers added an average of 2.2 brands to an initial set of 3.8 while evaluating, and that brands entering this way can force rivals out.

Analyzing active evaluation behavior means reconstructing what buyers did during research and linking each change in their shortlist to the touchpoint that caused it. The core output is a list of addition triggers and elimination triggers for your brand and your main competitors, ranked by how often they occur and how much they move the decision.

This phase is where challenger brands have their best chance and where incumbents can lose a lead they built in the consideration phase. It is also the phase where brands have the least direct control, because most of the influential touchpoints belong to someone else: reviewers, peers, comparison sites and the buyer's own memory of past experience.

The skill combines two kinds of evidence. Interviews with recent buyers give you the sequence of events and the reasons behind each change. Behavioral data, such as search terms, comparison page traffic and review site referrals, tells you how common each pattern is. Neither is enough alone, and the analysis is only as good as the join between them.

## How It Works

Buyers in active evaluation pull information more than they receive it. [McKinsey's authors](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/growth-marketing-and-sales/our-insights/the-consumer-decision-journey) found that two-thirds of the touchpoints in this phase were consumer-driven, such as internet reviews, word of mouth from friends and family, in-store interactions and recollections of past experience. Only a third were company-driven marketing. A brand that measures only its own channels therefore sees a minority of what shapes the decision.

The path through this phase is rarely a straight line. Google's research on [the messy middle](https://www.thinkwithgoogle.com/consumer-insights/consumer-journey/navigating-purchase-behavior-and-decision-making/) describes two mental modes, exploration and evaluation, that buyers loop between as many times as they need. Exploration widens the set by surfacing new options. Evaluation narrows it by comparing them. A single buyer can go through several rounds, and a brand can be added in one round and dropped in the next.

The same research identifies six cognitive biases that shape choices in this loop: category heuristics, the power of now, social proof, scarcity, authority and the power of free. These give you a vocabulary for why a touchpoint worked. A review page that tips a buyer is usually working through social proof. A comparison that lays out key specifications in a few words is working through category heuristics. Naming the mechanism helps you design a response instead of copying a competitor's tactic.

The analysis has four parts. First, you inventory the touchpoints buyers use during research, including the ones you do not own. Second, you reconstruct actual evaluation paths from interviews and data, recording which brands were in the set at each point. Third, you code every change in the set, whether an addition or an elimination, with the touchpoint that caused it and the reason given. Fourth, you rank the triggers and compare your brand's pattern with competitors'.

The ranking is what turns the research into decisions. A trigger that appears in many paths and reliably adds a brand is an asset to invest in. A trigger that frequently removes your brand is a problem to fix, and its reason tells you whether the fix is product, price, proof or presence. The comparison with competitors shows which triggers they are winning that you are not.

Keep in mind that customers now reach brands through many channels and that their experiences are increasingly social, as [Lemon and Verhoef](https://research.rug.nl/en/publications/understanding-customer-experience-throughout-the-customer-journey/) note in their review of customer journey research. That makes the inventory the step most likely to be incomplete, so build it from what buyers report, not from your channel list.

## Step-by-Step Guide

### Step 1: Inventory the Evaluation Touchpoints

List every place buyers go for information once they start researching: search results, review sites, comparison pages, forums, communities, peers, analysts, sales conversations, trials and your own site. Build the list from buyer interviews and referral data, not from your marketing plan. Mark each touchpoint as consumer-driven or company-driven. Note which ones you can observe in data and which you can only learn about by asking.

### Step 2: Reconstruct Evaluation Paths

Interview recent buyers, including people who chose a competitor, and walk them through their research from the first search to the decision. At each point, record which brands they were considering and what they had just seen or heard. Ask them to show you the pages, threads or messages they remember where possible, since recall of the sequence improves with prompts. Supplement the interviews with session and search data that shows the same paths in aggregate.

### Step 3: Code Additions and Eliminations

Go through each path and mark every moment a brand entered or left the set. For each change, record the touchpoint, the brand affected and the reason in the buyer's words. Tag the reason with a mechanism, such as social proof, authority or price, so patterns become visible across interviews. Keep additions and eliminations separate, because the touchpoints that add brands are often different from the ones that remove them.

### Step 4: Rank the Triggers

Count how often each trigger appears and how often it changed the outcome, not just the shortlist. Rank addition triggers and elimination triggers separately for your brand. Flag triggers that are frequent and decisive as priorities. A trigger that appears rarely but always removes your brand deserves attention too, since it may point to a serious gap in proof or product.

### Step 5: Compare with Competitors

Repeat the ranking for your two or three closest competitors using the same paths. Look for triggers where they are added and you are not, and for touchpoints where they are present and you are absent. Check whether competitors are winning through a mechanism you have ignored, such as authority from analyst coverage or social proof from reviews. This comparison usually produces the clearest list of actions.

### Step 6: Design Interventions

For each priority trigger, decide what would change the outcome. Addition triggers you win call for more presence at that touchpoint. Elimination triggers call for fixing the underlying reason: missing information, a weak comparison, a pricing surprise or a product gap. [Google's research](https://www.thinkwithgoogle.com/consumer-insights/consumer-journey/navigating-purchase-behavior-and-decision-making/) suggests giving buyers the information and reassurance they need to decide, rather than trying to force them out of the loop. Assign each intervention an owner and a metric.

### Step 7: Track Changes Over Time

Set up a small number of recurring measures: share of buyers who add your brand during research, share who drop it, and the most common reasons for each. Refresh the interview set on a fixed cadence and after major competitor moves. Watch for new touchpoints entering buyers' paths, since the inventory goes stale faster than any other part of the analysis.

## Best Practices

- Interview buyers who chose a competitor as well as your own customers. Your customers' paths show what worked, while lost buyers show where your brand was dropped and why, which is usually the more useful half.
- Record the shortlist at each point in the path, not only at the start and end. The value of this analysis lies in the changes during research, and those disappear if you only compare the first and last set.
- Tag every trigger with a mechanism. A shared vocabulary for why touchpoints work lets you compare across interviews and design responses that fit the mechanism instead of copying a competitor's tactic.
- Treat third-party touchpoints as part of your job. You cannot control a review site or a forum thread, but you can make sure accurate information about you is there, respond to criticism and give advocates something to point to.
- Keep a fixed interview guide across rounds. Comparable questions let you see whether a trigger is growing or fading, which matters more than any single round's ranking.

## Common Mistakes

- **Analyzing only owned channels**: Your analytics show your site and your ads, which are a minority of evaluation touchpoints. Build the inventory from buyers' own accounts and add third-party touchpoints even when you can only measure them through interviews.
- **Treating evaluation as a narrowing step**: If the analysis assumes brands only drop out, it misses the additions that let challengers in. Record additions explicitly and give them equal weight in the ranking.
- **Counting clicks instead of decisions**: A touchpoint with high traffic can have little effect on the outcome. Rank triggers by how often they changed the shortlist or the final choice, and use traffic only to size the opportunity.
- **Stopping at the diagnosis**: A ranked list of triggers with no owner or metric attached does not change the next buyer's path. Finish the analysis by writing interventions with owners, then measure whether additions rise or eliminations fall.
- **Letting the inventory go stale**: New review sites, communities and AI assistants enter buyers' research over time. Refresh the inventory each round instead of reusing last year's list.
