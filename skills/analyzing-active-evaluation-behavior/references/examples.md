# Examples: Analyzing Active Evaluation Behavior

## A CRM vendor that gets dropped after comparison pages

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a CRM vendor is often in prospects' initial set but wins less often than its rivals. Sales believes pricing is the problem. Marketing believes it is awareness. Nobody has looked at what prospects do during research.

**Walkthrough:**

The team interviews 20 recent buyers, half of whom chose a competitor, and records their shortlist at each step of their research. It codes every addition and elimination with the touchpoint and the reason.

Illustrative scenario: the coded eliminations of the vendor's brand look like this.

| Touchpoint | Times the brand was dropped | Most common reason given |
|------------|-----------------------------|--------------------------|
| Third-party comparison pages | 7 | Missing integration listed |
| Pricing page | 3 | Could not tell which plan fit |
| Peer recommendation | 2 | Peer had not used it |

Comparison pages account for most eliminations, and the reason is an integration the product actually has but the comparison pages list as missing. The team contacts the publishers with corrected information, adds a clear integrations page that comparison writers can reference, and rewrites the pricing page so each plan names the team it suits. It keeps the same interview guide for the next round to see whether comparison-page eliminations fall.

## A challenger added through community threads

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a small analytics tool is almost never in buyers' initial sets but still wins some deals. The founders want to know how those buyers found it.

**Walkthrough:**

Interviews with recent customers show a consistent pattern: buyers started with larger tools, hit a specific limitation, asked about it in a practitioner community, and were pointed to the challenger by other users. The addition trigger is peer recommendation in response to a named problem, which works through social proof and authority from experienced users.

The team decides not to spend more on broad advertising. It documents the limitation its product solves in plain terms, makes sure its own staff answer community questions openly and identify themselves, and asks satisfied customers whether they are willing to share their setup publicly. It tracks how many new customers mention a community thread as the moment they added the product.

## A retailer comparing two categories

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a home goods retailer sells both furniture and small kitchen appliances. Conversion from research to purchase is much weaker for appliances, and the category manager wants to know why.

**Walkthrough:**

The team reconstructs evaluation paths for both categories. Furniture buyers use the retailer's own room planner and in-store visits, both company-driven touchpoints the retailer controls well. Appliance buyers rely on independent review videos and ratings, where the retailer's own-brand appliances have few reviews.

The diagnosis is that the appliance problem sits in consumer-driven touchpoints the retailer has ignored. The retailer starts a post-purchase review request for appliance buyers and sends review units to independent reviewers. It sets a target for the share of appliance evaluation paths in which its own brand is added, and remeasures after two review cycles.
