# How to Write OKR Objectives That Focus a Team

> How to write OKR objectives: turn a strategic priority into one short, qualitative, concrete statement of what a team will achieve this cycle.

## Overview

Knowing how to write OKR objectives is the first skill in the [OKR method](../../methods/objectives-and-key-results-okrs/METHOD.md), because everything else hangs off the objective. The objective says what the team wants to achieve. The key results that follow only say how the team will know. If the objective is vague, the key results end up measuring whatever is easy to count; if it describes a task, the key results end up tracking the task.

What Matters, the site John Doerr co-founded, describes a good objective as significant, concrete, action-oriented and ideally inspirational ([What Matters: What is an OKR?](https://www.whatmatters.com/faqs/okr-meaning-definition-example)). Google's playbook adds that an objective must be tangible, objective and unambiguous, so that it is obvious to a rational observer whether it was achieved, and that achieving it must provide clear value ([Google's OKR playbook](https://www.whatmatters.com/resources/google-okr-playbook)). Those two lists pull in slightly different directions. Inspiration favors memorable language, and unambiguity favors plain language. A well-written objective does both: it names a specific end state in words the team would actually use.

Objectives are qualitative on purpose. Numbers belong in the key results, where they can be graded. An objective that already contains a number, such as a revenue target, is usually a key result that has been promoted, and it leaves the team without a reason for the number. An objective that contains no end state, such as "continue improving onboarding," gives the key results nothing to prove.

The output of this skill is a short list of objectives for one team and one cycle, each written as a single line, each labelled committed or aspirational, and each with a sentence explaining why it matters now. That list goes into the planning session, where key results are drafted under it.

The skill is small but it is where most weak OKR sets go wrong. The tests below are cheap to run and catch the common failures before anyone spends time on metrics.

## How It Works

Start from priorities and leave wording for later. What Matters recommends identifying the few things that would make the biggest difference in the next cycle, asking what needs to change from where the team is today, and narrowing a long list toward three ([OKR Examples: How to Write Them](https://www.whatmatters.com/faqs/okr-examples-and-how-to-write-them)). Only then do you convert each priority into an objective.

Each objective should describe an end state or a direction of change. Google's re:Work guide gives the contrast directly: avoid expressions that do not push for new achievement, such as "keep hiring," "maintain market position" or "continue doing X," and prefer expressions that convey endpoints and states, such as "ship feature Y" ([re:Work](https://rework.withgoogle.com/intl/en/guides/set-goals-with-okrs)). A maintenance goal may still matter, but it is business as usual and is usually better tracked as a KPI.

Test the objective for value. Google's playbook calls low-value objectives the "Who cares?" OKR and gives a litmus test: could the OKR score 1.0 under reasonable circumstances without providing direct end-user or economic benefit? If so, reword it around the benefit ([Google's OKR playbook](https://www.whatmatters.com/resources/google-okr-playbook)). This is the test that turns "Migrate to the new billing system" into something about what customers or the business get from the migration.

Test it for tasks in disguise. What Matters suggests asking, of any objective that reads like a to-do item, "If we achieve this, what would get better?" The answer is usually the real objective ([OKRs are not business as usual](https://www.whatmatters.com/series_entries/s2-3-okrs-bau-business-as-usual/)).

Test it for crispness. The playbook's quick checks include: if the objective does not fit on one line, it probably is not crisp enough, and if you wrote it in five minutes, it probably is not good ([Google's OKR playbook](https://www.whatmatters.com/resources/google-okr-playbook)).

Finally, decide what kind of objective it is. A committed objective is one the team agrees it will achieve. An aspirational one describes how the team would like the world to look without a clear path yet. The playbook's advice for aspirational objectives is to start from what the customer's world could look like if most constraints were removed, rather than from what the team could do with extra staff and a bit of luck.

## Step-by-Step Guide

### Step 1: Collect the team's candidate priorities

List everything the team believes it should work on next cycle, including requests from other teams. Next to each, note which company objective or strategic theme it supports. Anything that supports none is either business as usual or a sign the strategy is missing something, and both are worth saying out loud. Keep the list visible to the whole team.

### Step 2: Narrow to the few that matter most

Ask of each item what would be better if it were done and why it matters this cycle, as [What Matters suggests](https://www.whatmatters.com/faqs/okr-examples-and-how-to-write-them). Group related items. Keep the few that clearly rise above the rest and park the others as business as usual. A short list forces the trade-off conversation that makes OKRs useful.

### Step 3: Draft one line per priority

Write each priority as a single sentence that names the end state. Use a verb and a concrete subject: who or what changes, and in what direction. Leave numbers out; they go in the key results. Write in the team's own language so the sentence is easy to remember.

### Step 4: Run the four tests

Check each draft against the tests from How It Works: does it describe an end state rather than maintenance, would achieving it provide clear value, is it a task in disguise, and does it fit on one line? Rewrite any draft that fails. When a draft keeps failing the value test, the priority itself may be low value and should drop off the list.

### Step 5: Label each objective committed or aspirational

Decide with the team whether each objective is a promise or a stretch, and write the label next to it. What Matters suggests marking them "C" and "A" ([Committed vs. Aspirational OKRs](https://www.whatmatters.com/faqs/committed-aspirational-okrs-examples-difference)). If the team is not sure an approach will work at all, consider framing the objective around what it needs to learn instead.

### Step 6: Add a one-sentence rationale

Under each objective, write why it matters now and which company objective it supports. This sentence helps other teams understand the goal, and it gives the key result drafters something to check their metrics against. If the rationale is hard to write, the objective is probably not ready.

## Best Practices

- Write the objective before the metrics. Starting from a number you already track tends to produce objectives that describe the dashboard rather than the change you want.
- Keep one objective per idea. An objective joined by "and" usually hides two priorities, and splitting them makes each easier to measure and to drop if needed.
- Use words an outsider would understand. [Google's OKR playbook](https://www.whatmatters.com/resources/google-okr-playbook) warns that key results expressed in team-internal terms are probably not good, and the same applies to objectives.
- Name the customer or business outcome. Projects are how you get there, and they belong in plans under the key results.
- Allow some objectives to persist. What Matters notes an objective can be long-lived and roll over for a year or longer while its key results evolve ([What Matters](https://www.whatmatters.com/faqs/okr-meaning-definition-example)).
- Review drafts with a neighboring team. People outside the team spot jargon and vague end states faster than the authors do.

## Common Mistakes

- **Putting the number in the objective**: "Reach a revenue target" is a key result without a reason. Ask what the number is for, write that as the objective, and move the number underneath it.
- **Writing a maintenance goal**: Objectives such as "maintain uptime" or "keep hiring" describe business as usual. Track them as KPIs, or rewrite them around a real change if something must improve.
- **Writing a task**: "Launch the new dashboard" can be done without helping anyone. Apply the "what would get better?" test and write the answer instead.
- **Writing a slogan**: "Delight customers" could belong to any company in any quarter. Make it concrete enough that an observer could tell whether it happened.
- **Leaving the type unstated**: An objective with no committed or aspirational label invites the team and its dependents to read failure differently. Decide the label while writing the objective.
