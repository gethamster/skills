# Examples: Optimizing Moment-of-Purchase Triggers

## An online store with a shipping surprise

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: an online homeware store sees many buyers leave on the final checkout screen. The team's first idea is a discount pop-up for anyone about to leave.

**Walkthrough:**

Before adding anything, the team watches recordings of abandoned checkouts and adds an optional exit question. Most departures on the final screen happen right after shipping costs appear for the first time. Exit answers mention the shipping charge far more than anything else.

Illustrative scenario: the team tests showing shipping on the product page against the current checkout.

| Version | Checkouts started | Purchases completed |
|---------|-------------------|---------------------|
| Shipping shown at the end | 1,000 | 400 |
| Shipping shown on the product page | 1,000 | 460 |

Completion rises from 40% to 46% in this test, and refunds in the following weeks do not change. The team keeps the change and drops the discount pop-up idea, since the cause was the surprise, not the price level. It logs the result so the next person who proposes a pop-up can see what was tried.

## A software company with too many plans

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a software company's pricing page shows five plans and a long feature table. Buyers who reach it often contact sales with the same question: which plan do I need?

**Walkthrough:**

The team reads chat logs from the pricing page and finds that most questions are about plan fit, not price. It rewrites the page so each plan opens with one line naming the team it suits, moves the full feature table below the plans, and adds a short recommendation question for undecided visitors.

Because traffic is modest, the team compares two equal periods before and after the change and tracks both self-serve purchases and "which plan" chat questions. Questions fall and self-serve purchases rise. The team also checks plan downgrades in the following month to make sure buyers did not pick the wrong plan.

## A B2B deal stuck in security review

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a data tool wins technical evaluations but loses a noticeable share of deals in the final weeks. Lost-deal notes mention delays, and some buyers chose a competitor that "was easier to get approved."

**Walkthrough:**

The team treats the security and procurement review as its moment of purchase. It collects the questions buyers' security teams asked across recent deals and finds the same few dozen questions repeated. It writes a standard security package with those answers and the relevant certifications, and gives sales a checklist for starting the review earlier in the deal.

The team tracks time spent in the review stage and the number of deals lost during it over the following quarters. It keeps the package current as buyers ask new questions, since an outdated answer would reintroduce the delay.
