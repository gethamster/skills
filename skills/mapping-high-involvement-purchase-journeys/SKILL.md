# Research-Intensive Purchase Mapping for Planned Journeys

> Research-intensive purchase mapping: build a customer decision journey map for a high-involvement purchase from buyer interviews and behavioral data.

## Overview

Research-intensive purchase mapping is the practice of building a journey map for purchases that buyers research and deliberate over a long period, such as a car, a mortgage, a pension or premium electronics. The map covers the whole high-involvement purchase journey: what first made the buyer think about the purchase, how they researched it, which brands they weighed and why they chose as they did. It is the central deliverable of the [Planned Journey Framework](../../methods/planned-journey-framework/METHOD.md), which organizes it into latent, evaluation and buying stages.

The planned journey framework was published by SKIM's Alex Xiaoguang Zhu, who describes it as fitting purchases made relatively infrequently with heavy research, and names automobiles, financial services and consumer electronics as applicable industries ([Quirk's](https://www.quirks.com/articles/4-frameworks-for-mapping-customer-decision-journeys)). Those are the categories where this skill pays off. A short, habitual purchase does not have enough deliberate structure for this kind of map.

A customer decision journey map for a planned purchase is a specific kind of journey map. Nielsen Norman Group defines a customer journey map as a visualization of the process a person goes through to accomplish a goal tied to a specific business or product, and distinguishes it from experience maps, service blueprints and empathy maps ([NN/g mapping methods](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-mapping-cheat-sheet/)). The planned journey version keeps the actor and the phases of a standard map and adds two layers: the consideration set at each stage and the triggers that move buyers between stages.

The map is built from evidence. That means interviews with recent buyers, including people who chose competitors and people who stopped, plus whatever behavioral data exists. The result should be recognizable to buyers and specific enough that each team can see where its touchpoints fall and what buyers need at that point.

## How It Works

The map has one actor and one scenario. NN/g recommends one point of view per map so the story stays clear, with the actor's goal and expectations stated up front ([NN/g Journey Mapping 101](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/journey-mapping-101/)). For a planned purchase, the actor is a buyer segment defined by its trigger or situation, such as first-time home buyers, and the scenario is the purchase from trigger to decision. Different segments with very different journeys get separate maps.

The columns are the three stages. The rows follow the standard journey map structure, actions, mindsets and emotions, and opportunities, plus rows specific to the planned journey: the triggers that open and close each stage, the brands in consideration, and the information sources used. NN/g stresses that the actions on the map should be rooted in data and that mindsets should ideally use customer quotes from research ([NN/g Journey Mapping 101](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/journey-mapping-101/)).

Interviews carry the map. Buyers walk through their purchase from the first moment they thought about it, anchored to events and dates, and describe what they did, who they asked, what they read and what they were worried about. Asking about specific events limits the drift that affects long retrospective accounts, known as [recall bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recall_bias). Interviewing people who did not buy from you is necessary, because they reveal exits the brand's own customers never experienced.

Behavioral data checks and dates what buyers say. SKIM's journey mapping combines stated and behavioral consumer data, using passive metering and digital tracking to fill gaps that surveys leave ([SKIM journey mapping](https://skimgroup.com/services/decision-customer-journey-mapping/)). Even simple sources help: web analytics shows which comparison pages buyers visited and when, and CRM data shows how long quotes stayed open.

When journeys vary a lot, the analysis groups them before mapping. SKIM's six-step approach uses clustering to find groups of like-minded consumers and then sizes the clusters to see which journeys matter most ([SKIM six steps](https://skimgroup.com/blog/6-steps-untangling-mapping-consumer-decision-journeys/)). The result is a small set of maps for the journeys that matter, rather than one averaged map that describes nobody.

## Step-by-Step Guide

### Step 1: Scope the map

Name the category, the buyer segment and the decision the map covers, and state what the map will be used for. Confirm the purchase is deliberate enough for a planned journey map by asking a few recent buyers how long they thought about it and what they did. If journeys differ sharply by trigger or buyer type, plan separate maps from the start.

### Step 2: Recruit a varied sample

Recruit recent buyers of your brand, recent buyers of competitors and people who started looking and stopped. Aim for variety in triggers and outcomes rather than a large number of similar buyers. Keep recruiting until new interviews stop adding new triggers, sources or exits.

### Step 3: Run event-anchored interviews

Ask each buyer to start from the first moment they thought about the purchase and move forward in time, using dates and events as anchors. For each stage, ask what they did, which brands they considered, which sources they used, who they talked to and what worried them. Ask about moments they nearly stopped. Record quotes for the mindset row.

### Step 4: Gather behavioral evidence

Pull what data you have for the same period: site analytics, search trends, CRM records, store or showroom visit logs. Use it to check the timing and order of what buyers described. Where behavior and interviews disagree, note it on the map rather than choosing one silently.

### Step 5: Group journeys and draft the map

Sort interviews into groups with similar triggers and paths, following the clustering idea in [SKIM's approach](https://skimgroup.com/blog/6-steps-untangling-mapping-consumer-decision-journeys/). For each group that matters, draft a map with the three stages as columns and rows for triggers, actions, consideration set, sources, questions, mood and pain points. Fill each cell from evidence and mark any cell based on assumption.

### Step 6: Mark the moments that matter

On each map, mark the points where buyers most often drop a brand, stall or change their mind. Add the reasons in buyers' words. These moments become the opportunities row and the input for touchpoint and messaging work.

### Step 7: Validate and publish

Show the draft to a few buyers and to front-line staff and ask what is missing or wrong. Correct it, then publish it with a note on the evidence behind it and the date. Set a date to refresh it when the category changes.

## Best Practices

- Map one actor per map. Mixing segments with different triggers produces an average journey that no buyer recognizes.
- Include non-buyers and competitors' buyers. They show the exits and the rivals' strengths that your own customers cannot.
- Anchor interviews to events and dates. Specific moments give more reliable accounts of long purchases than general questions about the process.
- Use buyers' own words. Quotes in the mindset and pain point rows keep the map honest and persuade stakeholders more than paraphrase.
- Mark assumptions. A cell filled from team opinion should look different from one filled from evidence, so readers know what to trust.
- Keep the map in use. Share it with the teams that own each stage and refresh it on a schedule, so it guides decisions after the launch meeting.

## Common Mistakes

- **Mapping from internal knowledge**: A map drawn in a workshop without buyer research reflects the company's view of the journey. Build from interviews and data.
- **Starting the map at first contact with your brand**: The latent stage and the trigger come before any contact, and they often decide the first consideration set.
- **Interviewing only your customers**: Customer-only samples miss why buyers chose competitors or gave up. Recruit both.
- **Producing one averaged map**: When journeys differ sharply, an average hides the differences that matter. Group first, then map.
- **Treating the map as finished**: Categories change. A map that is not refreshed slowly turns into a record of past buyers.
