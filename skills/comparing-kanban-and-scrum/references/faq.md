# FAQ: Comparing Kanban and Scrum for Your Team

## How do I compare kanban vs scrum if my team has never used either method?

Focus on the six dimensions rather than method-specific experience. Score your work predictability, requirement stability, and the other four dimensions using your current work history, even if that history is informal. A team with no method experience and a score of 8 should start with Kanban because its lower ceremony overhead makes adoption easier for teams new to structured methods. A team scoring 16 should start with Scrum because the sprint structure provides more guidance for teams that need scaffolding.

## How long should the kanban vs scrum comparison process take?

The data gathering (work history export and stakeholder interviews) takes 2-3 hours spread across a day. The scoring workshop with the team takes 45-60 minutes. Designing the specific configuration takes another 30-60 minutes. Total elapsed time is typically one day if you schedule the stakeholder interviews in advance.

Do not stretch this into a multi-week analysis. The worksheet is intentionally simple because the decision should be made quickly and then validated through the 6-8 week trial period.

## Should I compare kanban vs scrum before or after setting up a board?

Before. Your board structure, WIP limits, and column design all depend on which method (or hybrid) you choose. A Scrum board has a sprint backlog column and uses sprint boundaries. A Kanban board has explicit WIP limits per column and no sprint boundaries.

A hybrid board combines elements of both. If you design a board first, you will likely need to redesign it after the comparison. Do the comparison, write the configuration document, then design your board using the guidance in [designing Kanban boards](https://tryhamster.com/skills/designing-kanban-boards).

## Why does my kanban vs scrum recommendation keep changing every time we revisit it?

This usually means your team's work mix is genuinely shifting, which is common in fast-growing companies. If your scores swing by more than 3 points between assessments, look at which specific dimensions changed and why. A team that moved from product-building to production support will see work predictability and requirement stability scores drop, naturally shifting the recommendation toward Kanban. This is the method following the work, which is correct behavior.

If scores fluctuate without an obvious cause, your evidence quality may be weak. Tighten the data sources you use for scoring.

## Can I use Kanban flow metrics even if I choose Scrum?

Yes, and you should. Cycle time, throughput, and cumulative flow diagrams provide information that sprint velocity does not. Velocity tells you how much you completed in a sprint. Cycle time tells you how long individual items took.

A Scrum team with consistent velocity but wildly variable cycle times has a hidden bottleneck. Adding Kanban flow metrics to a Scrum implementation is one of the most common hybrid configurations and is a natural stepping stone if you later decide to migrate more fully toward Kanban. See [measuring flow metrics](https://tryhamster.com/skills/measuring-kanban-flow-metrics) for implementation details.

## How do I handle a team where half the members want Kanban and half want Scrum?

Use the independent scoring approach from Step 9: have each person fill out the worksheet alone, then compare results. Disagreements on specific dimensions reveal valuable information. If one person scores work predictability at 1 and another at 3, they are experiencing different aspects of the work. The discussion about why they scored differently is more productive than debating method merits in the abstract.

Often the resolution is a hybrid that addresses both camps' concerns: the Scrum advocates get the planning rhythm they want, and the Kanban advocates get the flow flexibility they need.

## What if my organization mandates Scrum but my scores clearly favor Kanban?

Start with Scrum's ceremonies (sprint planning, review, retrospective) to meet organizational expectations, then introduce Kanban practices within that structure. Add WIP limits to your sprint board, track cycle time alongside velocity, and use cumulative flow diagrams in your retrospectives. Over time, present the flow data to leadership as evidence that certain Scrum constraints (like fixed sprint commitments) are hurting rather than helping. This gradual approach is more effective than asking for an exemption because you are showing results, not arguing theory.

Many teams have successfully migrated from Scrum to Kanban this way over 3-6 months.
