# Examples: Kanban or Scrum

## An IT operations team with constant interrupts

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: an IT operations team was told to adopt Scrum. Their Sprints keep failing because incidents and access requests arrive every day and take priority over planned work.

**Walkthrough:**

The team pulls three months of tickets and finds that most of their work was unplanned and needed a response within days. Stakeholders judge the team by response time on individual requests. The comparison points clearly toward Kanban.

They set up a board with an expedite lane for incidents, WIP limits on each stage and a weekly replenishment meeting for planned improvements. They keep a monthly retrospective from their Scrum period because the team valued it. After a trial, the share of urgent requests closed within their service level expectation has risen, and planned improvements finally move.

## A product team that needs rhythm

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a new product team works from a loose kanban board. Work flows, but stakeholders rarely see progress, and the team struggles to say what it is aiming for over the next month.

**Walkthrough:**

The history shows mostly planned feature work with few interruptions, and stakeholders ask repeatedly for regular demos and a clear goal. The team has a product manager who can act as Product Owner.

They move to Scrum with two-week Sprints, a Sprint Goal and a Sprint Review each cycle. They keep their WIP limit on In Progress inside the Sprint and continue to track cycle time. Stakeholder complaints about visibility stop, and the team uses cycle time to plan how much to select at Sprint Planning.

## A team split between two kinds of work

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a platform team builds new internal tools and also supports the teams that use them. Half the team wants Scrum for the tool work, and the other half wants Kanban for support.

**Walkthrough:**

Instead of forcing one answer, they design a scrumban setup. Tool work is planned in two-week cycles with a goal and a review. Support requests flow through a separate lane with its own WIP limit and are pulled continuously, and a rotating person handles the support lane each cycle.

They write the rules down: which events they keep, which limits apply to which lane, and how urgent support work may interrupt planned work. At the end of the trial they review both cycle time for support and progress toward the tool goals, and keep the setup with small changes.
