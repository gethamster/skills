## Overview

The planned journey framework for B2B applies the latent, evaluation and buying stages to purchases made by organizations, where the decision belongs to a group rather than one person. B2B purchases of software, equipment or professional services share the traits of a planned journey: they are infrequent, heavily researched and deliberated over a long time. What changes is that several people take part, each with their own stage, concerns and sources.

It helps to be clear about what SKIM, which published the framework, says about B2B. In the [Quirk's article](https://www.quirks.com/articles/4-frameworks-for-mapping-customer-decision-journeys) that sets out the planned journey, Alex Xiaoguang Zhu offers a separate ecosystem journey for B2B decisions involving multiple stakeholders, such as internal clients, distribution partners and end users. The article notes that such purchases are often automated through e-procurement and reconsidered only after a disruption, such as a safety incident or a budget cut. For the ecosystem journey, Zhu writes that "the key is to incorporate every stakeholder's involvement via the dominant decision-maker journey." This skill borrows that stakeholder view and combines it with the planned journey stages: each role gets its own lane through latent, evaluation and buying, and the map shows how the lanes line up. For background on the stages, see the [Planned Journey Framework](../../methods/planned-journey-framework/METHOD.md).

The group that makes an organizational purchase has a long-established name. Organizational buying research calls it the buying center or decision-making unit: all the members of an organization who become involved in the buying process for a particular product or service, a concept credited to Robinson, Farris and Wind in 1967 ([Buying center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buying_center)). Members take different roles, and the roles matter more for the journey than job titles do.

B2B buying process stages differ from consumer ones in one more way: the latent stage is long for most accounts. A study by John Dawes of the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute reports that companies change providers of services such as banking, legal advice, software or telecoms around every five years, which the Institute's summary says means "only 20% are in the market for those services in a given year and just 5% in a given quarter" ([Ehrenberg-Bass](https://marketingscience.info/news-and-insights/ehrenberg-bass-95-of-b2b-buyers-are-not-in-the-market-for-your-products)). Most of a B2B market is latent at any time, which makes multi-stakeholder journey mapping as much about memory and triggers as about the active sales cycle.

## How It Works

The map is built in lanes, one per role in the buying committee. Typical roles include the person who first raises the need, the people who will use the product, technical or security reviewers, the budget holder and procurement. The same person can hold several roles, and one role can be shared by several people. The role list comes from real deals, because committees vary by company size, industry and purchase type.

Each lane moves through the three stages at its own pace. The user who feels the problem may leave latent months before the budget holder does. Technical reviewers may enter only in late evaluation and bring new criteria with them. Procurement often appears only at buying. B2B buying committee journey mapping therefore places each role's entry into, and exit from, each stage on a shared timeline, and looks for the gaps.

Consensus points sit between the lanes. At certain moments the group must agree before the purchase can move forward: to fund an evaluation, to adopt a shortlist, to select a supplier. These points are where many deals stall. The map marks each one, names the roles that must agree and records what they need to see.

Triggers matter at two levels. An individual trigger moves one role out of latent, for example a user frustrated by a failing tool. An organizational trigger moves the whole committee, for example a budget cycle, a contract renewal, a merger or an incident. Zhu's article describes that kind of disruption as what reopens an otherwise automated purchase ([Quirk's](https://www.quirks.com/articles/4-frameworks-for-mapping-customer-decision-journeys)).

Evidence comes from buyers and from your own deal history. Interviews with members of won and lost buying committees give each role's view. Win-loss programs, which gather buyer feedback on the product, the sales experience, the vendor and price ([Win-loss analytics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Win%E2%80%93loss_analytics)), show which roles and concerns decided the outcome. Sales staff add which roles appeared late and changed the course of a deal.

## Step-by-Step Guide

### Step 1: Identify the buying committee roles

Review a set of recent won and lost deals and list everyone who took part, with the role each played. Interview sales staff about roles that influenced deals without appearing in the CRM. Merge the results into a role list that describes function, such as user, budget holder or technical reviewer. Note which roles appear in almost every deal and which appear only in some.

### Step 2: Place each role in the stages

For each role, describe what latent, evaluation and buying look like from that role's position: when they typically become aware of the need, when they start evaluating and when they take part in the final choice. Interview people who held those roles in real purchases, including purchases that went to competitors. Record each role's questions and sources at each stage.

### Step 3: Find the triggers

List the individual triggers that move each role out of latent and the organizational triggers that move the whole committee, such as budget cycles, renewals and incidents. Mark which triggers can be anticipated from outside, such as a contract end date. These become signals for when to engage an account.

### Step 4: Draw the multi-lane map

Lay out one lane per role on a shared timeline with the three stages marked in each lane. Show when each role enters and leaves each stage in a typical deal. Highlight gaps, for example when users are deep in evaluation while the budget holder is still latent. Keep the map to the roles that matter in most deals.

### Step 5: Mark the consensus points

Mark the points where the committee must agree to move forward. For each, record which roles must agree, what each needs to see and what usually blocks agreement. Use lost deals to find the consensus points where deals stall most often.

### Step 6: Match content and contact to each lane

For each role and stage, note what the role needs from you: evidence for users, security documentation for technical reviewers, a business case for budget holders, terms for procurement. Check what you currently provide against that list. Gaps at consensus points are the highest priority.

### Step 7: Validate with sales and recent buyers

Walk through the map with sales staff and a few recent buyers and ask where it is wrong. Adjust roles, timing and consensus points. Revisit the map when deal reviews show a new role or a new stall point.

## Best Practices

- Define roles by function. Organizational buying research describes the buying center through the roles its members take ([Buying center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buying_center)), and functions such as user, budget holder and technical reviewer are easier to compare across deals than job titles.
- Include lost deals. The roles and consensus points that block purchases show up most clearly in deals you did not win.
- Treat the latent stage as the normal state of most accounts. With most of a B2B market out of the market at any time ([Ehrenberg-Bass](https://marketingscience.info/news-and-insights/ehrenberg-bass-95-of-b2b-buyers-are-not-in-the-market-for-your-products)), memory built before the trigger decides which suppliers the committee thinks of first.
- Watch organizational triggers. Budget cycles, renewals and incidents move the whole committee, and some can be anticipated.
- Plan for late entrants. Technical, legal and procurement roles often join late with new criteria, so prepare their material before they appear.
- Keep the map small. A map with every possible role is unreadable. Show the roles that matter in most deals and note the rest.

## Common Mistakes

- **Mapping one buyer**: A single-persona map misses the roles that block or approve the purchase. Map the committee.
- **Assuming the committee moves together**: Roles enter and leave stages at different times. Treating the committee as one buyer hides the gaps where deals stall.
- **Relying only on CRM contacts**: Influential roles often never appear in the CRM. Interview sales staff and buyers to find them.
- **Ignoring the long latent stage**: Focusing only on accounts in an active cycle leaves the brand unknown when the next trigger arrives.
- **Using consumer stage definitions unchanged**: B2B stages need consensus points and organizational triggers. Adapt the definitions before mapping.
