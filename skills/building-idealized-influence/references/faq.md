# FAQ: Building Idealized Influence as a Role Model Leader

## What are the characteristics of transformational leadership related to idealized influence?

Idealized influence shows up as followers seeing the leader as [ethical, credible, consistent, dependable, respected and trustworthy](https://scu.edu/business/blog/leadership-ethics/what-is-transformational-leadership). Behind that perception sit two behaviors: modeling the standards you expect and putting the group's needs ahead of your own. The expected result is that people identify with the leader and want to emulate their conduct.

## What is the difference between attributed and behavioral idealized influence?

Attributed idealized influence is about whether followers [perceive the leader as ethical, confident, trustworthy, idealistic and charismatic](https://qic-wd.org/umbrella-summary/transformational-leadership). Behavioral idealized influence is about whether the leader's actions express shared values, beliefs and mission. You control the behavioral side directly, and the attributed side is the reputation it earns over time.

## Can I build idealized influence without a management title?

Yes, and the skill arguably matters most in that situation. It rests on [acting as the example of expected standards](https://scu.edu/business/blog/leadership-ethics/what-is-transformational-leadership) rather than relying on formal authority. A senior individual contributor who consistently models a practice and shares credit can shape team norms as much as a manager can.

## Is idealized influence the same as being charismatic?

Not quite. Early versions of the model [associated idealized influence with charisma](https://aeasseincludes.assp.org/proceedings/2012/docs/751.pdf), and charisma still appears among the attributed perceptions. But relying on personal magnetism risks followers depending on you, so it is safer to focus on consistent, values-driven behavior that others can copy.

## How do I know if my influence has turned manipulative?

Look at who benefits. Reviewers describe [pseudo-transformational leadership](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/23792981241267758) as influence used for the leader's self-interest at others' expense. If your decisions on credit, workload and visibility tend to favor you, or people agree with you publicly but disagree privately, treat that as a warning and seek candid feedback.
