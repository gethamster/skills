# Examples: Building Idealized Influence as a Role Model Leader

## New manager inheriting a review standard

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a newly promoted engineering manager leads a team of 6 that has a rule requiring 2 reviewers on every change, but the previous lead routinely skipped it for their own work.

**Walkthrough:**

The manager lists the review rule as one of three standards and audits the last month, finding the team skipped review on roughly 10 changes, mostly senior ones. Before saying anything to the team, the manager submits every one of their own changes for review in the open. In the next team meeting they state the rule applies to everyone including them and explain it protects the shared goal of fewer customer-facing bugs. Over the following weeks, senior engineers start requesting reviews without prompting.

The manager checks whether the habit holds by taking a week off and seeing whether review counts drop.

## Tech lead handling credit and blame

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a tech lead without direct reports runs a 4-person project that ships a strong feature and, a week later, has an outage caused by a rushed decision the lead approved.

**Walkthrough:**

For the launch presentation to leadership, the lead has the 2 engineers who built the core feature present it and introduces them by name. When the outage review happens, the lead opens by stating they approved the rushed change and owns that decision. They explain briefly that the team values honest reporting over looking good, so the review focuses on process, not individuals. Afterward, a junior engineer volunteers a near-miss they had been hesitant to mention.

That early bad news is the signal that followers now trust the lead enough to be candid.

## Operations lead checking perceptions

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: an operations lead with a team of 10 believes they model punctuality and follow-through, and runs a short anonymous survey of 3 behavioral questions.

**Walkthrough:**

The survey asks when the lead did not follow their own rules, when they put the team first, and whether commitments made in one-on-ones were kept. Several answers mention promised process changes that never happened. The lead compares this to their self-audit, which had missed those commitments entirely. They list each open promise, close out what they can within a month, and tell the team which ones they are dropping and why.

A repeat survey a quarter later shows the follow-through complaints gone, while one new comment about meeting overruns gives them the next gap to close.
