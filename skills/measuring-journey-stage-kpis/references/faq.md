# FAQ: Measuring KPIs and Metrics for Each Journey Stage

## How many customer journey metrics should I track in total across all five stages?

Aim for 10-20 total metrics across all five stages, with 2-4 per stage. Fewer than 10 usually means you are missing a stage entirely. More than 20 means you are tracking redundant indicators that will dilute focus during reviews. The acid test is whether your team can review all metrics in a single 30-60 minute session.

If the review takes longer, you have too many metrics or insufficient summarization.

## How do I measure customer journey metrics for stages that happen outside my owned platforms?

Some awareness and advocacy activity happens on third-party platforms where you have limited visibility. For awareness on channels like podcasts or word-of-mouth, use proxy metrics such as branded search volume (measured in Google Search Console), direct traffic spikes correlated with external activities, and post-purchase surveys asking "how did you hear about us?" For advocacy on review sites or social media, use social listening tools or periodic manual audits. Accept that these metrics will be less precise than your owned-platform metrics and set wider confidence bands around their baselines.

## Should I measure customer journey metrics before or after building a customer journey map?

After. The journey map defines the stages, touchpoints, and transitions that your metrics will instrument. Without a map, you risk assigning metrics to stages that are poorly defined or missing critical touchpoints. Start with [mapping customer touchpoints](https://tryhamster.com/skills/mapping-customer-touchpoints-across-stages), then build or refine your [journey map](https://tryhamster.com/skills/building-customer-journey-maps), and then layer on metrics.

That said, if you already have a rough understanding of your stages and need to move quickly, you can define preliminary metrics and refine them as your map matures.

## How often should I review customer journey metrics?

Match the review cadence to the metric's signal speed. High-volume digital metrics like website conversion rate or daily active users should be reviewed weekly. Lower-volume relationship metrics like NPS or referral rate should be reviewed monthly or quarterly because small sample sizes create noisy week-to-week readings. A good default rhythm is a weekly operational review of fast metrics and a monthly or quarterly strategic review of all journey-stage metrics together.

The strategic review is where you look at the full journey and identify which stage needs the most attention.

## How do I handle metrics that span multiple journey stages?

Some metrics naturally touch multiple stages. Customer Lifetime Value (CLV), for example, is influenced by acquisition cost (Purchase), retention duration (Retention), and referral value (Advocacy). Assign the metric to the stage where it is most actionable. CLV is usually best placed in the Retention stage because that is where the largest lever for improving it exists (extending customer duration and increasing purchase frequency).

Then note in your framework that the metric has upstream dependencies so that teams in earlier stages understand their contribution.

## Why does my metrics framework keep getting ignored after the first month?

This almost always happens because the framework is disconnected from decisions. If the metrics are reviewed but never trigger a change in priorities, budget, or effort, people stop paying attention. Fix this by tying at least one team-level OKR or quarterly goal to a journey-stage metric, so there is a concrete consequence of the metric moving. Also ensure that the review meeting has a standing agenda item: "Based on this data, what are we changing or investigating this week?" If the answer is consistently "nothing," the metrics are either not diagnostic enough or the team does not have the authority to act on them.

## How do I adapt customer journey metrics for a marketplace or two-sided business model?

In a marketplace, you need a separate metrics framework for each side: supply (sellers, providers, creators) and demand (buyers, consumers). Each side has its own five-stage journey with distinct KPIs. For the demand side, awareness might be search traffic, consideration might be search-to-browse conversion, and purchase might be first transaction rate. For the supply side, awareness might be seller acquisition campaigns, consideration might be listing creation rate, and retention might be seller monthly active rate.

Track both frameworks in parallel and watch for cross-side dependencies. For example, demand-side purchase conversion often depends on supply-side inventory quality, which is a retention metric on the supply side.
