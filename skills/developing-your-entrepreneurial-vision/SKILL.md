# Developing Your Entrepreneurial Vision

> Develop your entrepreneurial vision in the E-Myth Entrepreneur role: write a primary aim, a strategic objective and a one-page vision for your team.

## Overview

The Entrepreneur is the role in Michael Gerber's [technician manager entrepreneur framework](../../methods/technician-manager-entrepreneur-framework/METHOD.md) that decides where the business is going. [Shortform's guide to The E-Myth Revisited](https://www.shortform.com/blog/startup-roles/) describes the Entrepreneur as "always thinking of the future" and liking "to consider what-if scenarios, envisioning strategies, new ways of doing things, and ways to create or reach new markets." Developing an entrepreneurial vision means turning that restless future-thinking into something written that the Manager can build systems around and the team can act on.

Gerber anchors the vision in the owner's life. His business development program starts with the primary aim, which a [StoryShots summary of the book](https://www.getstoryshots.com/books/the-e-myth-revisited-summary/) frames as three questions: "What do I value most? What type of life do I want? Who do I want to become?" The business is meant to serve that aim. Next comes the strategic objective, a set of standards the business must meet, starting with money and including a clear view of what the customer is really buying.

This skill adds a practical frame from outside the E-Myth. Jim Collins and Jerry Porras, writing in [Harvard Business Review](https://hbr.org/1996/09/building-your-companys-vision), split a vision into two parts: core ideology, which "defines what we stand for and why we exist", and an envisioned future, made of a bold goal and a vivid description of achieving it. Their frame helps separate what should never change from what the business is reaching for.

The output is three short documents: the primary aim, the strategic objective and a one-page business vision canvas. Together they answer why the business exists for you, what it must achieve, and what it will look like when it gets there. They also give you decision criteria, which is how a vision earns its keep in daily work.

The entrepreneur mindset is not reserved for founders with a flair for big ideas. Owners who have spent years in Technician mode often find this role the hardest, because it has no deadlines and no immediate output. Treat it as work with a schedule and a deliverable, and it becomes much easier to do.

## How It Works

The primary aim comes first because it sets the terms for everything else. An owner who wants a business that runs without them in a few years will make different choices from one who wants to stay the lead practitioner. Writing the aim down forces that choice into the open. StoryShots describes the purpose as "actively creating your life, rather than waiting to see where life takes you next" ([StoryShots](https://www.getstoryshots.com/books/the-e-myth-revisited-summary/)).

The strategic objective translates the aim into standards for the business. The first standard, in the StoryShots summary, is money: revenue, profit or other financial targets. Another is whether the idea is worth pursuing, judged by the difference between the commodity and the product. "The commodity is the thing your customer walks out with in their hand. The product is what your customer feels as they walk out of your business" ([StoryShots](https://www.getstoryshots.com/books/the-e-myth-revisited-summary/)). Naming the product in that sense tells you what the systems must reliably deliver.

The vision canvas captures the rest on one page. It has four boxes: what the business stands for and why it exists (Collins and Porras's core ideology), the bold goal it is reaching for, a vivid description of what the business looks like when it gets there, and the customers it serves and the change it makes for them. Collins and Porras describe their big goals as ones that "typically require 10 to 30 years' work to complete" ([Collins and Porras, HBR](https://hbr.org/1996/09/building-your-companys-vision)); a small business may choose a shorter horizon, and should say so.

Pressure-testing keeps the vision honest. The Entrepreneur's optimism needs the Manager's pragmatism, which Shortform notes "sees problems rather than opportunities" ([Shortform](https://www.shortform.com/blog/startup-roles/)). Check the canvas against what customers actually buy, what the numbers support and what the team can deliver.

Decision criteria make the vision usable. A short list of questions derived from the canvas, such as "does this serve the customers we chose?" and "does this move us toward the goal?", lets you and your team say yes or no to opportunities without a long debate each time.

## Step-by-Step Guide

### Step 1: Write your primary aim

Answer Gerber's three questions in plain sentences: what you value most, the life you want, and who you want to become. Include how involved you want to be in daily work in a few years. Keep it to a page or less, and write it for yourself rather than for a website. Revisit it before moving on, since everything else depends on it.

### Step 2: Set the strategic objective

Write the standards the business must meet to serve your aim. Start with money: the revenue and profit the business needs, and by when. Then describe your product in Gerber's sense, what customers feel as they leave, as distinct from the commodity they carry away. Add any other standards that matter to you, such as where the business operates or how large the team should be.

### Step 3: Scan for opportunities

Spend a working session looking outward. List changes in your customers' needs, your market and the tools available to you. Talk to a few of your best customers about what they wish you offered. Note where competitors are weak. The output is a short list of opportunities to weigh in the next step.

### Step 4: Fill in the vision canvas

Write one page with four boxes: what the business stands for and why it exists, the bold goal, a vivid description of success, and who you serve and how. Use concrete language that a new employee would understand. The [Collins and Porras frame](https://hbr.org/1996/09/building-your-companys-vision) is a useful check: the first box should hold steady for years, while the goal and description can be updated.

### Step 5: Pressure-test it

Share the draft with someone who thinks like a Manager, such as a finance advisor, an operations lead or a sceptical peer. Ask them what would have to be true for the goal to work and what the numbers say. Revise the canvas where the evidence is weak. Keep the ambition where the evidence allows.

### Step 6: Turn it into decision criteria

Write a short list of questions drawn from the canvas that anyone can use to judge an opportunity or a request. Apply them to a few recent decisions to see whether they would have changed anything. Keep the list short enough that people will remember it.

### Step 7: Share it and review it

Walk your team through the canvas and the decision criteria, and explain how their work connects to it. Put the vision in the same place as your operations manual. Review it on a fixed schedule, usually quarterly, and update the goal and description as the business learns.

## Best Practices

- Write the primary aim before the business vision. A vision that ignores the life you want tends to produce a business you want to leave.
- Keep the canvas to one page. Long vision documents are not reread, and a vision nobody rereads does not guide decisions.
- Use concrete language. "Clients leave with a plan they can start on Monday" guides decisions better than a slogan.
- Separate what stays from what changes. Collins and Porras's distinction between core ideology and envisioned future keeps you from rewriting your values every time the goal moves.
- Schedule the Entrepreneur's work. Vision work has no deadline, so give it protected time and a deliverable.
- Test the vision with a pragmatist. Someone who looks for problems will find the gaps before customers do.

## Common Mistakes

- **Writing a slogan instead of a vision**: A single inspiring line gives no guidance. The canvas needs specifics about customers, goals and what success looks like.
- **Skipping the primary aim**: Owners who jump straight to business goals often build something that conflicts with the life they want. Start with the personal aim.
- **Confusing the goal with the purpose**: Collins and Porras warn against mixing up a big goal with core purpose. The purpose stays; the goal is reached and replaced.
- **Keeping the vision private**: A vision only the owner knows cannot guide the team. Share it and use it in real decisions.
- **Never revisiting it**: Markets and the business change. A vision that is never reviewed drifts away from reality and gets ignored.
