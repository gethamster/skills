# Examples: Choosing Kanban Software

## A startup picks its first digital board

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a four-person startup has run a whiteboard for months. One person now works remotely, and the team needs a digital board without spending much time on administration.

**Walkthrough:**

The team writes down what the whiteboard does: five columns, a limit of two per person in progress, and a short daily meeting. Must-haves are per-column limits, a link to their code repository and a free or low-cost tier. Nice-to-haves are cycle time reports.

They shortlist two tools and rebuild the board in each. One tool supports column limits only on a paid tier; the other records column timestamps that let them calculate cycle time in a spreadsheet. They trial the second for two weeks, run their daily meeting from it, and switch.

## An engineering organization consolidates boards

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: an engineering organization of several teams uses three different board tools. Leadership wants one view of cross-team work, and teams worry about losing their own setups.

**Walkthrough:**

A small group collects each team's columns, limits and policies, and lists shared needs: single sign-on, a code repository integration, cumulative flow diagrams and boards that can roll up into a portfolio view. They weight the criteria with representatives from every team before looking at tools.

Two finalists are set up with each team's real board. The trial shows one tool handles the portfolio view well but makes per-team customization awkward. The group picks the other, which scores slightly lower on reporting but higher on team fit, and records that trade-off. Migration moves active items only, with the old tools kept read-only for reference.

## A marketing team needs client access

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: an agency marketing team manages campaigns for several clients and wants clients to see progress on their own work without seeing other clients.

**Walkthrough:**

The must-haves are per-board or per-lane access control for guests, due dates for fixed-date items and a simple view clients can understand. Kanban practice support comes next: limits on the design column and visible policies.

The team rebuilds one real client board in each finalist and invites a friendly client to the trial. One tool's guest access exposes too much; the other lets clients see only their lane. The team chooses the second and writes a short guide for clients explaining the columns.
