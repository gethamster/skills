# FAQ: Industry Rivalry Assessment Using Qualitative Research

## What makes rivalry intense?

Structural drivers include slow industry growth, many competitors of similar size, low differentiation, low switching costs, high fixed costs and high exit barriers. The [Harvard Institute for Strategy and Competitiveness](https://www.isc.hbs.edu/strategy/business-strategy/Pages/the-five-forces.aspx) lists slow growth among the conditions for fierce rivalry. Behavior matters too: frequent price moves and aggressive switching offers are signs of intense rivalry.

## Why use qualitative market research for rivalry?

Structural data shows how much pressure the industry creates, but not how rivals act on it. Interviews with buyers, salespeople and partners show whether competition runs on price, features, service or sales effort, and what triggers competitive moves. That form matters for strategy.

## How do I measure concentration?

Use market shares to calculate a concentration measure such as the Herfindahl-Hirschman Index, which squares each firm's share and sums the results. The [Department of Justice page](https://www.justice.gov/atr/herfindahl-hirschman-index) explains the calculation and the thresholds it uses. Where shares are uncertain, calculate a range.

## Who counts as a rival?

Companies selling similar or closely related products to the same buyers. Companies that meet the same need with a different kind of product are substitutes, and companies whose products make yours more valuable are complementors. Keeping them apart keeps each force's rating clean.

## How often should rivalry be reassessed?

Set triggers such as mergers, new entrants, funding rounds, a growth slowdown or regulation changes, and reassess when one fires. Also review on a regular schedule, since rivalry can change without a single dramatic event.
