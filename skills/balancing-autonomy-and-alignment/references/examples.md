# Examples: Balancing Squad Autonomy with Organizational Alignment

## Squads moving fast in different directions

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a company has six squads that each ship quickly. In one quarter, two squads build overlapping notification features, and a third changes a shared API without warning the others.

**Walkthrough:**

Leadership writes down three ranked company priorities with the reasoning for each and publishes them. Each tribe links its priorities to that list, and each squad shows how its goals connect. The overlap between the two notification projects is visible within a week, and one squad changes course.

The team adds one collaboration rule: changes to a shared API need a short written notice to known consumers before release. They also add two enabling constraints on security and data retention. At the next health check, squads report their mission as clearer, and none say they feel like pawns.

## Too many constraints

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: after a painful incident, an engineering group adds a long list of mandatory rules, including a single approved framework, a fixed sprint length and architecture board approval for any new service.

**Walkthrough:**

Squad health checks turn red on speed and on "pawns or players" within one quarter. The group reviews each rule and asks what problem it prevents. Most were reactions to one incident, and only three address a real shared risk.

The group keeps those three with their reasoning written down, drops the rest, and lets squads choose their own process and sprint length again. Architecture review becomes advisory, with the final design decision left to the squad building the system.

## Replacing an alignment process that stopped working

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a company sets detailed goals at every level each quarter. Squads spend a week writing goals, and by mid-quarter most goals no longer reflect what matters.

**Walkthrough:**

The company switches to a short, ranked list of company bets on a shared board with Now, Next and Later columns, reviewed monthly. Tribes keep their own boards linked to the company bets, and squads plan against the tribe board without writing separate goal documents.

After two cycles, squads report less time spent on planning and clearer priorities. The company keeps a quarterly tribe review to look back at what was achieved and learned.
