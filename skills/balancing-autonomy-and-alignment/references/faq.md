# FAQ: Balancing Squad Autonomy with Organizational Alignment

## How do I know if my squads have too much autonomy or too little?

Look at two signals. If squads are frequently shipping features that do not contribute to company OKRs, or if two squads build overlapping solutions to the same problem, you have too much autonomy and not enough alignment. If squads consistently escalate routine product decisions to leadership, or if they describe their backlog as "handed down" rather than "chosen," you have too much alignment and not enough autonomy. The health check indicators for mission clarity and ease of release are reliable early warnings.

Green on both suggests the balance is right.

## Should I set OKRs before or after writing mission briefs?

Write mission briefs first. Mission briefs define the stable territory each squad owns and change infrequently. OKRs are quarterly adjustments to focus within that territory. If you write OKRs first, you end up with briefs that are just restatements of the current quarter's goals, which defeats their purpose as a durable alignment tool.

Start with briefs, let them settle for at least one quarter, then use them as the foundation for OKR cascading.

## How many guardrails is too many?

A practical ceiling is fifteen to twenty for a mid-size organization (50-200 engineers). Beyond that, compliance overhead starts eating into squad velocity, and engineers begin ignoring guardrails wholesale because there are too many to remember. The test is whether every engineer in the organization can name the top five guardrails from memory. If they cannot, you have too many or they are not well communicated.

Start with ten, add incrementally, and retire any guardrail whose underlying problem has been architecturally resolved.

## How do I handle a squad that consistently ignores alignment mechanisms?

First, diagnose whether the alignment mechanisms are the problem, not the squad. If the squad's mission brief is vague, its OKRs do not connect to meaningful outcomes, or the guardrails feel arbitrary, the squad's non-compliance may be rational resistance to a broken system. Talk to the squad lead and the team. If the mechanisms are sound and the squad is genuinely optimizing for local convenience over organizational coherence, address it as a leadership coaching issue with the squad lead.

Make the cost of misalignment visible by showing the impact on other squads. Do not solve it by adding more rules, which punishes compliant squads.

## How long should it take for a new alignment framework to start showing results?

Expect one quarter of learning and adjustment, during which health check scores may actually dip as squads adapt to new expectations. By the second quarter, you should see improvements in decision speed (fewer escalations), mission clarity health check scores, and a reduction in cross-squad integration failures. By the third quarter, the system should be producing measurable progress on company OKRs. If you see no improvement by the third quarter, review whether your mission briefs are specific enough, your OKRs are outcome-based, and your guardrails are actually enforced.

## Can I balance autonomy and alignment without using OKRs specifically?

Yes. OKRs are the most common tool, but the underlying principle is expressing desired outcomes without prescribing solutions. You can achieve this with mission-type orders (borrowed from military doctrine), quarterly bets (as used by some product-led companies), or even well-structured North Star metrics with supporting input metrics. ' If your alternative goal-setting framework preserves that separation, it will work.

Avoid frameworks that collapse into task assignment regardless of their name.

## Why does my alignment framework keep drifting and losing effectiveness over time?

Alignment frameworks drift for three common reasons. First, mission briefs are not updated when company strategy shifts, so squads are aligned to an outdated direction. Second, OKR reviews become perfunctory check-the-box exercises rather than genuine discussions about whether outcomes are being achieved. Third, guardrails are not enforced consistently, so squads gradually learn that compliance is optional.

The fix is to treat the alignment framework itself as a product: assign ownership to someone (often a tribe lead or an agile coach), review its effectiveness quarterly using health check data and dependency map trends, and make explicit changes when signals indicate drift.
