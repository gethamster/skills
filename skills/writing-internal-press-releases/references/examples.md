# Examples: Writing an Internal Press Release for a Product Idea

## A scheduling tool that starts from the wrong customer

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a product manager at a small B2B software company wants to propose a shared scheduling feature. The first draft of the internal press release opens with "Teams everywhere struggle with scheduling" and describes an AI engine that finds open slots.

**Walkthrough:**

Her manager asks who "teams everywhere" are, and she cannot answer from evidence. She reads recent support tickets and finds that most scheduling complaints come from clinic front-desk staff who rebook patients after cancellations. She rewrites the subheading around that customer: front-desk staff at small clinics can fill a cancelled appointment from the waitlist in one step.

The problem paragraph now describes the phone calls and sticky notes the front desk uses today. The engine disappears from the first two paragraphs and appears only as one sentence in the solution. The second draft is shorter than the first, and reviewers can argue about something specific.

## Two ideas compared on one page each

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a consumer app team has budget for one new initiative and two candidates: a family sharing plan and an offline mode. Leadership asks the product lead to recommend one.

**Walkthrough:**

The product lead writes an internal press release for each, in the same format, in one afternoon. The offline mode release has a sharp problem paragraph, because commuters without signal already complain about it in reviews. The family plan release struggles to say what is meaningfully better than each person keeping their own account, and its customer quote sounds invented even to its author.

The team reviews both pages side by side. They move the offline mode into the FAQ stage and keep the family plan draft on file with a note on what evidence would revive it.

## A press release that fails the "so what?" test

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: an internal tools team proposes a new dashboard that merges three reporting screens for the finance team into one.

**Walkthrough:**

The draft headline announces "one dashboard for all finance reporting." In review, a finance lead asks "so what?" and explains that the three screens are used by different people at different times, so merging them saves nobody any work. The team's evidence for the problem turns out to be its own frustration maintaining three screens.

The author rewrites the press release with the real customer, the analyst who closes the books each month, and discovers the actual problem is reconciling numbers that disagree between screens. The new solution paragraph describes a single source for those numbers, and the dashboard becomes a detail in the FAQ.
