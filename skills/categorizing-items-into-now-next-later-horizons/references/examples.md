# Examples: Now Next Later Categorization

## A B2B analytics product sorting a long request list

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a product manager inherits a backlog of about sixty feature requests, a few strategic bets from leadership and some technical debt items. The company's objectives this year are to raise retention among mid-sized customers and to open a new industry segment.

**Walkthrough:**

The team groups the requests by problem and ends up with nine initiatives. Several requests for exports, scheduled reports and a dashboard API collapse into one problem: "How can customers get their data into the tools their managers already use?" Interviews and support tickets back the problem strongly, and engineering has built similar export pipelines before, so it goes to Now.

"How can we serve customers in the new industry?" is important to the second objective but rests on a handful of sales conversations, so it goes to Later, at the top of the column, with a note that discovery should start there first. Three initiatives serve neither objective and are parked with a written reason.

## A mobile app where a validated problem still lands in Next

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a consumer fitness app has clear evidence that users drop off after the first week. The team wants to put a "personalized plan" feature straight into Now.

**Walkthrough:**

The categorization session separates problem from solution. The problem, early drop-off, is well validated by usage data and exit surveys. The solution is not: nobody has tested whether personalized plans change behavior, and the recommendation logic would need data the app does not collect yet.

The team places "How can we keep new users active through their second week?" in Next, with personalized plans as one of three candidate ideas, alongside a simpler reminder change and an onboarding redesign. The reminder change is cheap and testable, so it becomes the first experiment. Only the problem framing and the evidence summary go on the roadmap card.

## A platform team with a regulatory deadline

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a payments platform team has to support a new reporting requirement by a fixed date set by a regulator. The team has barely scoped the work, and several product initiatives compete for the same engineers.

**Walkthrough:**

By confidence alone, the reporting work would sit in Next, because the solution is unclear. The external deadline changes that. The team places it in Now, flags it as deadline-driven, and plans a short discovery sprint at the start to reduce the uncertainty quickly.

To make room, two product initiatives move from Now back to Next with a note that they are waiting on capacity and that their evidence has not changed. The rationale lines make the trade visible to stakeholders, who can see why their items moved.
