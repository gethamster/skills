# Examples: Assessing Threats of New Entrants and Substitutes

## Example: B2B SaaS project management tool (mid-market)

**Scenario:**

A 200-person SaaS company offers project management software for 10-200 person teams in North America. They have $15M ARR and compete with well-known players like Asana, Monday.com, and Basecamp. The VP of Strategy wants to understand whether new entrants or substitutes threaten their position over the next 18 months. The team has access to a recent 500-respondent customer survey and Crunchbase data.

**Walkthrough:**

' They scan Crunchbase and find 8 startups that raised Series A or B rounds in the past 18 months targeting similar keywords. They also identify 3 adjacent incumbents (a CRM vendor, a communication platform, and a document collaboration tool) whose job postings and product roadmaps suggest PM feature expansion. For substitutes, the customer survey reveals that 28% of churned customers reverted to shared spreadsheets, 15% hired a dedicated project coordinator, and 12% switched to a Slack-based workflow without formal PM software. The team builds an entrant rubric with 6 factors (scale economies, capital, distribution, brand loyalty, regulatory, network effects) weighted toward brand loyalty (25%) and distribution access (25%) because mid-market buyers rely heavily on trusted brands and integration ecosystems.

The substitute rubric weights switching costs (30%) and buyer propensity (25%) highest. 1 (high threat) due to existing distribution through a massive sales force and low capital requirements since they can cross-sell. 6 because switching costs are near zero and 28% of churners already take this path. The team color-codes the CRM vendor and spreadsheet substitutes as red, assigns owners, and sets up quarterly re-scoring.

The matrix feeds directly into the Six Forces synthesis, where the VP uses it to justify investing in deeper integrations (raising switching costs) and a customer loyalty program (raising brand barriers).

## Example: Direct-to-consumer meal kit brand

**Scenario:**

A DTC meal kit company with $40M annual revenue operates in the US market. They have seen customer acquisition costs rise 35% over the past year and suspect new entrants and substitutes are fragmenting demand. Their consumer market research team recently completed a 1,200-person conjoint analysis study on meal preparation preferences.

**Walkthrough:**

' Entrant identification surfaces 6 new meal kit startups (4 funded, 2 bootstrapped), plus 2 grocery chains launching their own meal kit lines using existing store infrastructure. Substitutes identified through the conjoint study include grocery delivery services with recipe suggestions (mentioned by 22% of respondents), restaurant meal prep subscriptions (18%), and simply eating out more frequently (14%). The entrant rubric weights distribution access (30%) and capital requirements (20%) most heavily because fulfillment logistics are the primary barrier in this category. 4 on entrant threat because they already have distribution, cold chain, and brand recognition, reducing capital requirements to near zero for the kit extension.

9 because switching costs are minimal (consumers already have grocery delivery apps) and the conjoint data shows that 22% of respondents rated the substitute as 'equally satisfying' for their underlying need. The team marks the grocery chain entrants and grocery-delivery substitute as red tier. The strategic response includes pursuing exclusive supplier relationships (raising costs for grocery chain entrants to replicate recipes) and deepening the 'cooking experience' brand positioning to differentiate from the transactional substitute of grocery delivery.

## Example: Small fintech startup targeting underserved banking segment

**Scenario:**

A 30-person fintech startup offers mobile banking for gig workers in the US. They have $2M ARR and recently closed a Series A. The CEO needs to understand whether larger banks or other fintechs will crowd the space before the startup can reach scale. The team has limited budget for consumer market research but has access to 3 months of churn data and a 200-person survey of gig workers.

**Walkthrough:**

' Substitutes from the gig worker survey include staying with a traditional bank and just tolerating the friction (41%), using multiple cash apps for payments without a formal bank account (19%), and relying on employer platforms that offer built-in payments (15%). The entrant rubric weights regulatory barriers (25%) and network effects (20%) highest because banking licenses create hard barriers and gig-worker referral networks drive organic growth. 8 because it already has a license and massive capital, but its score is moderated by low network effects in the gig community (gig workers distrust large banks, per survey data). 0, making it the single largest threat, because switching costs are real (direct deposit changes, new account setup) and 41% of the addressable market already takes this path.

The CEO uses the matrix to prioritize reducing onboarding friction (attacking the substitute's advantage) and building community referral programs (strengthening the network effect barrier against entrants) in the next two quarters.

## Example: Enterprise cybersecurity vendor in a consolidating market

**Scenario:**

A $200M ARR cybersecurity company provides endpoint protection for enterprises with 1,000+ employees. The CISO advisory board has flagged concerns about platform vendors (Microsoft, CrowdStrike) absorbing endpoint functionality into broader suites, and the strategy team needs a structured threat assessment. They have access to Gartner reports, 300-respondent buyer survey data, and a competitive intelligence subscription.

**Walkthrough:**

' Entrant identification focuses on platform consolidation: Microsoft Defender for Endpoint (already live but gaining share), two cloud-native startups with AI-driven detection (Series B and C funded), and a major networking vendor adding endpoint capabilities through acquisition. Substitutes include managed security service providers that replace in-house tools entirely (mentioned by 16% of survey respondents) and relying on operating system built-in security and accepting higher risk (8%, primarily at the lower end of the enterprise segment). The entrant rubric weights switching costs (25%), brand trust (20%), and regulatory compliance certifications (20%). 5 because it bundles endpoint protection with existing E5 licenses, eliminating incremental cost for customers who already pay for Microsoft 365.

Switching cost barriers are inverted: staying with the incumbent vendor is harder to justify than switching to a bundled solution. 3, meaningful for budget-constrained enterprises. The strategy team uses the red-tier Microsoft rating to justify accelerating product differentiation into advanced threat hunting and compliance automation features that Microsoft does not yet bundle. They also initiate a pricing restructuring to compete with the 'free with E5' bundling by demonstrating total cost of ownership savings through superior detection rates, supported by original benchmark data from their consumer market research on enterprise buyer priorities.
