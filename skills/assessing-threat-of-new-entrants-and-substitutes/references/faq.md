# FAQ: Assessing Threats of New Entrants and Substitutes

## What is the difference between a new entrant and a substitute?

A new entrant starts selling the same kind of product to the same buyers. A substitute is a different kind of product that meets the same customer need. Video calls are a substitute for business travel; a new airline on a route is an entrant.

## What are the main barriers to entry?

Porter's list, as summarized in the [five forces article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porter%27s_five_forces_analysis), has seven sources: supply-side economies of scale, demand-side benefits of scale, customer switching costs, capital requirements, incumbency advantages independent of size, unequal access to distribution channels and government policy. Rate each one for your industry with evidence.

## How does consumer market research help?

Customers decide whether switching costs and brand loyalty hold, and whether a substitute is good enough. Interviews with recent and former customers show what alternatives they considered and why, and short surveys can check how common a finding is across the customer base.

## Why worry about substitutes that are worse than our product?

Because they may improve. The [Christensen Institute](https://www.christenseninstitute.org/theory/disruptive-innovation/) describes products that take root at the bottom of a market by being cheaper and more accessible and then move upmarket. Record each substitute's direction as well as its current performance.

## How do we keep the assessment current?

Name signals for each important candidate, such as patent filings, funding rounds, product launches or customers mentioning an alternative, and assign someone to check them on a schedule. Reassess when a signal fires.
