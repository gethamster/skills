# FAQ: Assessing Threats of New Entrants and Substitutes

## How do I assess threats of new entrants when my industry has very few public funding records?

Shift your intelligence sources from funding databases to hiring patterns, patent filings, and conference participation. Job boards like LinkedIn and Indeed reveal companies hiring for roles in your market segment months before public announcements. Patent filings in relevant technology classes surface R&D investments. Industry conference exhibitor and speaker lists identify companies positioning themselves as players.

Supplement these with consumer market research: ask your customers and prospects directly whether they have been approached by or evaluated any new vendors in the past 12 months.

## How long should the initial threat assessment take for a typical mid-market company?

Plan for 3-5 hours of focused work spread over 1-2 weeks. The calendar spread matters because Steps 2 and 3 (identification) benefit from letting your subconscious process between sessions, and Step 8 (frontline validation) requires scheduling conversations with sales and success teams. If you already have recent consumer market research and competitive intelligence subscriptions, you can compress to the lower end. If you need to commission new survey data, add 2-3 weeks for fielding and analysis.

## Should I assess entrant and substitute threats before or after evaluating buyer and supplier power?

The [Six Forces Model](https://tryhamster.com/methods/six-forces-model) does not require a strict sequence, but most practitioners find it efficient to assess buyer and supplier power first because those analyses surface data you will reuse here. For instance, buyer power research reveals switching cost perceptions that directly feed your substitute scoring rubric. If you run entrant and substitute assessment in parallel with [evaluating buyer and supplier bargaining power](https://tryhamster.com/skills/evaluating-buyer-and-supplier-power), share switching cost data between the two workstreams to avoid redundant research.

## Why does my threat matrix keep showing every entrant as 'medium' threat with scores clustered around 3.0?

Score compression usually indicates one of two problems: either your rubric definitions lack sufficient granularity (the difference between a 2 and a 3 is ambiguous), or your team scored collaboratively and anchored on the first suggestion. Fix the rubric by writing specific, observable criteria for each level, such as 'Score 1 means less than $1M funding and no distribution partnerships. Score 5 means over $100M funding and existing access to 50%+ of target customers.' Fix the anchoring by having each scorer submit ratings independently before group discussion.

## How do I incorporate consumer market research into entrant threat scoring when the entrant has not launched yet?

Use concept testing and willingness-to-switch survey questions. Describe the entrant's likely value proposition (based on their funding pitch, job postings, and patent filings) without naming the company, then ask your customer panel how likely they would be to evaluate such a product on a 1-5 scale, what price point would make them consider switching, and what features would need to be present. This gives you proxy data for the switching cost and buyer propensity factors in your entrant rubric even before the competitor has a live product.

## What is the minimum sample size for consumer market research to make threat scores credible?

For B2C markets, aim for at least 300 respondents per segment to get statistically meaningful results for switching propensity and feature preference questions. For B2B markets where your total addressable market is smaller, 50-100 respondents from your ICP can be sufficient if the sample is well-targeted. The key is not just sample size but sample quality: respondents must be actual buyers or users in your market, not general population panels. If you cannot reach minimum sample sizes, supplement survey data with behavioral data (churn analysis, trial rates) and qualitative interviews (10-15 deep interviews can surface patterns that surveys miss).

## How often should I update the threat matrix after the initial assessment?

Quarterly updates are the standard cadence for most industries. At each quarterly review, re-score the top-10 threats using any new consumer market research, funding announcements, or product launches that occurred since the last review. Between scheduled reviews, monitor for predefined triggers: a major funding round, a regulatory change, a spike in customer churn to a specific alternative, or a public product launch by a tracked entrant. Any trigger should prompt an out-of-cycle update to the affected rows in the matrix.

Markets undergoing rapid disruption (AI, crypto) may need monthly reviews until the landscape stabilizes.
