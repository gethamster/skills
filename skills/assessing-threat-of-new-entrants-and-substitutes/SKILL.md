# Assessing Threats of New Entrants and Substitutes

## Overview

Assessing the threats of new entrants and substitutes covers the two forces in the [Six Forces Model](../../methods/six-forces-model/METHOD.md) that come from outside the current set of competitors. New entrants are companies that could start selling the same kind of product to the same buyers. Substitutes are different products that meet the same customer need, such as video calls in place of business travel. Both cap what incumbents can earn: entrants by adding capacity and competition, substitutes by limiting the price customers will pay before they switch to something else.

The two forces need different questions. The entrant question is about barriers: what stops a capable company from entering? The [five forces article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porter%27s_five_forces_analysis) lists the seven major sources of entry barriers Porter identifies: supply-side economies of scale, demand-side benefits of scale (network effects), customer switching costs, capital requirements, incumbency advantages independent of size, unequal access to distribution channels, and government policy. The substitute question is about the customer: what else could do the job, and at what price and performance? The same article lists factors such as buyer propensity to substitute, relative price-performance, switching costs and the ease of substitution.

Consumer market research is central to both, because customers decide whether a barrier holds and whether a substitute is good enough. Switching costs, brand loyalty and the appeal of an alternative are hard to judge from company data alone. Interviews with recent buyers and lost customers, and short surveys where the population is large, show how customers see the alternatives. The [SBA's competitive analysis guidance](https://www.sba.gov/business-guide/plan-your-business/market-research-competitive-analysis) asks for both barriers to market entry and indirect or secondary competitors, which are the entrant and substitute questions in plain terms.

The trap in both forces is looking only at what exists today. Serious entrants can come from adjacent markets, where a company already has customers, technology or distribution. Some of the most serious substitutes are worse than your product today but improving fast. The [Christensen Institute's summary of disruptive innovation](https://www.christenseninstitute.org/theory/disruptive-innovation/) describes this pattern: a product takes root in simple applications at the bottom of a market, typically by being cheaper and more accessible, and then moves upmarket. An assessment that rates only current threats misses the ones that matter most.

## How It Works

The two forces are assessed in parallel but rated separately.

For new entrants, start by listing the plausible candidates: companies in adjacent segments or regions, suppliers or customers that could integrate, and well-funded startups. Then test each of the seven barriers for your industry. Some can be measured, such as capital requirements or regulatory approvals. Others, such as switching costs and brand loyalty, need customer evidence. For each candidate, note which barriers it could overcome with assets it already has. A barrier that stops a startup may not stop an adjacent company with an existing customer base.

For substitutes, start from the customer need and look beyond your product category. Ask customers what they did before they bought your product, what else they considered and what they would do without it. Lost customers are especially useful: those who left the category entirely point to substitutes, while those who moved to a competitor point to rivalry. For each substitute, compare price and performance with your product and note the trend. A substitute that is improving quickly deserves a higher rating than its current performance suggests.

Public signals help find candidates early. The USPTO's [Patent Public Search](https://www.uspto.gov/patents/search/patent-public-search) shows who is filing patents in your space. Company filings and funding news show adjacent companies building relevant capabilities. Search interest can hint at a rising alternative, but [Google Trends](https://support.google.com/trends/answer/4365533) reports relative interest scaled from 0 to 100, not search volume, so use it for direction only.

Customer evidence ties it together. Interviews should ask about specific past decisions, following [Nielsen Norman Group's advice](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/user-interviews/) to ask about specific events and to avoid leading questions. Surveys can then check how widely an interview finding holds.

The output is two short assessments. Each has a rating, the barriers or substitute factors behind it, the named candidates, the evidence and a list of signals to watch.

## Step-by-Step Guide

### Step 1: Define the scope and the customer need

Write down the industry scope used for the analysis, then state the customer need in terms that do not name your product category, such as "keep a remote team aligned" rather than "project management software." The scope defines who counts as an entrant. The need defines what counts as a substitute. Check both with a colleague who knows the customers.

### Step 2: List candidate entrants

List companies that could enter: adjacent segments, other regions, suppliers or customers that could integrate, platforms that could add the capability, and funded startups. For each, note the assets it would bring, such as customers, distribution, technology or brand. Use filings, funding news and patent searches to find candidates the team does not already track. Keep the list to candidates with a plausible route in.

### Step 3: Test the entry barriers

Rate each of the seven barriers for your industry, citing evidence. Use customer interviews for switching costs and loyalty, public data for capital and regulatory requirements, and channel checks for distribution access. Then, for each candidate entrant, note which barriers it could get past. Base the threat rating on the strongest credible candidates.

### Step 4: List candidate substitutes

Using the customer need, list the alternatives customers use or could use, including manual work, general-purpose tools and doing nothing. Ask recent buyers what they used before and lost customers what they switched to. Group similar alternatives together. Include alternatives that are worse today but improving.

### Step 5: Rate each substitute with consumer market research

For each substitute, compare its price and performance with yours, estimate the cost of switching, and judge how willing customers are to switch. Use interviews for the reasons behind switching and, where the customer base is large, a short survey to check how common those reasons are. Record the direction of improvement for each substitute. Rate the overall threat of substitutes from the strongest candidates.

### Step 6: Set signals to watch

For each high-rated entrant or substitute, write down the signals that would show the threat growing: a patent filing, a funding round, a pricing change, a partnership, a rise in customers mentioning the alternative. Assign someone to check them on a schedule. These signals turn the assessment into an early warning system.

### Step 7: Write and hand off the two assessments

Write the new entrant and substitute assessments separately: rating, drivers, named candidates, evidence, direction and signals. Note links to other forces, such as a substitute that also weakens your complement ecosystem. Hand both to the synthesis step with confidence levels marked.

## Best Practices

- Keep the two forces separate. Entrants sell the same kind of product; substitutes meet the same need differently. Mixing them blurs both ratings.
- Look at adjacent markets early. Companies with existing customers or distribution can get past barriers that stop startups.
- Rate by trajectory. A substitute that is weak today but improving fast is a bigger threat than a mature one, the pattern the [Christensen Institute](https://www.christenseninstitute.org/theory/disruptive-innovation/) describes as moving upmarket.
- Talk to customers who left the category. They are the most direct evidence of substitutes, and they are easy to miss if you only interview current customers.
- Name the candidates. A threat rating with no named entrant or substitute behind it is hard to check and hard to act on.
- Treat government policy as a barrier that can move. Government policy, including patents and regulatory requirements, appears in [Porter's list of entry barriers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porter%27s_five_forces_analysis), and a policy change can open or close a market quickly.

## Common Mistakes

- **Only counting startups as entrants**: Established companies from adjacent markets can be stronger entrants than startups. List them explicitly and test barriers against their assets.
- **Defining substitutes by product category**: Starting from your category finds rivals. Start from the customer need to find substitutes.
- **Rating current performance only**: Substitutes that are improving quickly get dismissed. Record and weigh their trajectory.
- **Treating search trends as demand**: A rise in relative search interest is only a hint about adoption. Confirm with customer evidence.
- **No follow-up signals**: The assessment is written once and forgotten. Set signals and owners so the team notices when a threat grows.
