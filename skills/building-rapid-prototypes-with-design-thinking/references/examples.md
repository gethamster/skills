# Examples: Building Rapid Prototypes

## Paper screens for a clinic check-in app

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a small product team wants to know whether patients can check in on a tablet without help from reception staff.

**Walkthrough:**

The team writes its learning question: can a first-time patient reach the confirmation screen unaided? In about an hour they sketch six paper screens, making the button labels real words and leaving everything else as boxes. They tape the screens to a tablet-sized card and set up at the clinic entrance, where patients arrive carrying coats and forms. One teammate flips screens as patients tap, while another notes every hesitation without explaining anything.

Most patients stall on the insurance screen, so the team decides to refine that step and retest the next morning, leaving the rest untouched.

## Role-playing a returns desk

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a retail operations lead is exploring a new returns process where customers are greeted by a floor associate instead of queuing at a counter.

**Walkthrough:**

The team assigns roles for customer, floor associate, and stockroom, and writes a loose script covering greeting, inspection, and refund. They act it out in a corner of the store using a clipboard and a shopping bag as props. A colleague from finance plays the customer and immediately asks where to go when the associate is busy with someone else. That question was not in the script, and it exposes a gap in how waiting customers are handled.

The team rejects the no-counter version and prototypes a hybrid with a small visible waiting spot.

## Storyboard for a onboarding email sequence

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a marketing and product pair want to test whether a new customer onboarding sequence tells a coherent story before anyone writes the emails.

**Walkthrough:**

They draw eight rough panels on sticky notes, each showing the customer's situation and the message they receive at that moment. Instead of presenting it, they hand the storyboard to three recent customers and ask them to narrate what they think is happening. Two customers read the third panel as a sales pitch rather than help, which the team had not expected. The pair keep the overall sequence, rewrite that panel's intent, and retest with new customers.

Only after the storyboard reads clearly do they start drafting real email copy.
