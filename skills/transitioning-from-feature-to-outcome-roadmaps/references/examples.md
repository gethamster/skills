# Examples: Transitioning from Feature to Outcome-Based Roadmaps

## A pilot quarter in a sales-led company

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a B2B software company plans its roadmap as a list of features with quarter dates, most of them requested by sales. The new head of product wants to move to outcomes but knows sales relies on the dates to talk to customers.

**Walkthrough:**

The head of product audits the roadmap with the sales lead. Of the dozen items, a few are tied to signed contracts, several support a retention problem everyone agrees on, and the rest have no clear reason beyond a single customer request. The CEO agrees to sponsor a one-quarter pilot on one team.

The pilot outcome is fewer accounts going inactive in their first two months, measured weekly. The contract items stay with their dates. The retention-related features become candidate initiatives, and the rest are parked with a note to each requester. At the end of the quarter, inactivity has dropped modestly, one initiative was abandoned after two weeks of flat readings, and the contracts were delivered on time. Sales agrees to extend the approach to a second team, provided the committed items stay visible with dates.

## Relabeling caught early

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a product manager rewrites the roadmap with outcome headings. Under "improve engagement" sit the same five features, in the same order, with the same dates as before.

**Walkthrough:**

In review, a peer asks which of the five features would be dropped if engagement rose after the first two. Nobody knows, because the outcome was added after the features were chosen. The roadmap has changed labels without changing how decisions are made.

The product manager redoes the step properly: writes the engagement outcome with a metric and baseline, maps each feature to it with a hypothesis, and ranks them by evidence. Two features turn out to have no plausible link to engagement and move to a separate list tied to a platform-health goal. The first initiative is chosen, and the others wait as reserves.

## Using a dated template as a bridge

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: an operations-heavy business has leadership that is uncomfortable with any roadmap that lacks dates. A pure Now-Next-Later roadmap is rejected in the first discussion.

**Walkthrough:**

The product manager switches to a goal-oriented template that keeps a date or timeframe on each goal, with a few coarse features and a metric under it, similar to Roman Pichler's GO product roadmap. Leadership sees dates, but the dates now belong to goals rather than to individual features.

After two quarters of goals being reviewed against their metrics, leadership starts asking about the metrics first. The team then widens the later timeframes and shows only the nearest goal with a specific date, which moves the roadmap closer to a horizon format without a second argument.
