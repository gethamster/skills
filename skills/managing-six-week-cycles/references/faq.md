# FAQ: Managing Shape Up Six-Week Build Cycles

## Why does Shape Up use six-week cycles?

Basecamp found two-week cycles too short to get anything meaningful done and expensive in planning overhead. Six weeks was long enough to finish a whole project and short enough that the team could feel the deadline from the start. The book presents this as the length that worked for Basecamp after years of experimenting.

## What is the circuit breaker?

It is the policy that a project which does not ship by the end of its cycle does not get an extension by default. The book's glossary calls it a risk management technique: cancel projects that do not ship in one cycle by default instead of extending them by default. The idea goes back to shaping, where a better approach can be pitched again.

## When is it acceptable to extend a project?

Rarely. The book allows an extension only when the outstanding tasks are true must-haves that survived scope hammering and all of the remaining work is downhill, with no open questions. Even then it prefers to enforce the appetite, and says the cool-down usually gives enough slack for a short overrun.

## What does the manager do during the cycle?

Protect the team's time, answer questions about the pitch, and watch the hill chart for scopes that stop moving. The team defines its own tasks, so the manager does not assign work or run daily stand-ups. At the end, the manager helps make the ship or circuit-breaker call.

## Can we use shorter cycles?

Yes. The book says six weeks may not be the exact time frame for your team, as long as bets are deliberate and the downside is capped. Shorter cycles fit smaller projects; if you shorten the cycle, shape smaller bets instead of splitting one project across several cycles.

## What if the team finishes early?

Let them use the time to polish, handle nice-to-haves, or start cool-down work early. The book's advice on getting started is to judge a cycle by its outcome: whether the project shipped and the team feels it made progress. Do not pull the team onto unrelated work mid-cycle, because it weakens the commitment that makes the next bet credible.
