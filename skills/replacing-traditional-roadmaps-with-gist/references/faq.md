# FAQ: Replacing Traditional Product Roadmaps with GIST Planning

## How long does the full transition from a traditional product manager roadmap to GIST take?

Plan for one quarter of parallel operation followed by one quarter of full GIST adoption with refinements. The parallel quarter lets you honor existing commitments while piloting GIST on two to three goals. The second quarter is when you expand GIST to all goals and sunset the old roadmap. In total, expect three to six months before the team is operating fluently in GIST.

Smaller teams with fewer stakeholder dependencies can move faster, sometimes completing the transition in six to eight weeks.

## Should I replace my product manager roadmap with GIST before or after defining measurable goals?

Define at least your pilot goals before starting the transition, but you do not need all goals defined upfront. The goal-setting process is covered in [defining measurable product goals](https://tryhamster.com/skills/defining-measurable-product-goals). Start with two or three goals for your pilot quarter. You will define the remaining goals as part of expanding GIST in the following quarter.

Trying to define every goal before beginning the transition is a form of over-planning that delays action.

## What do I do if stakeholders refuse to give up the traditional roadmap format?

Do not fight the format war directly. Instead, change the content while keeping a familiar wrapper. You can produce a document called a "roadmap" that lists goals instead of features, top-ranked ideas instead of committed builds, and evidence-based timelines instead of arbitrary dates. Many teams successfully run GIST internally while producing a stakeholder-facing summary that looks like an evolved roadmap.

Over time, as stakeholders see better results, they stop caring about the format.

## How do I handle features that are contractually committed to customers during the GIST transition?

Contractual commitments are non-negotiable. Deliver them on schedule and do not route them through GIST. Track them separately as a "commitments" workstream that runs alongside your GIST goals. The purpose of GIST is to improve how you make new decisions, not to break existing promises.

As old commitments are fulfilled and new deals are structured around outcomes rather than features, the commitments workstream will naturally shrink.

## Why does my GIST plan keep reverting back to a feature list?

This usually happens because goals are too vague, which makes the team default to the familiar concreteness of features. If a goal says "improve the user experience," the team will instinctively convert it to specific features because they need something actionable. Tighten every goal to a specific metric with a baseline and target. The second common cause is that ICE scores are not being updated, so the idea bank ossifies into a static list that feels just like the old roadmap.

Review and rescore ideas every two to three weeks.

## Can I use GIST for just part of my product while keeping a traditional roadmap for the rest?

Yes, and this is actually the recommended approach for the first quarter. Many teams permanently run GIST for new product development and innovation while keeping a more traditional execution plan for maintenance, infrastructure, and compliance work that does not benefit from hypothesis testing. The key is to be explicit about which workstreams use which system. Confusion arises when the boundary is unclear and people are unsure which process to follow.

## How do I convince my CEO or VP that a product manager roadmap based on GIST is better than feature commitments?

Do not try to convince them with theory. Run a small pilot and let the results speak. Executives respond to evidence: "We tested three ideas in two weeks and found the one that actually moves the metric, saving six weeks of engineering time on the other two." Frame GIST as risk reduction and faster ROI, not as a process preference. The financial argument is powerful: GIST prevents the team from spending months building features that do not move business metrics, which is the most expensive mistake a product team can make.
