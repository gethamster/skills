# Examples: Replacing Traditional Product Roadmaps with GIST Planning

## Example: B2B SaaS startup transitioning a 12-person product team

**Scenario:**

A Series A B2B SaaS company with a 12-person product and engineering team has a traditional quarterly roadmap that lists 15-20 features per quarter. The CEO uses the roadmap in board presentations. Sales references it in prospect calls. The Head of Product wants to move to GIST because the team keeps building features that do not move activation or retention metrics.

**Walkthrough:**

The Head of Product audited the Q2 roadmap and found 18 items. Only 4 were hard commitments tied to signed customer contracts. She mapped the remaining 14 to five business goals: increase trial-to-paid conversion (8% to 14%), reduce churn in the first 90 days (18% to 10%), grow enterprise pipeline (10 to 25 qualified leads per month), improve NPS from 32 to 50, and reduce support ticket volume by 30%. Each original feature became one idea under the relevant goal, and she added 2-3 alternative ideas per goal from customer interview notes and support ticket analysis.

For the pilot quarter, she chose two goals: trial-to-paid conversion and support ticket reduction. These had clear metrics, no hard commitments attached, and supportive team leads. She ran ICE scoring with the relevant teams. For the conversion goal, the highest-scored idea was not the planned "add more onboarding tooltips" but "send personalized setup assistance emails based on usage patterns," which scored higher on impact and ease.

The team ran a two-week step-project: they manually sent personalized setup emails to 50 trial users and tracked conversion versus a control group. Conversion jumped from 8% to 11% in the test group. Meanwhile, the four hard-commitment features continued shipping on the old roadmap schedule. At the end of Q2, she presented both systems side by side: the GIST pilot produced a validated approach to conversion that was now being productized, while two of the old roadmap features shipped on time but had no measurable metric impact.

The CEO saw the comparison and approved expanding GIST to all goals for Q3.

## Example: Enterprise product organization with multiple stakeholder dependencies

**Scenario:**

A 200-person product organization at a mid-market enterprise software company has annual roadmaps reviewed by the executive team, referenced by field sales, and embedded in customer contracts as delivery commitments. The VP of Product wants to adopt GIST but faces significant organizational inertia and contractual constraints.

**Walkthrough:**

The VP started by cataloging every contractual feature commitment across all customer agreements and found 23 features that were legally required for delivery within the next two quarters. These were non-negotiable. She then identified 40+ features on the roadmap that were soft commitments based on sales conversations or executive preferences. She converted the soft commitments into goals and ideas using a two-day workshop with her product directors.

The group identified eight strategic goals for the year, each with measurable metrics. She chose one goal per product line, three total, for a Q3 pilot. " She built a one-page translation document that showed executives the same information they expected: what the team was working on, when results were expected, and what confidence level the team had. The only difference was that confidence was now backed by step-project evidence rather than gut feel.

After one quarter, two of the three pilot goals showed measurable progress. The third goal's top idea was invalidated by a step-project, which actually impressed the CFO because the team caught a bad bet before spending a quarter of engineering capacity on it. The VP expanded GIST to all product lines the following quarter, while maintaining a "contractual commitments" track that ran separately with fixed timelines.

## Example: Small startup with a two-person product team and no formal roadmap

**Scenario:**

A pre-seed startup with two co-founders and one engineer has been building features based on whatever the CEO heard in the last customer call. There is no formal roadmap, just a Notion page with a scrambled list of feature ideas. The product co-founder wants to introduce structure without slowing down the team's pace.

**Walkthrough:**

The product co-founder spent two hours reviewing the Notion page and grouped the 30+ feature ideas under four business goals: reach $10K MRR by end of Q2 (currently at $3K), reduce weekly churn from 8% to 3%, get 50 organic signups per week (currently 12), and close the first enterprise deal. She did not need a formal transition because there was nothing formal to transition from. She created a simple spreadsheet with four tabs, one per goal, and listed every feature idea under the relevant goal. She added two alternative ideas per goal from her own customer research.

The three-person team ran ICE scoring in a 45-minute meeting. For the MRR goal, the top idea was not the CEO's favorite feature (a dashboard redesign) but a pricing page overhaul with annual billing, which scored highest on impact and ease. They ran a one-week step-project: they added an annual billing option to the existing pricing page and emailed 200 trial users. Twelve users upgraded within the week, adding $1,800 in committed annual revenue.

The CEO, initially skeptical of the process, immediately saw the value of testing before building. The team adopted GIST fully without any formal transition period because the old system was just informal chaos. Within two months, they had tested six ideas, killed three, and were building the two that had the strongest validation data. The engineer reported feeling more confident about what he was building because he could see the evidence behind each decision.

## Example: Consumer mobile app team managing executive pressure for a specific feature

**Scenario:**

A consumer mobile app company with 2M users has a C-suite executive who has been pushing for a social sharing feature for six months. The feature is prominent on the roadmap despite no user research supporting it. The product lead wants to use GIST to introduce evidence-based decision making without directly opposing the executive.

**Walkthrough:**

" She listed social sharing as Idea #1 and added four alternatives: referral rewards program, content partnerships with influencers, push notification optimization, and app store optimization. " The executive agreed. The team ran a two-week step-project on social sharing: they added a basic share button to three high-engagement screens and tracked usage. 3% of users tapped it, and of those, the shared links generated only 12 new installs in two weeks.

In parallel, they ran a one-week step-project on the referral rewards program: a simple "invite a friend, both get premium for a week" mechanic. This generated 340 new installs in one week. The evidence was unambiguous. The product lead presented both results to the executive with no editorial commentary, just the numbers.

The executive dropped the social sharing push and became an advocate for step-project testing. " She said "let's test all the ideas and see which one wins," and then let the data do the work.
