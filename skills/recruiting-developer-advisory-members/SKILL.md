## Overview

Developer advisory board recruitment is the first practical step of the [Technical Advisory Board (TAB) Framework](../../methods/technical-advisory-board-tab-framework/METHOD.md). The goal is a set of potential users and customers, drawn from every role that influences whether your product gets adopted, who each agree to a short monthly interview for six months. Adam Frankl, who designed the TAB, is explicit that these are not senior technical advisors helping you build the product. They are the people whose problems you are trying to understand ([Frankl](https://medium.com/@graphmaven/the-1-most-important-process-64c037d9b0c2)).

Recruiting takes a long time. Frankl's own numbers are sobering: he expects to contact 50 people for every one who joins, which for a board of 50 means 2500 outreach messages, and he recommends sending 50 every day ([How to recruit TAB members](https://medium.com/@graphmaven/how-to-recruit-tab-members-a35d44904b24)). Most developers will ignore you. Frankl treats that as a filter: the people for whom the problem is really painful are the ones who reply.

The invitation is what makes the difference between a reply and silence. Frankl's formula is "Thank, praise, and then beg": thank the person for reading, praise something specific they have written or done, and ask whether they would consider joining your technical advisory board. Every message is researched and written for one person. He is blunt about the alternative: "If you don't have the time to research each person, you don't deserve a reply."

The ask itself is deliberately small and bounded. Members give thirty minutes a month for six months, and the arrangement ends after that ([Scaling DevTools](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gdqqovc3REs)). They are thanked with stickers, mugs, or t-shirts, not money or equity. Recruiting ends when a person agrees to the first call; everything after that belongs to the interview and board management skills.

## How It Works

Recruiting starts from a hypothesis about who influences adoption. Frankl describes adoption inside a company as a lock with several cylinders: the developer who wants the tool is one, and the budget holder, the security lead, and the platform owner may be others ([Scaling DevTools clip](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6-zY1JRxjV4)). If you only recruit enthusiasts, you learn nothing about the people who will block you. So the recruiting plan lists every persona and aims for several members in each. Frankl's list for developer tools includes developers and dev leaders, CTOs and VPs of engineering, and where relevant SREs, QA, and security staff.

For each persona, write down job titles, company types, and regions to search for. Frankl calls LinkedIn his favorite platform for finding people, because nearly everyone's work history is there. He also recruits from people the founders already know. Those early contacts are useful for momentum, but they may share the founders' assumptions, so balance them with strangers.

Where you find a person determines what you can praise. The strongest invitations reference something the person has published about the problem: a post, a talk, a thread. Frankl's sample invitation is a few short sentences. It thanks the reader, says their post about the problem was insightful, says you founded a startup to attack that problem, and asks whether they would consider joining your technical advisory board, with a short chat offered to explain what that involves ([Frankl](https://medium.com/@graphmaven/how-to-recruit-tab-members-a35d44904b24)).

Frankl has noted that cold outreach has become harder. In a 2026 interview he says LinkedIn messages are easier to ignore than they were, partly because of AI-generated messages, and recommends a complementary route: publish regularly on topics your target members care about, and when someone engages, reach out quickly and ask for a short call ([Scaling DevTools](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=O7Dj4zriBeY)). The published material and the TAB calls feed each other.

When someone replies, the short chat confirms two things: that the person actually has the problem, and that they understand the commitment. Frankl treats the thirty-minutes-a-month ask as a test in itself. If the problem matters to someone, they have the time; if nobody will give it, he says, it is time to rethink what the startup is about ([Scaling DevTools](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gdqqovc3REs)).

## Step-by-Step Guide

### Step 1: Write the persona list

List every role that can help or block adoption of your product, from the person who uses it daily to the person who signs off on budget or security. For each, write the job titles, company types, and regions you will search. Treat the list as a hypothesis that the calls will test. Aim for several members per persona, following [Frankl's guidance](https://medium.com/@graphmaven/the-1-most-important-process-64c037d9b0c2) that you need several from each persona. Keep the list on one page so the whole team uses the same targets.

### Step 2: Build a candidate list

Start with people the founders know, then search LinkedIn by the titles and company types on your list. Look hardest for people who have written or spoken publicly about the problem, because that gives you something specific to praise. Record each candidate with their persona, where you found them, and the item you plan to mention. Keep a running count per persona so gaps are visible early.

### Step 3: Write each invitation by hand

Use Frankl's structure: thank, praise, then ask ([How to recruit TAB members](https://medium.com/@graphmaven/how-to-recruit-tab-members-a35d44904b24)). Keep it to a few sentences, name the specific post or achievement, state the problem your startup is attacking, and ask whether they would consider joining your technical advisory board. Do not mention product features or ask for a demo slot. Offer a short chat to explain what membership involves.

### Step 4: Send a batch every day

Send a set number of individual messages every working day and keep going. Frankl's own target is 50 a day, and he describes outreach as the best use of a founder's time at this stage ([Frankl](https://medium.com/@graphmaven/how-to-recruit-tab-members-a35d44904b24)). If your volume is lower, the board will simply fill more slowly. Do not send follow-up sequences that turn the message into a campaign.

### Step 5: Publish on the problem and reach out to people who engage

Post regularly about the problem area on the channels your personas use. When someone comments on or shares a post, contact them soon after and ask for a short call, as Frankl recommends in [his 2026 interview](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=O7Dj4zriBeY). These people have already shown interest in the topic, so the invitation reads as a continuation of a conversation. Share what you are learning from calls in the posts, with members' permission and without naming them.

### Step 6: Hold the short chat and set expectations

When someone replies, explain the commitment plainly: a thirty-minute call each month for six months, one on one, recorded with permission, about their problems and not your product. Confirm that they actually have the problem you are studying. Tell them how you will thank them, with swag rather than payment, following Frankl's principle of "appreciation, not compensation" ([Scaling DevTools](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gdqqovc3REs)). Book the first call before you hang up.

### Step 7: Review coverage every week

Once a week, count members by persona and compare with the list from Step 1. Shift outreach toward personas with too few members, especially the gatekeeping roles that are easy to neglect. Note any persona that turns out not to exist in practice, and any new role that members keep mentioning. Update the persona list so the next batch of outreach reflects what you have learned.

## Best Practices

- Recruit for every role that touches adoption. Frankl's lock metaphor explains why: one unrepresented role can block a purchase that every developer wanted ([Scaling DevTools clip](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6-zY1JRxjV4)).
- Praise something real and specific. A reference to a person's own post shows you did the research, and it filters your list toward people who care about the problem.
- State the commitment and its end date up front. A six-month term is easier to accept than an open-ended relationship, and it gives both sides a clean finish.
- Keep swag ready. Frankl's post suggests stickers, mugs, or t-shirts ([Frankl](https://medium.com/@graphmaven/the-1-most-important-process-64c037d9b0c2)), and in a [Scaling DevTools interview](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gdqqovc3REs) he explains that the swag is there so members can brag to their peers that they sit on the startup's technical advisory board.
- Treat silence as information. If nobody in a persona responds after sustained outreach, the problem may not matter to that role, which is worth knowing before you build for them.
- Keep one tracker for candidates, invitations, replies, and members, so anyone on the team can see recruiting progress without asking.

## Common Mistakes

- **Recruiting only friendly developers**: A board of enthusiasts from your network confirms what you already believe. Balance known contacts with strangers found through search and published work.
- **Batch-and-blast messages**: Template outreach sent to a list reads as marketing and gets ignored. Frankl's rule is to research each person, and he says those who will not do that "don't deserve a reply" ([Frankl](https://medium.com/@graphmaven/how-to-recruit-tab-members-a35d44904b24)).
- **Recruiting advisors instead of users**: Senior engineers who will tell you how to build the product are useful, but they are a different group. TAB members are the people who have the problem.
- **Paying members cash**: Frankl argues payment makes the relationship feel like a bribe and changes what people tell you. Offer appreciation and swag instead.
- **Stopping outreach once the first members join**: Members finish their six months and some drop out earlier. Keep a steady flow of invitations so there are always calls on the calendar.
