# Call-to-Action Copywriting: Writing High-Converting CTAs

> Call to action copywriting: write CTA button copy that names what the visitor gets and what happens next, with simple formulas and microcopy.

## Overview

A call to action is the button or link that asks the reader to take the page's next step, and call to action copywriting is the craft of labeling it. The label is a promise about the next screen. When the promise is specific and accurate, the right visitors click and are not surprised. When it is vague, some visitors hesitate and others click, find something they did not expect, and leave.

This skill applies the [copywriting framework](../../methods/copywriting-framework/METHOD.md) to those few words. Corey Haines's [copywriting skill](https://github.com/coreyhaines31/marketingskills/blob/main/skills/copywriting/SKILL.md) lists Submit, Sign Up, Learn More, Click Here and Get Started as weak labels, and gives a simple CTA copy formula: an action verb, plus what the visitor gets, plus a qualifier if needed. His examples include "Start My Free Trial," "Get the Complete Checklist" and "See Pricing for My Team."

Usability research explains why generic labels underperform. NN/g's study of [Get Started buttons](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/get-started/) found that the phrase is ambiguous and can apply to almost any goal a user might have, and that such buttons sometimes pushed people into sign-up flows when they only wanted information. The authors note that setting the wrong expectation may reach superficial conversion goals without keeping customers happy. A related NN/g article on [Learn More links](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/learn-more-links/) finds the label weak on information scent and poor for screen reader users, who may hear it without the surrounding text.

Button copy best practices therefore come down to three questions the label and its microcopy should answer: what will I get, what happens when I click, and what will it cost me in money, time or commitment. The GOV.UK guidance on [writing links](https://guidance.publishing.service.gov.uk/writing-to-gov-uk-standards/writing-guidelines/add-links/) states the same idea for links in general: the text should tell users where they are going, what they need to do there and why.

The output of this skill is a set of labels and microcopy for every conversion point on a page, each matched to the reader's readiness at that point and to what the next screen really shows.

## How It Works

Start with the page's one job and the visitor's readiness. A visitor reading a pricing page is closer to a decision than one who has just landed from a social post. Early in a page, or for colder traffic, the primary action may be a low-commitment step such as seeing a demo or reading a guide. Later, or for warmer traffic, it can be a trial or a purchase. Asking for more commitment than the reader is ready for tends to produce hesitation, while asking for too little leaves ready buyers without a direct path.

Then write the label with the formula. The action verb should describe what the visitor does or receives, such as start, get, see, book or download. The object should name the specific thing: the trial, the report, the pricing for their team size, the demo. A qualifier can remove a doubt, as in "free" or "for my team." Several of Haines's examples use the first person, as in "Start My Free Trial," so the button reads like the visitor's own sentence. Whether first or second person works better on your page is worth testing.

Next, add microcopy where a doubt remains. A short line next to or under the button can answer the most common hesitation from your research: whether a card is required, how long setup takes, whether they can cancel. The line must be true. If a card is required, say so, because a promise the next screen breaks costs more than the click earned.

Check the label against the destination. NN/g's research on [Get Started buttons](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/get-started/) is at bottom a warning about mismatched expectations. Click the button yourself and confirm the next screen delivers exactly what the label promised. If it does not, change the label or change the flow.

Finally, organize the page's actions. One primary action per page keeps the page's job clear, and Haines's landing page guidance calls for a single message and a single call to action. Secondary actions, such as seeing pricing from a homepage, can stay visually quieter. Repeat the primary action where a reader might be ready: after the hero, after proof and at the end.

## Step-by-Step Guide

### Step 1: Map every conversion point on the page

List each button and link that asks for action, where it sits, and what screen it leads to. Mark which one serves the page's main job. Note the reader's likely readiness at each point, based on traffic source and how far into the page it appears. Remove actions that serve no one on this page.

### Step 2: Define what the visitor gets at each point

For each action, write in one plain sentence what the visitor receives or can do after clicking. Check it by clicking through yourself. If you cannot describe the outcome specifically, the destination may be unclear too, and that needs fixing before the label.

### Step 3: Write the label with a CTA copy formula

Combine an action verb with the specific thing the visitor gets, adding a qualifier if it removes a doubt, following the formula in Haines's [copywriting skill](https://github.com/coreyhaines31/marketingskills/blob/main/skills/copywriting/SKILL.md). Front-load the meaningful words, as the [GOV.UK link guidance](https://guidance.publishing.service.gov.uk/writing-to-gov-uk-standards/writing-guidelines/add-links/) recommends. Replace generic labels such as Submit, Learn More or Get Started. Keep the label short enough to read at a glance.

### Step 4: Match commitment to readiness

Compare what each label asks for with the reader's readiness at that point. Offer a lower-commitment step to colder readers and a direct step to warmer ones. On pages with mixed traffic, pair a primary action with one quieter secondary option. Keep the primary action the same wherever it repeats on the page.

### Step 5: Write microcopy for the remaining doubt

Pick the most common hesitation at this point from your customer research, such as cost, commitment or time. Answer it in a short line next to the button, and make sure the answer is true. Leave the microcopy out if there is no real doubt to answer.

### Step 6: Check the label against the destination

Click every action and confirm the next screen delivers what the label promised. Read each label aloud as a screen reader user would hear it, without the surrounding text, following NN/g's advice on [descriptive link labels](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/learn-more-links/). Fix any label that promises something different from what the visitor gets.

### Step 7: Test the primary label

When traffic allows, test the primary label against one alternative that changes one thing, such as the object named or the level of commitment. Decide the sample size and the metric before starting, as [Evan Miller](https://www.evanmiller.org/how-not-to-run-an-ab-test.html) recommends, and measure what happens after the click as well as the click itself. Record the result for future pages.

## Best Practices

- Name the outcome in the label. Haines's formula of verb plus what the visitor gets, from his [copywriting skill](https://github.com/coreyhaines31/marketingskills/blob/main/skills/copywriting/SKILL.md), keeps labels specific.
- Avoid ambiguous labels. NN/g found [Get Started](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/get-started/) ambiguous enough to send people into flows they did not expect.
- Make labels work out of context. Screen reader users and scanners may meet the label alone, so it should make sense without the paragraph above it.
- Answer the biggest doubt next to the button. A short, true line about cost, time or commitment can remove the last reason to hesitate.
- Keep one primary action per page. Secondary actions can exist, but the page's main job should be obvious from its buttons.
- Measure beyond the click. A label that raises clicks but lowers completed sign-ups is promising something the next screen does not deliver.

## Common Mistakes

- **Using generic labels**: Submit, Learn More and Click Here tell the reader nothing about what they get. Name the outcome.
- **Promising what the next screen does not deliver**: A "free" label that leads to a card form, or a demo label that leads to a sales form, breaks trust. Match the label to the destination.
- **Asking cold readers for a big commitment**: A purchase button in the first screen of a page for new visitors can feel premature. Offer a step that fits their readiness.
- **Giving every button equal weight**: Several competing primary actions make the page's job unclear. Choose one and make the others secondary.
- **Writing microcopy that is not true**: A reassurance like "no credit card required" must be accurate. A false reassurance costs more than the hesitation it removed.
