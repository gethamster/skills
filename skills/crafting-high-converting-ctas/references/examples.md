# Examples: Call-to-Action Copywriting: Writing High-Converting CTAs

## A trial button that hid a card form

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a bookkeeping app's landing page has a single "Get Started" button. Many visitors click it, but few finish sign-up, and session recordings show people leaving on the payment screen.

**Walkthrough:**

The writer clicks through and finds that the button leads to a trial sign-up that asks for a card on the second screen. The label promised nothing specific, and the card request surprised people.

The new label names the outcome, "Start my free trial," and the microcopy underneath states the truth: a card is required, and the trial can be cancelled before the first charge from the account settings. Clicks drop slightly, and completed sign-ups become the metric the team watches, since that is the page's real job.

## Two readiness levels on one page

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a consulting firm's services page gets both cold visitors from articles and warm visitors who were referred by clients. The only action is "Contact us."

**Walkthrough:**

The writer maps the page and sees that a cold reader in the first screen is asked for the same commitment as a warm reader at the bottom. Research notes show new visitors want to see how the firm works before talking to anyone.

The first screen gets a low-commitment primary action, "See how we run an engagement," which leads to a short page with the process and sample deliverables. The bottom of the page keeps a direct action for ready buyers, "Book a call with a partner," with a line saying the call is free and takes half an hour. Both labels describe exactly what follows.

## Generic links on a resource hub

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a software company's resource hub lists guides, each followed by a "Learn more" link. An accessibility review flags that screen reader users hear a list of identical links.

**Walkthrough:**

The writer rewrites each link to name its destination, putting the distinctive words first, such as "Onboarding checklist for new admins" in place of "Learn more." Where a card already has a descriptive title, the title itself becomes the link and the separate link is removed.

The change makes the list scannable for sighted users too, since each link now carries its own meaning. The team adds the rule to its style note so new cards follow it.
