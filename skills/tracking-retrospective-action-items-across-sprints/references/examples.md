# Examples: Tracking Retrospective Action Items Across Sprints

## Moving actions out of meeting notes

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a team of six writes its retrospective actions in a shared document. At the start of each retrospective, nobody remembers what was agreed, and most actions are not done.

**Walkthrough:**

The Scrum Master proposes a change. From now on, each action becomes a card on the team board, in the same column flow as product work, with a label marking it as an improvement and the owner's name on it. In sprint planning, the team sizes the improvement cards and includes them in the sprint.

At the next retrospective, the first agenda item is the improvement cards. One is done, one is halfway and blocked on another team. The team agrees to carry the blocked one forward with a new first step. Over the following sprints, fewer actions are left undone at review time.

## A recurring theme that actions did not fix

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a team of five keeps a simple log of retrospective actions and outcomes. Looking back over several sprints, the facilitator sees that "unclear requirements" has come up in most retrospectives.

**Walkthrough:**

The log shows three different actions aimed at the theme: a checklist, a refinement meeting and a template for stories. Each was completed, and the theme came back anyway. The facilitator raises this at the next retrospective as data.

The team spends the insight phase on the theme alone, using a 5 Whys. It finds that requirements change after the sprint starts because a stakeholder is not involved in refinement. The new action is to invite that stakeholder to refinement for the next two sprints and check whether mid-sprint changes drop.

## Dropping an action deliberately

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a team of seven has carried an action to "set up a shared test data environment" through several retrospectives. It is always postponed for product work.

**Walkthrough:**

At the review, the facilitator asks the team directly whether the action is still worth doing. The team agrees the problem is real but the action is too large for the time it has. They drop the action from the board and record why in the log.

In its place they choose a small first step: document the three datasets people use most, owned by one developer, finished within the sprint. The larger idea goes on the improvement backlog with a note on what it would take.
