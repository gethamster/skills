# FAQ: Building Opportunity Solution Trees

## How do I build an opportunity solution tree if I don't have enough customer interview data yet?

You can start a preliminary tree using whatever data you have, support tickets, NPS verbatims, sales call notes, behavioral analytics, but label the source of each opportunity and note your confidence level. A tree built on indirect data is better than no tree, but treat it as a hypothesis to be validated rather than a map of reality. Plan to conduct at least five to eight interviews focused on your tree's key opportunities within the next two to three weeks, and update the tree aggressively as firsthand insights come in. Expect the tree to change substantially once real interview data arrives.

## Should I build an opportunity solution tree before or after defining product outcomes?

After. The outcome is the root of the tree, without it, you have no anchor for what's relevant. If your team hasn't yet agreed on a desired outcome, start with [defining product outcomes over outputs](https://tryhamster.com/skills/defining-product-outcomes-over-outputs) first. Attempting to build the tree without a clear outcome leads to a sprawling map of everything customers mentioned, with no way to prioritize.

The outcome is the filter that makes the tree useful rather than overwhelming.

## How many opportunity solution trees should a product team maintain simultaneously?

Ideally one per active desired outcome, and most teams should only be pursuing one or two outcomes at a time. If you're maintaining three or more trees, you're likely spread too thin to make meaningful progress on any of them. A single, well-maintained tree with active assumption testing will drive more impact than multiple neglected trees. If your team has been assigned multiple outcomes, negotiate with leadership to sequence them rather than parallelize.

## What tool should I use to build and maintain the tree?

The best tool is the one your team will actually look at daily. Physical walls with sticky notes work exceptionally well for co-located teams because they're always visible. For distributed teams, Miro and FigJam are the most common choices, they support sticky notes, connectors, and color-coding. Some teams use dedicated tools like Productboard or Vistaly that have built-in opportunity solution tree features.

Avoid tools that hide the tree behind multiple clicks (Confluence, Google Docs), if it takes more than one click to see the tree, the team won't reference it spontaneously.

## How do I handle stakeholder feature requests that don't fit the tree?

This is one of the tree's most powerful functions. When a stakeholder requests a feature, walk them to the tree (literally or virtually) and ask: 'Which opportunity does this address?' If they can point to one, add the request as a candidate solution under that opportunity, it now competes on equal footing with other solutions. If they can't connect it to an opportunity, you have two productive paths: either the request reveals a customer need you haven't captured (add the opportunity, then investigate it in interviews), or it's a solution without a validated problem (park it and revisit if evidence emerges).

Either way, the tree turns a political negotiation into a structured conversation.

## Why does my opportunity solution tree keep getting too large to be useful?

Tree bloat happens when the team adds opportunities without pruning. Every week, apply two pruning rules: first, remove opportunities that you've learned (through interviews or data) don't meaningfully influence the desired outcome. Second, archive opportunity branches you've decided not to pursue in the current cycle, move them to a 'parking lot' section rather than keeping them on the active tree. For example, a healthy active tree for a single team typically has 8 to 15 leaf-level opportunities, 2 to 4 solutions per focused opportunity, and a clear visual hierarchy.

For example, if your tree exceeds roughly 50 total nodes, it's time for aggressive pruning.

## Can I use an opportunity solution tree for platform or infrastructure work that isn't customer-facing?

Yes, but you need to reframe who the 'customer' is. For platform teams, your customers are internal developers or other teams. For infrastructure, your customers are the engineers relying on your systems. For example, the tree works the same way: define an outcome (reduce deploy failures by 50%), map the opportunities your internal customers face (unclear rollback procedures, flaky integration tests, no staging environment parity), generate solutions, and test assumptions.

The framework is about connecting solutions to validated needs through structured exploration, that applies whether the end user is external or internal.
