## Overview

A 7 Ps marketing mix analysis, often called a 7 Ps marketing audit or 7 Ps evaluation, is a structured review of an existing offer across all seven elements of the [7 P's Marketing Mix](../../methods/7-p-s-marketing-mix/METHOD.md). It asks two questions of each P: what did we decide, and what does the customer actually experience? Then it asks a third question of the whole mix: where do the seven decisions contradict each other?

The practice borrows from the marketing audit. Kotler, Gregor and Rodgers discuss the marketing audit as the marketing counterpart of the public accounting audit and note that the idea dates back to the early 1950s ([The Marketing Audit Comes of Age](https://sloanreview.mit.edu/article/the-marketing-audit-comes-of-age/)). Their case for it still holds: strategies go stale as competitors launch, customers switch, distributors lose effectiveness and costs rise, and an annual plan rarely stops to question the strategy underneath it.

A 7 Ps audit narrows that broad review to the marketing mix. It is most useful when growth has slowed and teams disagree about the cause, before a price change or repositioning, and on a regular schedule so drift is caught early. The three service Ps matter most here, because People, Process and Physical Evidence usually sit outside marketing's reporting line and are the least likely to have been reviewed.

The output is short: one scorecard, one list of contradictions, and a handful of fixes with owners and dates. The audit is done when the team agrees on the two or three changes that would most improve what the customer experiences, and someone owns each one.

## How It Works

The audit works from evidence rather than opinion. For each P, the team writes the current decision in one or two sentences, then gathers what customers and frontline staff say about it. Useful inputs include win and loss notes from sales, support tickets, reviews, churn interviews, channel data and competitor offers.

Service quality needs its own measure, because People, Process and Physical Evidence show up in how customers rate the service. The SERVQUAL instrument compares what customers expect with what they perceive across five dimensions: reliability, assurance, tangibles, empathy and responsiveness ([Wikipedia: SERVQUAL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SERVQUAL)). The gap between expectation and perception is the measure, which suits an audit because it shows where the service falls short. SERVQUAL does not map its dimensions to the 7 Ps, but a practical working mapping is tangibles to Physical Evidence, reliability and responsiveness to Process, and assurance and empathy to People.

Scoring uses a simple scale, such as strong, adequate and weak, with a written definition for each P. The definitions matter more than the scale. Without them, scores drift toward optimism and one audit cannot be compared with the next.

Contradictions are the heart of the audit. The mix only works when the elements support each other, and Goi's review of the marketing mix literature stresses that a decision on one element cannot be made without considering its impact on the others ([Goi, A Review of Marketing Mix](https://ccsenet.org/journal/index.php/ijms/article/download/97/1552)). A contradiction is any pair of Ps that send different messages: a premium price with a slow, impersonal onboarding, or a promise of expertise with junior staff on every call. The team lists each one as a pair and describes what the customer sees.

Constantinides found that the marketing mix tends toward an internal orientation and lacks personalisation ([The Marketing Mix Revisited](https://research.utwente.nl/en/publications/3269e512-4186-47cc-9d05-e052ad573f17)). The audit guards against that by writing each finding in the customer's words wherever possible, and by including at least one input that comes directly from customers for every P.

Finally, the team ranks findings by how much they hurt the customer and how hard they are to fix. The top few become actions. The rest go into a backlog that the next audit reviews.

## Step-by-Step Guide

### Step 1: Set scope, owners and inputs

Decide which offer and which customer segment the audit covers; mixing segments hides problems. Name a facilitator and a small group that includes marketing, sales, service delivery and someone who owns pricing. List the inputs you will gather for each P and who will gather them. Set the date for the review meeting before you start collecting.

### Step 2: Write the current decision for each P

For each of the seven Ps, write what the business has decided today in plain language. Product is the core service and what surrounds it. Price is the level, model and payment terms. Place is where customers find, buy and get access. Promotion is the main message and channels. People, Process and Physical Evidence describe who serves the customer, the steps they go through and the tangible cues they see. If nobody can say what was decided for a P, record that as the first finding.

### Step 3: Gather evidence from customers and staff

Collect the inputs you listed: feedback, reviews, support data, sales notes and competitor offers. Run a short service quality survey if you have none, using the expectation and perception structure of SERVQUAL ([Wikipedia: SERVQUAL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SERVQUAL)). Interview a few frontline staff, because they see process failures first. Walk through the customer journey yourself, noting every tangible cue along the way.

### Step 4: Score each P in the marketing mix analysis

In the review, take the Ps one at a time and agree a score using definitions written in advance. Where people disagree, record both views and the evidence each relies on. Mark a P as unknown when evidence is missing instead of guessing. Compare with the last audit if there is one and note the direction of change.

### Step 5: List the contradictions between Ps

Go through the Ps in pairs, starting with Price against People, Process and Physical Evidence, since price sets the expectation the others must meet. Write each contradiction as what the customer experiences. Check Promotion against Process too: a promise the process cannot keep turns into a service failure. Goi's summary that no element can be decided in isolation ([Goi](https://ccsenet.org/journal/index.php/ijms/article/download/97/1552)) is the test for every pair.

### Step 6: Rank findings and assign owners

Rank weak scores and contradictions by customer impact and by effort. Pick the two or three that would change the customer experience most. Give each an owner, a date and the evidence that should move if the fix works. Put the rest in a backlog.

### Step 7: Record the audit and schedule the next one

Store the scorecard, the contradictions, the actions and the inputs with the date. Schedule the next audit, and list the events that should trigger an earlier one: a price change, a new channel, a new competitor or a drop in service quality scores. Open the next audit by checking whether the actions happened and whether the evidence moved.

## Best Practices

- Audit one segment at a time. Different customers experience different mixes, and averaging them hides the contradictions the audit is meant to find.
- Write scoring definitions before the first audit. Without them, scores reflect mood and cannot be compared over time.
- Give People, Process and Physical Evidence as much time as the first four. They are the service Ps that [Booms and Bitner added](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marketing_mix) and they are often nobody's job in marketing.
- Bring frontline staff into the evidence gathering. They hear complaints and see workarounds that never reach a dashboard.
- Treat missing evidence as a finding. A P scored from opinion should say so, and gathering the evidence becomes an action.
- Keep the output short. A one-page scorecard and a short action list get acted on; a long report gets filed.

## Common Mistakes

- **Auditing only the 4 Ps**: Teams often review product, price, channels and campaigns and skip the service Ps. Most service contradictions sit in People, Process and Physical Evidence, so review all seven every time.
- **Scoring from internal opinion**: A score that does not point at customer or staff evidence gives false comfort. Require at least one external input per P, or mark it unknown.
- **Listing problems with no owners**: An audit that ends with observations changes nothing. Every action needs a name, a date and the evidence that should move.
- **Fixing Ps one at a time in separate teams**: Each team optimizing its own P can create new contradictions. Review proposed fixes against the other six Ps before committing.
- **Running the audit once**: Markets move and a mix that fit last year drifts. Keep a schedule and add event triggers.
