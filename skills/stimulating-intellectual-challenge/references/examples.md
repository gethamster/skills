# Examples: Stimulating Intellectual Challenge and Creative Problem-Solving

## Rethinking a weekly status meeting

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: A team lead runs a 60-minute weekly status meeting for a team of 8 people that everyone considers slow, but nobody has proposed changing it.

**Walkthrough:**

The lead writes a problem statement: the meeting takes 8 person-hours a week and decisions still happen in side conversations. The future state is that every blocker gets an owner within one day, and the constraint is that the client update still goes out on Friday. In the assumption session, the team realizes the belief that everyone must attend live is inherited, not evidenced. Two alternatives emerge: written async updates, or a 15-minute blockers-only call.

The team runs a two-week experiment with the async option, owned by one engineer, with the decision rule that it stays if all blockers get owners within a day. The review shows one blocker slipped, so the team keeps async updates and adds a 15-minute call only when a blocker is flagged.

## Handling dissent on a product roadmap

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: A junior designer questions a roadmap priority in a planning session, and a senior colleague dismisses the point as naive in front of the group.

**Walkthrough:**

The manager redirects the discussion to the idea by asking what evidence supports the current priority and what evidence the designer has. The group finds the priority rests on a customer request from a year earlier that nobody has revalidated. The manager asks for one alternative explanation of what customers need now before anyone decides. After the meeting, the manager speaks privately with the senior colleague about challenging ideas without labeling people.

The team schedules three customer calls over one week as a bounded test, with the rule that the priority stays unless at least two customers describe a different need. At the review, the designer gets explicit credit for raising the question, regardless of the outcome.

## Recognizing a failed experiment

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: A support team tests a new triage script for 3 weeks, hoping to cut average ticket handling time from 20 minutes to 15 minutes, and the time does not improve.

**Walkthrough:**

The owner reports honestly that handling time stayed at 20 minutes and that the script added confusion for two ticket types. Because the decision rule was written in advance, the team drops the script without debate. In the learning review, the lead asks what the test revealed and the team learns that the slow tickets are the two confusing types, not the triage step. The lead publicly thanks the owner for clear data and quick reporting.

That new insight becomes the next assumption to test. Meanwhile, the team's service commitments were tracked weekly throughout and never slipped.
