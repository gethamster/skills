# Promotion Strategy in the Marketing Mix: Integrated Plans

> Build a promotion strategy in the marketing mix: an integrated promotion plan that coordinates advertising, content, PR and sales around one message.

## Overview

Promotion is the P that tells customers the offer exists and persuades them to look closer. Wikipedia's summary of the marketing mix defines it as "the marketing communication used to make the offer known to potential customers and persuade them to investigate it further," with advertising, public relations, direct selling and sales promotions as its main elements ([Wikipedia: Marketing mix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marketing_mix)). In the [7 P's Marketing Mix](../../methods/7-p-s-marketing-mix/METHOD.md), a promotion strategy has to fit the product, price and place, and it has to promise only what the service Ps can deliver.

Borden's original list split this territory into separate elements: personal selling, advertising, promotions and display, each with its own policies ([Borden, The Concept of the Marketing Mix](https://www.guillaumenicaise.com/wp-content/uploads/2013/10/Borden-1984_The-concept-of-marketing-mix.pdf)). That split still shows up in many organizations, where each tool has its own team, budget and message. The result is a customer who hears different things from the ad, the website and the salesperson.

An integrated promotion plan fixes that. [Wikipedia's article on integrated marketing communications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrated_marketing_communications) defines IMC as "the use of marketing strategies to optimize the communication of a consistent message of the company's brands to stakeholders," and reports general consensus that it should be viewed as a planning process. This skill turns that idea into a plan: one message, a deliberate channel mix, a shared calendar and shared measures.

It covers the 7 P's promotion strategy from message to measurement. Use it before a launch, when campaigns in different channels are drifting apart, or when a 7 Ps audit shows that promotion promises more than the service delivers.

## How It Works

The skill works from message to channels to calendar to measurement, and it checks the plan against delivery at the end.

The message comes first. A promotional mix strategy starts with the one or two things the target audience should believe about the offer, taken from the product strategy and positioning. Every channel then expresses that message in its own format. The Wikipedia article on IMC says that a coordinated set of communications makes a brand more credible because it is seen as a whole "rather than a mixture of different messages being sent out" ([Wikipedia: Integrated marketing communications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrated_marketing_communications)).

Channel choice follows the audience and the stage of the buying decision. Advertising and PR build awareness. Content and search help people who are evaluating. Direct selling and sales promotions help close. For a service, reviews, referrals and case stories deserve a planned place, because the services literature argues that service buyers are more likely to search for information before they buy, driven by perceived purchase risk ([Wikipedia: Services marketing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Services_marketing)).

For services, promotion has one more job: it sets expectations. The gaps model of service quality defines quality as the gap between what customers expect and what they perceive ([Wikipedia: Services marketing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Services_marketing)). What a business says in its promotion is one input to those expectations. A campaign that overpromises raises the bar the service is judged against, so a stronger promise can produce lower perceived quality. Shostack made a related point from the design side: a service blueprint gives promotion tangible evidence it can manage and control ([Shostack, Designing Services That Deliver](https://strategicdesignthinking.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2012/11/hbr-shostackpdf.pdf)).

A shared calendar coordinates timing so channels reinforce each other: the PR story, the ad campaign and the sales outreach run in the same weeks. Shared measures keep teams from optimizing their own channel at the expense of the whole plan.

## Step-by-Step Guide

### Step 1: Audit current promotional activity

List everything currently running: ads, content, PR, email, events, sales materials and promotions. For each, note the message it carries, the audience, the cost and the results you can see. Look for contradictions between channels and for audiences no channel reaches.

### Step 2: Set objectives for the plan

Write what the promotion must achieve, tied to business goals: awareness in a segment, qualified enquiries, trials or renewals. Decide how you will measure each objective before choosing channels. Keep the list short so the plan stays focused.

### Step 3: Write the core message

Write the one or two claims every channel will carry, drawn from the product strategy. Add the proof points behind each claim. Check each claim with whoever owns delivery: can the People, Process and Physical Evidence back it up for every customer?

### Step 4: Choose the promotional mix

Choose the channels that reach the audience at each stage of the decision: awareness, evaluation and purchase. Use the elements of the [promotional mix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrated_marketing_communications) that fit the audience and budget, and drop those that do not. For a service, include reviews, referrals and case stories, since buyers look for evidence from others.

### Step 5: Build a shared calendar

Put all channels on one calendar so launches, stories and outreach reinforce each other. Assign an owner for each activity. Plan the handoffs, for example from a campaign to the sales follow-up, so the customer hears a consistent story.

### Step 6: Create shared assets and guidelines

Produce the core assets once and adapt them per channel: key messages, visuals, proof points and examples. Write short guidelines so partners and sales staff use the same language. Update them when the offer or positioning changes.

### Step 7: Measure, review and adjust

Track results against the objectives, per channel and for the plan as a whole. Review regularly, move budget toward what works, and retire what does not. Watch complaints and service scores too, since a campaign that raises expectations too far shows up there before it shows up in sales.

## Best Practices

- Write the message before choosing channels. A clear message lets each channel adapt the format without changing what is said.
- Promise what the service reliably delivers. For services, [expectations set by promotion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Services_marketing) become the standard customers judge quality against.
- Keep one calendar for all channels. Separate calendars lead to campaigns that compete for attention instead of reinforcing each other.
- Involve sales and service in the plan. They repeat the message to customers every day and hear which claims land and which do not.
- Measure the plan as a whole as well as by channel. Channel metrics alone push each team to optimize its own piece.

## Common Mistakes

- **Running channels as separate campaigns**: Different messages in different channels confuse customers. Agree one core message and adapt only the format.
- **Overpromising to win attention**: A promise the service cannot keep turns into a quality problem. Check every claim with the people who deliver it.
- **Choosing channels by habit**: Channels picked because they were used last year may no longer reach the audience. Choose by audience and stage of decision.
- **Leaving sales out of the plan**: If sales materials and conversations tell a different story, the campaign is undone at the moment of purchase. Brief sales before launch.
- **Measuring only reach and clicks**: Activity metrics do not show whether promotion brought the right customers. Tie measures to enquiries, conversions and retention.
