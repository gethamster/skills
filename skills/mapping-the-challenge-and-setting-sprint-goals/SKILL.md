# Design Sprint Problem Mapping: Framing Monday Right

> Frame a sprint's Monday: agree a long-term goal, turn risks into sprint questions, map the customer journey and pick one target.

## At a Glance

| Field | Value |
|-------|-------|
| Difficulty | Intermediate |
| Time to Learn | Half a day of preparation plus one full sprint day |
| Outcome | A written long-term goal, a short list of sprint questions, a problem map annotated with How Might We notes, and one chosen target that every sketch on Tuesday will address. |
| Prerequisites | A committed sprint team and a person with authority to make the final call, Existing customer research, data or support history on the problem, A room or shared board where the map and notes can stay visible all week, Basic familiarity with the five-day sprint structure |
| Part of | [GV Design Sprint](../../methods/gv-design-sprint/METHOD.md) |

## Overview

Problem mapping is the Monday work of a design sprint: the team turns a broad challenge into a goal, a set of answerable questions and a shared picture of the customer journey. For the method's history and the full five-day structure, see the [GV Design Sprint method page](https://tryhamster.com/methods/gv-design-sprint). This page covers only how to do the framing well.

In Jake Knapp's original write-up, the first day exists to [dig into the design problem through research, competitive review and strategy exercises](https://jakek.medium.com/the-product-design-sprint-a-five-day-recipe-for-startups-84fde3e97d79), and the later GV sequence labels the same day [Understand, ahead of Sketch, Decide, Prototype and Test](https://zapier.com/blog/google-ventures-design-sprint). Everything after Monday depends on it. The sketches on Tuesday draw directly on Monday's artifacts: summaries of the book describe sketchers starting by [copying down details from the flowchart, the goal, the questions and the inspirational examples](https://shortform.com/pdf/sprint-pdf-jake-knapp-john-zeratsky-and-braden-kowitz). If those artifacts are vague, the sketches will be vague too.

The skill matters because a sprint is good at solving and poor at discovering. A recent review of the method notes that it is [primarily a solving and de-risking tool, not a problem-finding tool](https://geyfman.org/dossiers/gv-design-sprint-deep-research-report), so weak research or a badly framed problem leads the team to optimize an answer to the wrong question. The same review quotes practitioners saying sprints are [not worth the effort if you don't have enough data on the problem to solve](https://geyfman.org/dossiers/gv-design-sprint-deep-research-report). Mapping is where you either catch that gap or bake it in.

The work produces four concrete outputs. First, a single long-term goal written as an optimistic outcome the team wants in the future. Second, a short list of sprint questions that name what could stop you reaching that goal and that Friday's test can speak to. Third, a map: a simple left-to-right diagram of how the key customers move from first encounter to the goal. Fourth, a target, meaning one customer and one moment on the map that the rest of the week will focus on.

The skill is mostly facilitation and editing. You are not generating solutions on Monday. You are deciding what problem the sprint will attack, deliberately narrowing a big challenge to something a team can sketch, prototype and test in the days that follow. A good Monday ends with a room that agrees on where to aim, even if nobody yet knows what they will build.

## How It Works

The Monday sequence is a funnel. It starts wide, with everything the team and outside experts know, and ends narrow, with one target. Each artifact constrains the next.

It begins before the sprint. Knapp's recipe opens with a preparation stage to [get the people and things you need](https://jakek.medium.com/the-product-design-sprint-a-five-day-recipe-for-startups-84fde3e97d79), and the most useful thing to gather is evidence about the problem: research findings, analytics, support tickets, sales objections. The practitioner warning that sprints fail [without enough data on the problem](https://geyfman.org/dossiers/gv-design-sprint-deep-research-report) is a prompt to check sufficiency before you book the room, not on Monday morning.

The long-term goal comes first on the day because it gives every later choice a reference point. Asking where the team wants to be well after the sprint forces people to state their optimism out loud, which surfaces disagreements early. The sprint questions are the pessimistic mirror of that goal: assume the effort failed a year from now and ask what caused it. Each cause becomes a question phrased so a prototype test could shed light on it, for example "Will first-time visitors trust us enough to enter payment details?" Questions keep Friday honest, because the test plan is written against them.

The map translates goal and questions into a customer journey. Actors go on the left, the goal or end state on the right, and a handful of boxes and arrows sit between them showing the path. Keeping it simple is the point. A map that shows every edge case cannot be used to pick a target. The GV framing of Monday bundles [research, competitive review, strategy exercises, expert interviews and problem mapping](https://zapier.com/blog/google-ventures-design-sprint) into the same day because the map is meant to be corrected by what the experts say.

Expert interviews feed the map through How Might We notes. Book summaries describe each team member keeping their own sticky notes, [writing the letters HMW in the top corner, and converting anything interesting into a question](https://paulminors.com/blog/sprint-jake-knapp-book-summary-pdf) while the expert talks. Reframing observations as opportunities stops the notes from becoming a list of complaints and gives Tuesday's sketchers prompts they can respond to. After the interviews, the team clusters the notes by theme, votes on the most useful ones and places them on the map where they apply.

The last move is choosing the target. Dots and HMW notes will cluster around a few spots on the map; the person with final authority picks one customer and one event to focus on, guided by the sprint questions. This is a real trade-off: the target is where the biggest risk or opportunity sits, and everything outside it is parked for later.

The main weakness is time. Research on sprint contests notes that tight time constraints can [shorten user interaction and reduce opportunities for deep understanding](https://cris.fbk.eu/retrieve/883a8fe4-957f-44fa-af9e-6159cfa53bd4/1-s2.0-S0166497225000719-main.pdf) before a solution is chosen. Monday cannot replace discovery research. It can only organize and sharpen what the team already knows, which is why the preparation check matters as much as the day itself.

## Step-by-Step Guide

### Step 1: Check data sufficiency before booking the sprint

A week or two ahead, list what you already know about the problem: research findings, analytics, support themes, sales feedback and prior experiments. For each, note whether it describes real customer behaviour or internal opinion. If the list is mostly opinion, the sprint risks polishing a guess, which is exactly the failure practitioners describe when they call sprints [not worth the effort without enough data on the problem](https://geyfman.org/dossiers/gv-design-sprint-deep-research-report). In that case, run a short round of discovery interviews or pull the data first, then sprint.

> **Pro tip:** Write a one-page brief of the evidence and circulate it to the team before Monday so nobody spends the morning catching up.

### Step 2: Set the long-term goal

Ask the team where they want this product or service to be well after the sprint ends, and why they are doing the project at all. Collect answers, discuss briefly, then write one goal sentence on the board in plain language. The goal should be optimistic and outcome focused, not a feature list. If the room cannot agree, the person with final authority chooses, and the disagreement itself is useful input for the sprint questions.

> **Pro tip:** Test the goal by asking whether a customer would care if you reached it. If only the company benefits, rewrite it.

### Step 3: Turn risks into sprint questions

Ask everyone to imagine it is well into the future and the goal was missed, then write down what went wrong. Rewrite each cause as a question a prototype test could inform, such as "Can customers understand the pricing without a sales call?" Keep the questions that threaten the goal most and that Friday's test could plausibly address. Drop questions that only a large launch or long-term data could answer.

> **Pro tip:** Aim for a short list, for example three to five questions, so the test script can cover every one.

### Step 4: Draw the first version of the map

On a whiteboard, list the key customers and other actors on the left and write the goal or end state on the right. Between them, sketch the few steps a customer takes from discovering you to reaching that end state, connected by arrows. Use words and boxes, not screens or detailed flows. The map should fit on one board and be readable from across the room.

> **Pro tip:** If the map needs more than a handful of steps per actor, you are describing edge cases. Collapse them into one box.

### Step 5: Interview experts and capture How Might We notes

Bring in people who know the problem from different angles: someone who talks to customers, someone who knows the technology, someone who owns the business numbers. Ask each to walk through the map and correct it, and let the team ask questions. While they speak, every participant [writes HMW in the corner of their own sticky notes and turns anything interesting into a question](https://paulminors.com/blog/sprint-jake-knapp-book-summary-pdf). Update the map live whenever an expert reveals a missing step or actor.

> **Pro tip:** Keep interviews short and ask each expert, at the end, what they would worry about if they were running the sprint.

### Step 6: Organize and vote on the How Might We notes

Stick all HMW notes on a wall and have the team group them by theme in silence, labelling each cluster. Give everyone a small number of dot votes to place on the notes they think matter most. Move the top-voted notes onto the map, next to the step they relate to. The resulting clusters show where the team's collective attention lands without anyone arguing for it.

### Step 7: Choose the target

Look at the map with the voted HMW notes on it and reread the sprint questions. The person with final authority picks one target customer and one event on the map where the week's solutions will focus, usually where risk and opportunity cluster. Circle it on the map and write it next to the goal. Everything outside the target is recorded and parked, not deleted.

The same decision pattern is used on Wednesday, described in [making structured decisions under time constraints](https://tryhamster.com/skills/making-structured-decisions-under-time-constraints).

> **Pro tip:** Ask the decider to say out loud which sprint question the target addresses. If none, the target or the questions need revisiting.

### Step 8: Hand the artifacts to Tuesday

Leave the goal, sprint questions, map, HMW wall and target visible where the team will sketch. Photograph everything in case the room changes. Tuesday's sketching starts from these artifacts, with each person reviewing them and noting useful details, as described in [sketching competing solutions individually](https://tryhamster.com/skills/sketching-competing-solutions-individually). A quick end-of-day recap confirms everyone can state the target in one sentence.

> **Pro tip:** Ask each person to write the target on a card before leaving. Mismatched cards mean the framing is not yet shared.

## Best Practices

- Treat preparation as part of the skill. The warning that sprints are [not worth the effort without enough data](https://geyfman.org/dossiers/gv-design-sprint-deep-research-report) means the evidence check belongs before Monday, when there is still time to fill gaps.
- Write the goal and questions in customer language. Internal jargon hides disagreement, and plain words make it obvious when two people mean different things by the same goal.
- Keep the map deliberately crude. Its job is to let the team point at one step and say "here"; a detailed flow diagram makes that impossible and invites debate about details that do not matter yet.
- Let experts correct the map live rather than presenting it as finished. The GV framing puts [expert interviews and problem mapping](https://zapier.com/blog/google-ventures-design-sprint) in the same day because each improves the other.
- Capture observations as How Might We questions, not complaints. The reframing turns problems into prompts that Tuesday's sketchers can answer directly.
- Make the target a decision, not a consensus. One person choosing keeps the day on schedule, and the team's votes still inform the call.
- Link every sprint question to the test plan. If a question cannot be addressed by showing a prototype to customers, move it to a follow-up list instead of pretending the sprint will answer it.

## Common Mistakes

- **Using the sprint to discover what the problem is.** — A sprint is [a solving and de-risking tool rather than a problem-finding tool](https://geyfman.org/dossiers/gv-design-sprint-deep-research-report). If you do not know which problem matters, run discovery research first and sprint once you can name a target.
- **Writing a long-term goal that is really a feature or output, such as "launch the new dashboard".** — Rewrite it as the outcome the feature is supposed to create for customers. An output goal leads the team to prototype the feature it already assumed rather than exploring alternatives.
- **Keeping every risk as a sprint question.** — Prune to the questions a prototype test can inform. Questions about long-term retention or market size cannot be settled in a day of testing, and leaving them in sets up a false sense of validation.
- **Drawing an exhaustive map with every branch and edge case.** — Collapse detail into a few steps per actor. If the team cannot point to a single target on the map in seconds, it is too complicated to guide sketching.
- **Taking notes as raw observations during expert interviews.** — Convert each observation into a How Might We question as it is heard, the way summaries describe [writing HMW on each note](https://paulminors.com/blog/sprint-jake-knapp-book-summary-pdf). Raw notes pile up and are hard to cluster, vote on or sketch against.
- **Ending Monday without an explicit target.** — Have the decider circle one customer and one moment on the map before the day closes. Without it, Tuesday's sketches scatter across the whole journey and cannot be compared on Wednesday.
