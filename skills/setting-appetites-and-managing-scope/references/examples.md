# Examples: Setting Appetites and Cutting Scope

## Example: Small B2B SaaS team scoping an invoicing improvement

**Scenario:**

A 15-person B2B SaaS company has a shaped pitch for improving their invoicing feature. Customers have complained that creating recurring invoices requires too many manual steps. The team has one designer and two developers available for the next cycle. The company is pre-Series A and cannot afford to spend more than a few weeks on any single feature.

**Walkthrough:**

" She assesses the strategic value as medium, since 40% of paying customers use recurring invoices and churn data shows invoicing friction as the third most cited reason for leaving. She declares a three-week appetite. The shaped solution includes: save invoice as template, auto-populate from template, edit template, schedule recurring send, email notification on send, and invoice analytics dashboard. She classifies save-as-template, auto-populate, and edit-template as must-haves because without them the core problem (re-entering data) is not solved.

Schedule recurring send is a nice-to-have ranked first because it adds significant value. Email notification and analytics dashboard are nice-to-haves ranked second and third. She pressure-tests: three must-haves in three weeks with two developers feels feasible with buffer. The pitch goes to the betting table with the appetite and both lists documented.

The team ships all three must-haves in two and a half weeks, pulls in schedule recurring send in the remaining time, and skips email notification and analytics.

## Example: Large enterprise team setting appetite for a platform migration

**Scenario:**

A 200-person enterprise company needs to migrate their notification system from email-only to a multi-channel system supporting email, in-app, and SMS. The project has been discussed for a year but never started because estimates ranged from three to nine months. The VP of Product wants to apply Shape Up principles to break the logjam. Two full teams (each with a designer and three developers) could be allocated.

**Walkthrough:**

" She assesses the strategic value as large based on support ticket data showing 200+ tickets per month about missed notifications and a direct correlation with late payments. She declares a six-week appetite for the first cycle, acknowledging that multi-channel notification is a multi-cycle effort but wanting to ship value in the first cycle. The team lists 22 elements across the full vision. She classifies only five as must-haves: in-app notification center, real-time delivery of payment-related alerts in-app, user preference toggle (email vs.

in-app), fallback to email if in-app is unread after 24 hours, and a notification read/unread state. SMS support, notification grouping, quiet hours, digest mode, and admin analytics are all nice-to-haves. She pressure-tests: five must-haves for one team in six weeks feels feasible. She assigns one team instead of two, holding the second team for other bets.

The pitch is approved. The team ships the five must-haves and pulls in notification grouping. SMS becomes a separate pitch for a future cycle.

## Example: Consumer mobile app team with a two-week small batch

**Scenario:**

A consumer fitness app wants to add a "share workout summary" feature so users can post their completed workouts to social media. The product manager has heard the request from dozens of users but believes it is a small enhancement, not a major project. The team has a one-designer, one-developer pair available for small batch work alongside two other small batches in the same cycle.

**Walkthrough:**

" He assesses the strategic value as small, since sharing drives organic acquisition but is not a core retention feature. He declares a two-week appetite. The shaped solution includes: generate shareable image card, customize card color theme, add personal record badges to card, share to Instagram Stories, share to Twitter, share to generic share sheet, and preview before sharing. He classifies generate image card and share via generic OS share sheet as must-haves.

The generic share sheet covers Instagram, Twitter, and every other app without building platform-specific integrations, so platform-specific sharing becomes unnecessary. Customize color theme, personal record badges, and preview are nice-to-haves. Two must-haves in two weeks for one developer is comfortably feasible. The team ships both must-haves in eight days and adds the preview screen in the remaining two days.

## Example: B2C e-commerce team deciding between appetites

**Scenario:**

An e-commerce company selling custom furniture wants to add a room visualization tool where customers upload a photo of their room and see how a piece of furniture would look in it. The CTO thinks it needs 12 weeks. The CEO thinks it could be done in three weeks with a simpler approach. They disagree on the appetite because they are imagining different solutions.

**Walkthrough:**

" She assesses the strategic value as large, since conversion rate on items over $1,500 is 40% lower than on items under $500, and customer interviews cite visualization uncertainty as the top reason. She proposes two versions. Version A (six-week appetite): upload a room photo, the system uses basic image compositing to overlay the furniture at approximate scale, the customer can drag to reposition. Must-haves are photo upload, static overlay of furniture image, and basic repositioning.

Nice-to-haves are perspective correction, shadow rendering, and multi-item placement. Version B (two-week appetite): instead of photo upload, show the furniture in three pre-built room templates (modern living room, traditional den, minimalist bedroom). Must-haves are three room templates and furniture swap within template. Nice-to-haves are additional templates and color variant previews.

The team bets on Version A with the six-week appetite because the value warrants the investment and the photo upload is the key differentiator. The pitch documents both versions so if Version A fails to come together, the team can pivot to Version B's approach as a scope cut.
