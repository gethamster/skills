# FAQ: Making Pivot-or-Persevere Decisions

## What is the difference between a pivot and an iteration?

An iteration improves execution within the current strategy, such as refining onboarding or fixing a confusing flow. A pivot changes a fundamental part of the strategy, like the target customer, the problem, the channel, or the revenue model. Iterations happen inside a persevere decision. If your planned change still depends on the hypothesis that just failed, you are iterating, not pivoting.

## How do I set a pivot threshold when I have no historical data?

Start from what the business needs to be viable, then work backward to the behavior that would make that plausible. For example, if the model needs a certain share of trials to convert, set the success threshold near that level and the trigger well below it. Accept that early thresholds are rough. Recording them in advance still beats having none, and your decision log will show how to calibrate them.

## What should I do when stakeholders disagree with the pre-set criteria after results come in?

Hold the current decision to the criteria that were agreed before launch, and record the disagreement. If the objection reveals a real flaw in the metric, fix it for the next test rather than rewriting this one. Changing the rules after seeing the data is exactly the rationalization the criteria exist to prevent. It helps to get stakeholder sign-off on the criteria before the experiment starts.

## How many times should I retest an inconclusive result?

Set a cap in advance, for example one or two retests per hypothesis. Each retest must change something concrete, such as sample size, segment definition, or instrumentation, or it will just reproduce the same ambiguity. If results remain inconclusive after the cap, treat that as a lack of support and consider pivoting. Endless retesting is a slow way of avoiding the decision.

## Is a pivot a sign that the team failed?

No. A pivot based on evidence means the loop worked: the team learned something true about customers and acted on it cheaply. Research comparing planning and lean activities found that pivoting based on customer feedback correlated with performance, though that does not prove causation. The real failure is persevering on a direction the evidence has already contradicted.
