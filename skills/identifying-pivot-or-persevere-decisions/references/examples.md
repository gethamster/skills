# Examples: Making Pivot-or-Persevere Decisions

## Clear contradiction on a B2B scheduling tool

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a team hypothesized that clinic managers would pay for automated staff scheduling. They set a success threshold of 15% of trial clinics converting to paid and a pivot trigger at 5%.

**Walkthrough:**

After the agreed four-week window, 3% of trial clinics converted, below the pivot trigger. The founder argued that the pricing page was confusing, but that explanation had not been flagged as a risk before launch, so the team treated it as a hypothesis for later rather than a reason to discount the result. Usage logs showed that clinic managers rarely logged in, while shift leads used the tool daily. The team recorded a contradicting outcome and pivoted the customer segment from managers to shift leads.

The new hypothesis and a fresh set of thresholds went into the next cycle within the week.

## Inconclusive result on a consumer habit feature

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a fitness app tested a streak feature, expecting it to lift week-two retention. The team set success at 40% week-two retention and a pivot trigger at 30%.

**Walkthrough:**

The test cohort landed at 34%, inside the inconclusive zone. The review also found that a logging bug had dropped events for one mobile platform for three days. Rather than calling it a win because the number beat the old baseline, the team classified it as inconclusive. They fixed instrumentation, extended the cohort, and specified that this retest was the last one before treating the feature as unsupported.

The second run came in at 42%, and the team persevered and moved on to testing whether streaks affected paid conversion.

## Resisting a vanity-metric persevere

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a marketplace team saw signups triple after a launch campaign and several stakeholders wanted to declare the new positioning validated. The pre-set criterion, however, was the share of new buyers completing a second purchase within 30 days, with success at 25% and a trigger at 10%.

**Walkthrough:**

The review opened by placing the written rule beside the data. Repeat purchase for the campaign cohort was 11%, just above the trigger but far from success. The signup spike was acknowledged but excluded from the decision because it had not been named as the test metric. The team classified the outcome as inconclusive leaning negative and designed a retest that isolated the campaign audience from organic buyers.

That retest came in at 8%, below the trigger, and the team pivoted the positioning while keeping the channel that had driven cheap signups.
