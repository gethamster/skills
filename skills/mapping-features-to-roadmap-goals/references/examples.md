# Examples: Mapping Features to GO Roadmap Goals

## Trimming a crowded column

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a team building a recruiting tool has the goal "help hiring managers fill roles faster and grow usage among hiring managers". The first draft of the features row lists eleven items gathered from sales, support, and the team.

**Walkthrough:**

The product manager asks, for each item, whether the goal could be met without it. Five items are clearly nice to have and move to the backlog under the goal. Two are requests from one large customer that do not affect time to fill a role; they are declined with a note explaining why, and flagged for the next roadmap workshop in case the customer's need points to a missing goal.

Four remain: a shared candidate scorecard, interview scheduling from within the tool, a hiring-manager digest, and faster search. The team writes one line for each explaining how it shortens time to hire. Search is later dropped as well, because the reason sounded weak once written down. The column ends with three features and a clear story.

## Finding enabling work

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a team maintaining a banking app has the goal "help customers complete a transfer on their first try and reduce support calls about failed transfers". The first feature list contains only visible user-interface changes.

**Walkthrough:**

The development team works through the questions about user experience, functionality, non-functional requirements, and technical health. They point out that a large share of failed transfers are caused by timeouts in an old payment service, which no interface change will fix.

The features row becomes: a clearer transfer flow, inline validation of account details, and a rebuild of the payment timeout handling. The third item would never have appeared on a feature-first roadmap, yet it is the one most likely to move the goal's metric.

## Sorting an inherited roadmap

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a new product manager inherits a roadmap of about twenty dated features for a learning platform and is asked to move to a GO roadmap without losing stakeholder trust.

**Walkthrough:**

She first agrees three goals for the year with the key stakeholders. She then takes each inherited feature and asks which goal it serves. Most fit under one of the three and become backlog items or roadmap features. A handful serve none, and she lists them separately with the stakeholder who asked for each.

At the next roadmap workshop the group reviews that list. Two items are dropped, and one, an accessibility overhaul, turns out to matter so much that the group adds an accessibility goal for later in the year. Nothing was silently deleted, which keeps the stakeholders' trust in the new format.
