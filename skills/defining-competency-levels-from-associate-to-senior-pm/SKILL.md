# Defining PM Competency Levels from Associate to Senior

## Overview

Defining PM competency levels means writing down, for each rung of your product manager career ladder, which competencies the role owns and how well. The [Product Team Competencies Framework](../../methods/product-team-competencies-framework/METHOD.md) gives you a strong starting point, because Neal Cabage built seniority into the vertical axis of his chart. Junior product managers tend to focus at the bottom of the chart, while senior PMs who think strategically operate in the upper part, "especially team leads, Directors" ([Cabage, Product Team Competencies](https://nealcabage.com/framework/product-team-competencies/)).

That reading turns the chart into a ladder. An associate PM owns tactical boxes such as Backlog Grooming, Product Requirements and UAT Testing. A senior PM owns strategic boxes such as Opportunity Discovery, Product Roadmap and Business Case, while still being able to do the tactical work. Cabage's account of the role supports this: he describes the junior PM role as often more "heads down" and focused on feature definition and development, and says responsibility for strategy often lies with someone more senior ([The Role of Product Management](https://nealcabage.com/product-management/)).

Cabage's page does not include a level-by-level matrix or rating scale, so the levels are yours to write. Other published models show what the finished product can look like. Ravi Mehta's Product Competency Toolkit includes a matrix of expectations from APM to VP, and he observes that Product Execution is critical early in a PM's career, while Product Strategy and Influencing People become the critical path to growth as PMs become more senior ([Mehta](https://www.ravi-mehta.com/product-manager-skills/)). Marty Cagan adjusts his skill importance ratings by level and notes that a senior PM has a much higher bar than an associate ([SVPG](https://www.svpg.com/developing-strong-product-managers/)).

The result is a competency matrix for product management that works as PM promotion criteria. Each level names the boxes it owns, the boxes where it contributes, and a short behavioral description of good work. A promotion case then shows evidence that the PM already works in the next level's boxes at the expected standard.

The product-type axis still matters. A senior PM on an internal platform moves up the right side of the chart, toward Technical Partnerships and Build vs Buy, while a senior consumer PM moves up the left, toward Market Positioning and Jobs to be Done. A good matrix allows both paths instead of assuming every senior PM ends up in the same boxes.

## How It Works

The matrix has three dimensions: level, box and expectation. Levels come from your company, typically something like associate PM, PM, senior PM and group or principal PM. Boxes come from your mapped grid. Expectations are short descriptions of what good work in a box looks like at a given level.

For each level and box, choose one of three states. Owns means the PM is expected to do this work independently and well. Contributes means they do parts of it with guidance or as part of someone else's effort. Not expected means the box is outside the level. Keeping to three states makes the matrix readable. A matrix that rates every box at every level on a numeric scale becomes too dense to use in a promotion discussion.

Cabage's vertical axis sets the general shape. Lower levels own tactical boxes and contribute to strategic ones. Upper levels own strategic boxes and keep the tactical ones as a foundation. The shape is a guide, and some boxes break it. Product-Market Fit sits on the tactical side of Market on Cabage's chart, yet it matters a great deal in consumer work, so you may decide that seniors on consumer products own it.

Expectations should describe behavior and scope. "Writes clear requirements for features" is an associate expectation. "Sets the requirements approach for a product area and reviews others' requirements" is a senior one. Both describe the same box at different levels. Each description should be testable against real work, so a promotion panel can point to an example.

Promotion criteria come from the difference between two adjacent levels. List the boxes that change state between them, for instance from contributes to owns. Those boxes are what a PM must demonstrate to move up. Cagan's suggestion fits here: once a PM has closed their gaps, show them how the importance ratings move for the next level so they know what to develop next.

## Step-by-Step Guide

### Step 1: List your levels and their scope

Write down each PM level at your company with one sentence on its scope, such as a feature, a product area or a portfolio. Scope determines how high on the chart a level can reasonably work. Confirm the list with whoever owns leveling and compensation. Keep management roles separate if you have them.

### Step 2: Sketch each level's home region

Using Cabage's reading of the axes, mark the region of the chart where each level mostly works. Associate levels sit low, senior and lead levels sit high. Where you have different product types, sketch a left-side path and a right-side path. This sketch is the skeleton of the matrix.

### Step 3: Assign a state to every box at every level

For each box, choose owns, contributes or not expected at each level. Follow the sketch but decide box by box, and note any box that breaks the general pattern with a reason. Check that each level owns enough boxes to fill a job. Check that no level owns every box.

### Step 4: Write behavioral expectations

For boxes a level owns, write one or two sentences describing good work at that level. Describe outputs and scope rather than traits. Pull language from real examples of strong work by PMs at that level. Leave contributes cells short.

### Step 5: Test the matrix against real PMs

Place several current PMs on the matrix and see whether it matches how leaders already judge them. If a PM everyone considers senior fails the senior criteria, the criteria are probably wrong. If a PM who struggles meets them easily, the expectations are too loose. Revise until the matrix explains the judgements people already make.

### Step 6: Derive promotion criteria between levels

For each pair of adjacent levels, list the boxes that change state and the expectations that rise. Turn that list into the promotion criteria for the next level. Require evidence of sustained work at the next level across several examples. Publish the criteria with the matrix.

### Step 7: Publish and schedule a review

Publish the matrix, the promotion criteria and the reasoning behind unusual choices. Walk each PM through where they stand and what the next level asks for. Review the matrix when the product mix or organization changes, and record what changed.

## Best Practices

- Use the vertical axis as the backbone of the ladder. Cabage places junior PMs at the bottom and senior PMs at the top of [his chart](https://nealcabage.com/framework/product-team-competencies/), which gives levels a natural order.
- Allow more than one senior path. Platform and consumer PMs climb different sides of the chart, and a single path forces one of them into boxes their product does not need.
- Keep lower-level tactical boxes as a foundation for senior levels. Senior PMs still need to write requirements and run acceptance testing when the team needs it.
- Keep three states per box. Owns, contributes and not expected are enough for promotion decisions and keep the matrix readable.
- Test against people leaders already agree on. A matrix that contradicts shared judgement will be ignored.
- Borrow shape from published ladders. [Mehta's toolkit](https://www.ravi-mehta.com/product-manager-skills/) shows how expectations can be described from APM to VP.

## Common Mistakes

- **Treating seniority as more of everything**: A senior column that demands strength in every box describes nobody. Cabage's framework does not assume any PM should do everything.
- **Using traits instead of outputs**: "Strategic thinker" cannot be verified in a promotion panel. Describe the work and scope the level produces.
- **Promoting on tactical speed alone**: A PM who gets faster at grooming and requirements has improved at their level. Promotion should require work in the boxes above.
- **One path for every product type**: Forcing a platform PM toward consumer market boxes to reach senior level punishes them for their product. Write paths for both sides of the chart.
- **Never revisiting the matrix**: Product changes shift which boxes matter. A matrix that is not reviewed drifts away from the work.
