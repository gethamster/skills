# Examples: OKR Cadence and Planning Cycles

## Fixing a cascade that always starts late

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a company with company, department and team OKRs finds that team OKRs are usually published three or four weeks into each quarter. By then, most teams have already started work without them.

**Walkthrough:**

The OKR lead builds the calendar backwards from the quarter start. Company OKRs move earlier, to about two weeks before the quarter, and departments and teams draft at the same time against them instead of waiting for each other. One alignment review replaces the department approval round.

The publication date is fixed for the first week of the quarter and does not move. In the next cycle, most teams publish on time, and the few that are late publish partial drafts and refine them at the first check-in.

## Choosing a shorter cycle for a young company

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a startup of a dozen people is still searching for a repeatable sales motion. Its priorities have shifted twice in the last quarter, and its OKRs were out of date within weeks.

**Walkthrough:**

The founders keep a single annual objective about finding a repeatable way to win customers. Under it, they run shorter cycles with lightweight planning: a short session at the start, weekly check-ins, and a brief reflection at the end.

They agree to review the cycle length once the sales motion stabilizes. When it does, they move to quarterly OKRs so that teams have time to pursue bigger outcomes, keeping the weekly check-in rhythm they have built.

## Agreeing a rule for mid-cycle changes

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: halfway through a quarter, a partner the business development team was counting on pulls out of a joint launch. One of the team's key results can no longer be met.

**Walkthrough:**

The company's written rule allows changes only at the mid-cycle review, with a reason recorded. At that review, the team explains the change and proposes a replacement key result around a different partner. Leadership agrees, and the original key result is kept in the record, marked as retired with the reason.

At the end-of-cycle reflection, the team discusses why it depended on a single partner and adds a check for single points of failure to its planning checklist. The rule let the team adapt without making mid-cycle changes routine.
