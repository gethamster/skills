# Synthesizing Six Forces into Strategic Recommendations

> Turn six force ratings into a narrative about the forces that dominate an industry and a short list of strategic options your team can act on.

## Overview

Synthesizing six forces into strategic recommendations is the last step of the [Six Forces Model](../../methods/six-forces-model/METHOD.md). By this point the team has rated rivalry, new entrants, substitutes, buyer power, supplier power and complementary products, each with evidence. Synthesis turns those six ratings into a view of the industry as a whole and into choices: where to compete, whom to partner with, what to defend.

This step answers a well-known criticism of the framework. The [six forces model entry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Six_forces_model) notes that the model "does not provide any actions to help deal with high or low force threats." A table of six ratings is a description. Synthesis adds the judgment that turns it into strategy: which forces dominate, how they interact, and what the company could do about them.

The core of synthesis is weighting by consequence. Six ratings of equal visual weight suggest six equal problems, but most industries are shaped by two or three forces, and one of them often sets the terms for the rest. An industry with calm rivalry and very powerful buyers is shaped mainly by the buyers. Averaging the ratings would call it "moderate," which misleads anyone who reads the result.

The second part is position. The [Harvard Institute for Strategy and Competitiveness](https://www.isc.hbs.edu/strategy/business-strategy/Pages/the-five-forces.aspx) describes industry structure and a company's relative position within the industry as the two basic drivers of profitability. The forces set the average; position decides whether a given company does better or worse than that average. Synthesis therefore looks for asymmetries: forces that press on your company less, or more, than on your rivals.

This skill starts from finished force assessments. If the evidence is still being gathered, collect the [six forces model data](../collecting-data-for-six-forces-analysis/SKILL.md) first, since synthesis on thin evidence produces confident-sounding options with nothing under them.

## How It Works

Synthesis works in three passes: rank, connect and decide.

Ranking identifies the dominant forces. For each force, ask how much of the industry's profit it takes or protects, and how that will change over the analysis horizon. A force rated "high" that affects a small part of revenue may matter less than a "medium" force that sets prices for the whole market. Most analyses end with two or three dominant forces; if every force seems dominant, the scope is probably too broad.

Connecting maps how the dominant forces interact. Porter treats complements as a factor that works through the other five forces rather than a force of its own, according to the [five forces article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porter%27s_five_forces_analysis), and that view is a good guide to interactions generally. A strong complement ecosystem can raise switching costs and so reduce buyer power. A dominant complementor can behave like a powerful supplier. A new substitute can pull customers and complementors away at the same time. Write each interaction as a short cause-and-effect sentence, backed by evidence from the force assessments.

Deciding generates options. For each dominant force that works against you, ask three questions: can we weaken it, can we avoid it by choosing a different segment or position, or can we make it press harder on rivals than on us? For each force that works for you, ask how to reinforce it. Complements often open cooperative options: partnering, co-investing or opening an ecosystem. Brandenburger and Nalebuff describe [co-opetition](https://hbr.org/2021/01/the-rules-of-co-opetition) as cooperating with a competitor to achieve a common goal or get ahead, which is sometimes the right answer when a complement is missing.

Each option is written with the forces it addresses, what it requires, the evidence that supports it and the main risk. A positioning view helps the audience see the logic: a one-page chart or table that shows each force, its direction, and where the company sits relative to rivals. The deliverable is short: the narrative, the positioning view, the options and the triggers that would reopen the analysis.

## Step-by-Step Guide

### Step 1: Check the six assessments are ready

Gather the rated assessments for all six forces and read them side by side. Check that each rating has evidence attached and that all six use the same scope and time horizon. Note where confidence is low and why. If one force has no real evidence, send it back for research before going further, since a gap there distorts everything that follows.

### Step 2: Put the ratings on one scale

Make sure every force uses the same rating definitions, such as low, medium and high with written criteria. Add the direction of each force over the horizon. Record rating disagreements as ranges instead of forcing a single value. The aim is comparability, so a "high" on buyer power means the same strength of pressure as a "high" on rivalry.

### Step 3: Identify the dominant forces

Rank the forces by how much profit they take or protect in this industry, now and over the horizon. Pick the two or three that shape the industry most and write one sentence for each explaining why. Resist averaging the ratings into an overall score. If the team cannot agree on the dominant forces, list the evidence each side relies on and settle it before moving on.

### Step 4: Map how the dominant forces interact

For each pair of dominant forces, write down how one strengthens or weakens the other, using evidence from the assessments. Pay special attention to the complementary products force, which often acts through buyer power, supplier power or substitutes. Note any interaction that could change direction, such as a complementor that might enter your market. These links usually explain the industry better than the ratings do.

### Step 5: Find asymmetries between players

Compare how the dominant forces affect your company and your main rivals. Look for differences in switching costs, supplier access, complement ecosystems or buyer mix. An asymmetry in your favor is something to defend; one against you is something to fix or avoid. Build the positioning view from these findings, showing each force and where each player stands.

### Step 6: Draft strategic options

Write a few options that respond to the dominant forces and asymmetries. For each, state the forces it addresses, what it would take, what evidence supports it and what could go wrong. Include at least one option that changes where you compete as well as how. Drop options that do not address a dominant force, however attractive they look on their own.

### Step 7: Stress-test the options

Test each option against plausible changes in the forces: a new entrant, a supplier consolidating, a complementor changing terms, a substitute improving. Ask what a strong rival would do in response. Keep options that hold up in several scenarios and flag those that depend on one force staying still. Record the assumptions each option rests on.

### Step 8: Present and set triggers

Present the narrative, the positioning view and the options to the decision-makers, leading with the dominant forces. Record the decision made and the reasons. Agree on the events that would reopen the analysis, such as an acquisition, a regulation change or a shift in a key complement, and set a date for a scheduled review.

## Best Practices

- Lead with the story. Decision-makers remember "buyer power sets the terms in this market" better than six ratings.
- Tie every option to a force. An option that does not address a dominant force is a separate idea and should be evaluated separately.
- Show direction as well as level. A force that is rising matters more to a strategy than one that is high and stable.
- Treat complements as a source of options. Partnerships and ecosystem investments can change a force in ways that price and product changes cannot, as [Adner's HDTV example](https://hbr.org/2006/04/match-your-innovation-strategy-to-your-innovation-ecosystem) of missing complements shows.
- Keep the evidence trail. Each claim in the narrative should link back to the force assessment that supports it, so the audience can check it.
- Write down what would change the conclusion. Triggers turn a one-off report into something the team can keep current.

## Common Mistakes

- **Averaging the forces**: A mean score across six forces hides the force that dominates. Rank by consequence and name the dominant forces instead.
- **Stopping at description**: The analysis ends with ratings and a chart, and the options section is thin or missing. Reserve most of the synthesis time for options and their trade-offs.
- **Scoring complements in isolation**: The sixth force gets a rating but no link to the others. Map how complements act on buyer power, supplier power and substitutes.
- **Ignoring position**: The synthesis describes the industry but not how your company sits in it. Look for asymmetries between you and your rivals, since those are where strategy has room to act.
- **Treating the result as permanent**: The analysis is presented once and never revisited. Set triggers and a review date when you present it.
