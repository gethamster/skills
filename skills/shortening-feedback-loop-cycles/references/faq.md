# FAQ: Shortening Feedback Loop Cycles for Competitive Advantage

## Does shortening the loop just mean deciding faster?

No. Most time is lost in waiting between phases, not in the thinking itself. Predefined signposts, standing orientation cells, if-then rules and delegated authority remove that waiting, so decisions arrive sooner without anyone rushing their judgment. The method page covers why raw speed and effective tempo are different ideas.

## How many if-then rules should a team have?

Only as many as there are recurring situations you understand well. Start with decisions you have already made several times, since repetition shows a rule is safe. A rule set that grows faster than the team can review it becomes a source of stale, unexamined actions, so pruning matters as much as adding.

## Won't delegating decisions increase risk?

It shifts risk rather than simply adding it. The [recommended approach](https://umbrex.com/resources/frameworks/strategy-frameworks/ooda-loop) is to make low-risk actions routine while reserving escalation for decisions outside established guardrails. Clear intent and concrete boundaries let edge teams act quickly on routine cases while senior attention is saved for the situations that genuinely need it.

## Who should sit in an orientation cell?

People who cover the perspectives your key decisions touch. The Umbrex framework gives the example of combining risk, operations, legal, finance and product views. Keep it small enough to convene quickly, and build in a dissenting role so the cell does not converge on one comfortable reading of every signal.

## What should happen when a signal fits no rule?

Send it to the orientation cell for a fresh assessment rather than stretching the closest rule. The cell should return what is happening, the assumptions in play, what would change the view and the options that fit. If such signals become frequent, treat that as evidence the environment has shifted and your rule set needs revision.
