# FAQ: Setting the Stage in a Sprint Retrospective

## How long should setting the stage take?

Keep it short compared with the rest of the meeting. Sample one-hour plans from Retrium and Atlassian give it between five and ten minutes. A new team, a newly merged team or a team coming out of a conflict may need longer, especially for the working agreements. A stable team that knows its agreements can open in a few minutes.

## Should we read the Prime Directive every time?

Read it if the team finds it helpful, and stop if it has become a ritual nobody listens to. The Prime Directive was written by Norm Kerth and asks everyone to assume people did the best job they could with what they knew at the time. Some teams read it at every retrospective, others only when a sprint went badly or new people have joined. The goal is the belief behind it, and repetition alone does not create that.

## What are good retrospective check-in activities?

A one-word description of the sprint is the simplest and works for most sessions. ESVP (explorer, shopper, vacationer, prisoner) is useful when you suspect disengagement, because answers are anonymous. A single question tied to the session focus, such as what surprised people, works well for teams that have grown used to the one-word round. Libraries such as Retromat and FunRetrospectives list many more.

## What working agreements should a retrospective have?

Keep them few and about how people talk: speak from your own experience, discuss the process rather than individuals, one conversation at a time, and agree what stays in the room. Let the team write or amend them so they own them. Review them when the team changes and drop any that nobody follows.

## What if the check-in shows that people do not want to be there?

Treat it as data. Acknowledge the result without asking who answered what, and ask the group what would make the session worthwhile. The answer often becomes the focus for the rest of the retrospective. If the same result appears several sprints in a row, the problem is probably with how retrospectives are run or acted on, and that deserves its own discussion.

## Should the manager attend?

Agree it with the team. The Agile Alliance notes that a manager's presence can inhibit discussion of performance issues, and GitLab's handbook mentions that some groups choose not to have direct managers attend. If a manager does attend, make it clear in the opening what role they are playing.
