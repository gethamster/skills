# Closing a Retrospective Meeting Effectively

> How to end a retrospective: read back the action items and owners, thank people, collect a quick ROTI on the session and close on time.

## Overview

Closing is the fifth and last phase of the [Five-Step Retrospective Framework](../../methods/five-step-retrospective-framework/METHOD.md). It is short, and it decides whether the rest of the meeting turns into change. Retromat's summary lists what the close covers: "Clarify follow-up; Appreciations; Clear end; How could the retrospectives improve?" ([Retromat](https://retromat.org/blog/what-is-a-retrospective/)). Each item on that list has a practical purpose.

Clarifying follow-up protects the actions. By the end of a long discussion, people remember the conversation better than the decisions. Reading back each action with its owner and review point, while everyone is still in the room, catches misunderstandings and makes the commitment public. GitLab's handbook asks the close to make sure "all of the action items are appropriately assigned to one or more group members with clear expectations for when they should be completed" ([GitLab Handbook](https://handbook.gitlab.com/handbook/engineering/management/group-retrospectives/)).

Appreciations recognize the work people did in the sprint and in the meeting. GitLab's handbook also asks that everyone who participated receive positive feedback about how they helped create the results. Specific thanks tell people which behavior the team values, and they end a meeting that may have included hard conversations on a steadier note.

Asking how the retrospective could improve turns the close into a small retrospective on the retrospective. A quick return on time invested (ROTI) vote or a pair of keep-and-change questions gives the facilitator data to adjust the next session. Over time this keeps the meeting useful. MindTools' summary of the phase includes soliciting feedback on the retrospective's effectiveness and thanking participants ([MindTools](https://www.mindtools.com/ao5tslk/sprint-retrospectives-in-agile-project-management/)).

A clear end respects the timebox. A retrospective that runs over teaches people the timebox is not real, and the next one starts with less goodwill. This skill covers how to end a retrospective in a few minutes, what to say, and how to collect feedback that you can act on.

## How It Works

The closing phase runs in four moves: confirm, record, appreciate and review.

Confirming means reading the retrospective action items back to the whole team. For each action, say what will change, who owns it, and when progress will be checked. Ask whether anyone understood it differently. This is the last chance to fix a vague action cheaply, and it doubles as a public commitment by the owner.

Recording means putting the actions where the team will see them again. Ben Linders recommends that actions go on "the status board and/or to the backlog, so that they are visible and will not be forgotten" ([Ben Linders](https://www.benlinders.com/2015/getting-retrospective-actions-done/)). Atlassian's retrospective play suggests entering them in the team's project tool so they are part of sprint planning and can be reviewed at the next retrospective ([Atlassian](https://www.atlassian.com/team-playbook/plays/retrospective)). Capture a short summary of the main insights with them, since the reasons behind an action are easy to forget.

Appreciating means thanking people for specific contributions: someone who raised a hard topic, someone who handled an incident, a newcomer who spoke up. Keep it genuine and short. Some teams run an appreciation round in which anyone can thank anyone. Others leave it to the facilitator.

Reviewing the retrospective closes the loop on the meeting itself. ROTI is one quick option: Retrium describes it as a way for the team "to provide quick feedback to the meeting leader on how they feel the retrospective meeting went" ([Retrium](https://www.retrium.com/blog/new-feature-rate-your-time-invested-in-retrospectives)). In a common version described by Zen Ex Machina, people score the session from 1 to 5, where 3 means "I gained enough to justify the time I spent", and are asked for one thing within their control that would raise their score by a point ([Zen Ex Machina](https://zenexmachina.com/return-on-time-invested-roti-retrospective-pattern/)). Retrium also lists a check-out question and Retro as a Gif as closing activities ([Retrium](https://www.retrium.com/ultimate-guide-to-agile-retrospectives/five-phases-of-a-successful-retrospective)).

The whole close fits in a few minutes. Atlassian's one-hour play gives it 5 minutes ([Atlassian](https://www.atlassian.com/team-playbook/plays/retrospective)). Reserve that time when you plan the meeting, and protect it when earlier phases run long.

## Step-by-Step Guide

### Step 1: Signal the move to closing

Tell the team the discussion is ending and the close has started. If a conversation is still open, park it with a named person and a time to continue. Check the clock and keep the close inside its reserved minutes. A clear signal stops the meeting from drifting to an end.

### Step 2: Read back each action item

Read each action aloud with its owner and when progress will be checked. Ask the owner to confirm and the team to flag anything unclear. Rewrite vague actions on the spot. If an action has no volunteer owner, decide now whether to find one or drop it.

### Step 3: Record the actions where the team works

Put the actions on the team board or in the sprint backlog before people leave, or name who will do it within the day. Add a one-line note on the insight behind each action. Agree that the next retrospective will begin by reviewing them. Share a short written summary with anyone who missed the session.

### Step 4: Thank people for specific contributions

Thank people for what they did in the sprint and in the meeting, naming the contribution. Invite others to add their own appreciations if the team likes that. Keep it brief and sincere. Skip appreciation that sounds automatic, since it undermines the rest.

### Step 5: Collect feedback on the retrospective

Run a quick ROTI or ask what to keep and what to change about the retrospective. Use anonymous input if people are unlikely to criticize the facilitator openly. Note the lowest scores and ask what would improve them. Write down one change to try in the next retrospective.

### Step 6: End on time

Close at the time you announced, even if the meeting ran long earlier. State the next retrospective date if it is not fixed. Thank the team and end the call or leave the room. After the meeting, review the feedback and adjust your plan for next time.

## Best Practices

- Reserve the closing minutes when you plan the retrospective, and protect them. A rushed close is where actions lose their owners.
- Read every action aloud. Confirming owners and review points in the room, as the [GitLab Handbook](https://handbook.gitlab.com/handbook/engineering/management/group-retrospectives/) asks, catches misunderstandings early.
- Record actions where the team plans its work, as [Ben Linders](https://www.benlinders.com/2015/getting-retrospective-actions-done/) recommends, so they are visible during the sprint.
- Make appreciation specific. Thanking a person for a named contribution carries more weight than a general thank-you to the room.
- Ask for feedback every time, and act on it. Feedback that never changes the next session stops being given honestly.
- Rotate closing activities. A ROTI, a check-out question and a keep-and-change round each surface different feedback.

## Common Mistakes

- **Running out of time for the close**: When earlier phases overrun, the close gets cut and actions leave without owners. Shorten earlier phases instead.
- **Ending without reading the actions back**: People leave with different memories of what was agreed. A read-back takes little time and prevents that.
- **Leaving actions in the meeting notes**: Notes are rarely read again. Put actions on the team board or backlog before the meeting ends.
- **Collecting feedback and ignoring it**: If the team says the retrospective is too long and nothing changes, the feedback stops. Change one thing each time and say what you changed.
- **Overrunning the timebox**: A late finish tells people the timebox is optional. End on time and schedule a follow-up for any unfinished topic.
