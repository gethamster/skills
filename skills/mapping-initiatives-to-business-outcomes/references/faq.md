# FAQ: Mapping Product Initiatives to Business Outcomes

## What if an initiative supports more than one outcome?

Link it to the outcome it moves most directly and note the secondary effect. Counting it under several outcomes makes each outcome look better covered than it is. The primary link is also the one the team will measure when deciding whether the initiative worked.

## Where does technical debt fit on the map?

Give it its own goal, such as reducing development cost or improving reliability, and map the work to that. Roman Pichler uses "decrease technical debt to reduce development cost" as an example of a legitimate product goal. Labeling it this way lets it compete openly with customer-facing work.

## What do I tell a stakeholder whose request does not map?

Explain which outcomes the team is pursuing and ask whether the request serves one of them in a way the team missed. If it does, write the hypothesis together. If it does not, park it with a reason and a date to revisit. Pichler treats declining requests that do not serve the goal as part of the product manager's job.

## How is this different from an opportunity solution tree?

An opportunity solution tree is a discovery tool that maps a single outcome to customer opportunities and then to solutions. Initiative mapping applies the same logic to the whole roadmap and backlog, including work that did not come from discovery. Many teams use a tree for each outcome and a roadmap-level map to see coverage across outcomes.

## How detailed should each hypothesis be?

One or two sentences: the customers, the behavior change, the outcome it feeds and the indicator to watch. Longer write-ups belong in the discovery notes. The map needs to be readable at a glance.
