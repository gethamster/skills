# Examples: Running Chapters for Discipline-Based Management

## Standing up a backend chapter

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a tribe of five squads has nine backend engineers reporting to a single engineering manager outside the squads, and each squad has chosen its own logging and error-handling approach.

**Walkthrough:**

The tribe lead maps disciplines and confirms backend appears in all five squads. A senior engineer in one squad is chosen as chapter lead and takes over line management of the nine engineers, with her squad agreeing she will carry less delivery work. She writes a remit covering logging standards, the backend interview loop and onboarding, and lists release decisions as squad-owned. The first chapter meetings compare the five logging approaches and agree on one.

Within a couple of review cycles, every engineer has had one-to-ones with a manager who reads their code.

## Splitting an overgrown design chapter

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a design chapter has grown to 22 people after a hiring push, and its lead reports that one-to-ones are slipping to once a quarter.

**Walkthrough:**

The tribe reviews the reporting load against the rule it set at launch, which was to split above roughly fifteen members. Designers are grouped into two clusters: those working on consumer-facing squads and those on internal tooling squads. A second lead is chosen from the tooling cluster, and each lead now manages around eleven people. Both chapters keep one shared monthly session on the design system so standards do not diverge.

One-to-ones return to a fortnightly rhythm.

## Resolving a lead versus squad conflict

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a testing chapter lead asks a tester to spend two days building a shared test framework, while the tester's squad product owner needs them on a release that week.

**Walkthrough:**

The tester raises the clash, which exposes that the remit never said who schedules chapter-wide work. The chapter lead and product owner agree that delivery scheduling belongs to the squad, and the framework work moves to the following sprint by agreement with the squad. The remit is updated: the chapter proposes shared work, and squads decide when their members take it on. The chapter lead adds a quarterly slot where product owners review planned chapter initiatives.

Conflicting instructions stop showing up in member feedback.
