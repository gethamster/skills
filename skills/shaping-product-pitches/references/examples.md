# Examples: Shaping Product Pitches with a Shape Up Pitch Template

## From a vague request to a small batch pitch

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: sales keeps asking for "better reporting" in a scheduling app for dog groomers. The product lead has a two-week slot on the small batch team to fill.

**Walkthrough:**

The lead calls two groomers who asked. Both describe the same moment: at the end of the month, they copy appointments into a spreadsheet to see which clients have not booked in a while. That becomes the problem story, with the spreadsheet as the baseline.

The lead sets a two-week appetite and sketches with a programmer: a single list of clients whose last visit is older than a chosen period, sorted by date, with a button to send a reminder using the existing template. Rabbit hole: clients with no past visits, decided up front to be excluded. No-gos: charts, custom date ranges and export. The pitch fits on one page, with one fat marker sketch of the list, and is bet on at the next table.

## A pitch that was sent back

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a designer writes a six-week pitch for offline support in a note-taking app. The solution section describes the screens in detail, and the rabbit holes section is empty.

**Walkthrough:**

Before posting it, the designer walks a senior engineer through the concept. The engineer asks how edits made on two offline devices will be merged. Nobody has an answer, and the engineer thinks it could take longer than the whole appetite on its own.

The designer does not post the pitch. They return to shaping with the engineer and find a narrower version: offline reading of recent notes, with editing disabled until the device reconnects. Conflict handling becomes a no-go, and the pitch that goes to the table is smaller and far less risky.

## Splitting frame and shape

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: at a mid-sized company, pitches keep getting long and technical before anyone knows if leadership cares about the problem.

**Walkthrough:**

The product team adopts Ryan Singer's later suggestion of separate frame and shape documents. A frame is a short page with the problem, the outcome and why it matters now, used to get agreement to spend shaping time. Only framed problems get a shaping session with an engineer.

The shape document then carries the solution, rabbit holes and no-gos, with breadboards and dataflow sketches. Leadership reads frames, and the build team reads shapes. Fewer detailed pitches are written for problems nobody wanted solved.
