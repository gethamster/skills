# Examples: Structuring Landing Page Copy for Conversion

## A webinar registration page from a paid social ad

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: an HR software company runs a social ad promising a live session on cutting time-to-hire for hourly roles. The landing page reused the company homepage, with five different buttons.

**Walkthrough:**

The writer defines one goal, registrations, and notes that readers arrive knowing the topic but not the company. The outline drops the long problem section, since the ad already named the problem, and moves a short speaker introduction and two quotes from past attendees up near the hero.

The hero repeats the ad's promise in the headline, the subhead gives the date, length and who the session is for, and the button reads "Save my seat." The middle covers what attendees will leave with, as three concrete takeaways, then answers two common objections: whether a recording will be sent and whether the session is a sales pitch. The final block repeats the same button.

## A trial page for readers from a comparison search

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a password manager builds a landing page for people searching for alternatives to a well-known competitor. These readers already use a password manager and are unhappy with it.

**Walkthrough:**

Research quotes show the main frustrations are price increases and a clumsy sharing feature. The writer outlines the page so the problem section names those two frustrations in the users' words, and the solution section shows how sharing and pricing work in this product, each with the feature that delivers it.

Because the traffic is comparing options, a factual comparison table sits in the objection section, along with an answer to the biggest switching fear: moving existing passwords. The how-it-works section shows the import in three steps. The primary action throughout is "Start my free trial," with a line stating whether a card is needed.

## Reordering for a skeptical audience

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a clinical documentation tool sells to hospital department heads, who distrust vendor claims. The first draft followed the default order, with proof in the middle.

**Walkthrough:**

Interviews show that these buyers look for evidence first and read claims only after they see who else uses the product. The writer moves proof up, right after the hero: named departments that agreed to be listed, and a short summary of an evaluation the tool went through, with a link to the full document.

The benefit section follows, written as specific statements a department head can check. Objection handling covers data security and integration with existing records systems. The single action throughout is to request a pilot, and the button says so.
