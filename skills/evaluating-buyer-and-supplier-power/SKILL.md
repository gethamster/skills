# Evaluating Buyer and Supplier Bargaining Power

> Evaluate buyer and supplier bargaining power by segment, using secondary data and primary market research for a supplier power evaluation you can defend.

## Overview

Evaluating buyer and supplier bargaining power is the part of a six forces analysis that asks who at either end of the value chain can take profit away from the industry. Buyers take it by pushing prices down or demanding more for the same price. Suppliers take it by raising prices or limiting quality and availability. The two are separate forces in the [Six Forces Model](../../methods/six-forces-model/METHOD.md), and a good buyer power assessment and supplier power evaluation treat them separately, segment by segment.

Both come from Porter's original framework. In [The Five Competitive Forces That Shape Strategy](https://hbr.org/2008/01/the-five-competitive-forces-that-shape-strategy), he lists customers and suppliers among the four forces beyond established rivals that compete for an industry's profits. The factors that drive each are well documented. The [five forces article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porter%27s_five_forces_analysis) lists, for buyers, buyer concentration relative to firm concentration, switching costs, information availability, available substitutes and price sensitivity; for suppliers, it lists switching costs, differentiation of inputs, the impact of inputs on cost or differentiation, substitute inputs, supplier concentration and the ability to integrate forward.

The skill adds method to those lists. Power is rarely uniform. A handful of large accounts may negotiate hard while hundreds of small customers accept list prices; one critical supplier may hold real bargaining power while commodity suppliers hold none. Rating "buyers" as one group averages these into a number that describes nobody. So the first move is to segment, and every rating that follows is per segment.

The evidence comes from two kinds of market research. Secondary data (filings, industry statistics, public pricing, procurement practices) establishes the structure: how concentrated each side is, how many alternatives exist. Primary market research, mainly interviews, explains behavior: how buyers actually choose, what it would cost them to switch, how suppliers set terms. The [SBA's market research guide](https://www.sba.gov/business-guide/plan-your-business/market-research-competitive-analysis) describes primary research as giving a nuanced understanding of a specific audience at the cost of time and money, which is why it is used for the questions that secondary data cannot answer.

## How It Works

Segmentation comes first. For buyers, group customers by the characteristics that change bargaining position: size, purchase volume, how critical your product is to them, and how they buy. For suppliers, group inputs by how critical and how differentiated they are. Note the share of revenue each buyer segment represents and the share of cost each supplier category represents, so the ratings can later be weighted by consequence.

Each segment is then assessed against its factors. Buyer power is higher when buyers are few relative to sellers, when switching is cheap, when buyers have good price information, when substitutes exist and when your product is a large, price-sensitive cost for them. Supplier power is higher when suppliers are few, when their inputs are differentiated or critical, when substitute inputs are scarce, when switching suppliers is expensive and when suppliers could integrate forward. Integration threats deserve specific attention on both sides: a buyer that could make the product itself, or a supplier that could sell directly to your customers, has bargaining power even if it never acts.

Secondary sources supply the structural facts. Public companies describe their markets and main risks in their 10-K filings, as the SEC's [guide to the 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/answers/reada10k.htm) explains, so read both sections for any mention of major customers or suppliers. For concentration, the [Herfindahl-Hirschman Index](https://www.justice.gov/atr/herfindahl-hirschman-index) gives a standard measure: square each firm's market share and sum the results. The Department of Justice treats markets above 1,800 points as highly concentrated.

Primary research supplies the behavior. Interview customers who recently bought, renewed or left, procurement staff, and supplier account managers. Ask about specific recent negotiations, what alternatives were considered and what switching would involve. [Nielsen Norman Group](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/user-interviews/) cautions that interviews collect self-reported data and that leading questions compromise validity, so questions about past events are more reliable than hypotheticals.

The output is a short assessment per segment: the rating, the factors that drive it, the evidence for each factor, the direction over the analysis horizon, and a note on confidence.

## Step-by-Step Guide

### Step 1: Segment buyers and suppliers

List customer segments by size, volume, dependence on your product and buying process, and list supplier categories by criticality and differentiation. Record the share of revenue or cost each represents. Keep the number of segments small enough to research properly. If a single customer or supplier is large enough to matter on its own, treat it as its own segment.

### Step 2: Choose the factors and define the rating scale

Take the buyer and supplier factor lists as a starting point and keep the ones that apply to your industry. Write what low, medium and high look like for each factor so different analysts rate consistently. Decide in advance which factors carry more weight in your industry and write down why. Share the factors and definitions before research starts, so evidence is collected against them.

### Step 3: Gather secondary data on structure

Collect the structural facts for each segment: how many buyers and suppliers there are, how concentrated they are, what alternatives exist and what switching involves. Use filings, industry statistics, public price lists and procurement records. Compute concentration where shares are available. Record every source and date, and mark where the data is thin.

### Step 4: Run primary market research on behavior

Interview a few people per segment: customers who recently bought or left, procurement staff, supplier representatives and your own sales and purchasing teams. Ask about specific recent negotiations, alternatives considered and what switching would cost in time, money and risk. Avoid hypothetical and leading questions. Keep notes in the same format for every interview so answers can be compared.

### Step 5: Supplier power evaluation and buyer power assessment

Rate each factor for each segment, citing the evidence behind the rating. Then give each segment an overall rating for buyer or supplier power, explaining which factors drive it. Keep buyer and supplier ratings separate throughout. Where evidence conflicts, record both sides and lower the confidence.

### Step 6: Weight by consequence and note direction

Combine segment ratings into an overall view for each force, weighting by the share of revenue or cost each segment represents. Note which segment drives the result. Add the direction each force is moving, such as buyers consolidating or a new supplier entering. Flag integration threats separately, since they can change power quickly.

### Step 7: Write and test the assessment

Write a short narrative for each force: the rating, the drivers, the evidence and the direction. Review it with people who deal with buyers and suppliers every day and ask what it gets wrong. Revise where they point to evidence you missed. Hand the assessment to the synthesis step with confidence levels marked.

## Best Practices

- Segment before rating. Power differs across customer and supplier groups, and a single rating for "buyers" hides the segment that matters most.
- Keep the two forces separate. Buyers and suppliers have different drivers, and combining them into one "bargaining power" score loses the information the synthesis needs.
- Weight by consequence. A powerful segment that is a small share of revenue matters less than a moderately powerful one that is most of it.
- Watch for integration threats. The ability of a buyer or supplier to integrate, listed among the [supplier power factors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porter%27s_five_forces_analysis), creates bargaining power even when it is never used.
- Use recent negotiations as evidence. What happened in the last renewal or price change is more reliable than what people say they would do.
- Revisit after structural events. A merger among customers or suppliers can change a rating overnight.

## Common Mistakes

- **Rating buyers and suppliers as single groups**: The rating reflects an average that describes no real segment. Split them by size, criticality and dependence first.
- **Relying only on internal opinion**: Sales and purchasing teams have strong views, but they are one side of the negotiation. Check their views against interviews and secondary data.
- **Confusing concentration with power**: A concentrated supplier base with easy switching may hold less power than a fragmented one with high switching costs. Rate every factor; concentration is only one of them.
- **Ignoring switching costs on your side**: Supplier power depends on what it would cost you to change suppliers, including requalification and integration work. Estimate it explicitly.
- **Asking hypothetical questions**: "Would you pay more for this?" produces unreliable answers. Ask what the customer did in the last negotiation.
