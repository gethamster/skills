# FAQ: Defining the Customer's Core Functional Job

## How do I know the job statement is at the right level?

Ask "why?" and "how?" of it. If "why?" returns a task the executor clearly owns and would want help with, move up. If "how?" returns a list of separate jobs rather than a sequence of steps, move down. Stop at the level where "how?" produces steps you could put on a job map.

## Should I define the job before or after interviews?

Draft it before, so you know whom to recruit, and treat it as a hypothesis. Revise it after the first interviews, since customers often describe a slightly different job than the team assumed. Freeze it before writing outcome statements, because changing the job afterwards means rewriting them.

## Can a product serve more than one core functional job?

Yes, and many do. Define each job separately, with its own executor, and decide which one the current study covers. Mixing two jobs in one statement produces a job map that describes neither well.

## What about the buyer's goals?

Record them, but separately from the job statement. In business products the buyer often cares about cost, risk and reporting while the executor cares about getting the work done. Both matter for strategy, and keeping them apart stops the buyer's criteria from reshaping the executor's job.

## Where do emotional and social jobs go?

In their own list next to the functional job. The Christensen Institute describes jobs as having functional, social and emotional dimensions ([Christensen Institute](https://www.christenseninstitute.org/theory/jobs-to-be-done/)). They are real and useful for design and positioning, but a functional statement with feelings mixed in cannot be mapped into steps.
