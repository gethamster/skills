# Shifting from Working In to Working On Your Business

> Stop working in your business all day: find the delivery work you can drop or delegate, then spend the freed hours working on the business.

## Overview

"Work on your business, not in it" is the line EMyth calls the defining idea behind Michael Gerber's coaching company ([EMyth about page](https://www.emyth.com/about-us)). Working in the business is the delivery work customers pay for. Working on it is designing how that work gets done, who does it and where the business is heading. This skill is the practical routine for moving hours from the first kind to the second. The [technician manager entrepreneur framework](../../methods/technician-manager-entrepreneur-framework/METHOD.md) explains why owners drift into the first kind; this page is about getting out.

The problem is rarely a lack of intent. Delivery work comes with deadlines and customers attached, while work on the business has neither, so it loses every scheduling contest. Research outside small business points the same way. In a study published in Harvard Business Review, Julian Birkinshaw and Jordan Cohen found that knowledge workers spend on average 41% of their time on activities that "offer little personal satisfaction and could be handled competently by others" ([Birkinshaw and Cohen, HBR](https://hbr.org/2013/09/make-time-for-the-work-that-matters)). The study covered knowledge workers and did not single out small business owners.

The skill works in three moves. First you find out where your time goes. Then you choose which delivery tasks to drop, hand off or redesign. Finally you give the freed time a specific job, because time that has no assignment tends to fill up with the next urgent request.

The output is a short list of tasks you no longer do, the person or process now responsible for each, and a recurring calendar block with a named agenda for work on the business. Over several cycles the list grows and the block gets bigger.

This skill overlaps with its siblings on purpose, and each goes deeper on one part. For a detailed audit of your role balance, use the [E-Myth self-assessment](../assessing-your-technician-manager-entrepreneur-balance/SKILL.md). For documenting the work you hand off, use [building business systems](../building-systems-as-the-manager-role/SKILL.md).

## How It Works

The routine borrows its shape from the Birkinshaw and Cohen process, which they describe as a variant of the Start/Stop/Continue exercise ([HBR](https://hbr.org/2013/09/make-time-for-the-work-that-matters)). They ask people to identify low-value tasks, sort them into "quick kills", "off-load opportunities" and "long-term redesign", hand off what can be handed off, and then decide in advance how to use the freed time. In their trial with 15 executives, participants cut desk work by an average of six hours a week and meeting time by two hours a week ([HBR](https://hbr.org/2013/09/make-time-for-the-work-that-matters)).

The E-Myth version adds a test for each task: which role is it serving? A task that produces today's output is Technician work. A task that designs how output gets produced is Manager work. A task that decides what the business should become is Entrepreneur work. The routine targets Technician tasks that someone else could do to your standard, because those release the most time without weakening the business.

Handing off is where most attempts fail. Oncken and Wass's classic HBR article, first published in 1974, asks why "managers are typically running out of time while their subordinates are typically running out of work" ([Oncken and Wass, HBR](https://hbr.org/1999/11/management-time-whos-got-the-monkey)). Their answer is that the next move on a task, which they call the monkey, keeps landing back on the manager. A handoff only frees time if the next move stays with the person you gave the task to, which means they need the information and authority to make it.

The freed time needs a job. Birkinshaw and Cohen tell participants to write down two or three things they should be doing but are not, and they report that some participants found the saved time "immediately swallowed up" by firefighting ([HBR](https://hbr.org/2013/09/make-time-for-the-work-that-matters)). In E-Myth terms, the block becomes Manager time (documenting and improving systems) or Entrepreneur time (setting direction), each with a written output.

The weekly review keeps the shift from reversing. Tasks creep back when a delegate is stuck, a client asks for you by name, or you step in to save time. A short review each week catches the creep early and turns each case into a fix to the handoff.

## Step-by-Step Guide

### Step 1: Track a real week

Log what you actually do for one normal working week, in blocks of about half an hour. Record the task, who asked for it, and whether it produced output or improved how output is produced. Do it as you go, since reconstructing a week from memory flatters it. Include email, messages and small interruptions, because that is where delivery work hides. At the end, total the hours by task type.

### Step 2: Tag each task by role

Mark each task as Technician, Manager or Entrepreneur by its purpose. Writing a client proposal is Technician work if you are doing the writing and Manager work if you are building the template others will use. Be strict about meetings: attending to do the work yourself is Technician time. The result shows how much of the week is working in the business.

### Step 3: Sort the Technician tasks

Go through the Technician tasks and put each into one of the three groups from [Birkinshaw and Cohen](https://hbr.org/2013/09/make-time-for-the-work-that-matters): quick kills you can simply stop, off-load opportunities someone else can take with little preparation, and long-term redesign work that needs a documented process first. Keep the tasks that genuinely need your expertise, and write down why each one does. Expect the "only I can do this" list to shrink when you have to justify each item.

### Step 4: Define the standard for each handoff

For each off-load task, write what a good result looks like, what the person should decide on their own, and when they should come back to you. Use the degrees of initiative from [Oncken and Wass](https://hbr.org/1999/11/management-time-whos-got-the-monkey), from "recommend, then take resulting action" up to "act on own, then routinely report", and agree which one applies. A written standard lets you accept work that meets it even when it differs from how you would have done it.

### Step 5: Hand off and set check-ins

Transfer the quick kills and off-load tasks first, with a short overlap where the other person does the work and you review it. Set a fixed check-in time for each task so questions come at an agreed moment instead of as interruptions. Put the long-term redesign tasks on the list for your first blocks of Manager time.

### Step 6: Book time on the business

Create a recurring calendar block for work on the business and give each session a written agenda and output, such as "document the onboarding process" or "decide which service to stop selling". Paul Graham's point that makers need time "in units of half a day at least" ([Maker's Schedule, Manager's Schedule](http://www.paulgraham.com/makersschedule.html)) applies here, so prefer one long block to several short ones. Treat the block like a client meeting that cannot move.

### Step 7: Review weekly and expand

Once a week, check which handed-off tasks came back to you and why, whether the block was used for its agenda, and what to hand off next. Fix the cause of each returned task, which is usually a missing standard or unclear authority. When the current block holds for a few weeks, add the next group of tasks to the list.

## Best Practices

- Track before you decide. Owners consistently misjudge where their time goes, and a real log gives you something to argue with other than memory.
- Start with the tasks you are worst at or like least. They are easier to let go of, which builds the habit before you tackle the tasks tied to your identity.
- Keep the next move with the delegate. When someone brings a problem back, agree on what they will do next before the conversation ends, following [Oncken and Wass's rule](https://hbr.org/1999/11/management-time-whos-got-the-monkey) that every monkey should have "an assigned next feeding time and degree of initiative".
- Give every block an output. "Strategy time" with no agenda turns into inbox time. A named document or decision at the end keeps it honest.
- Expect the first weeks to feel slower. Reviewing someone else's work takes time until the standard settles, and that cost is the price of the handoff.
- Tell clients and the team what changed. People keep asking for you by name unless they know who now owns the work.

## Common Mistakes

- **Delegating without a standard**: Handing over a task with no written definition of done produces work you redo yourself. Write the standard first, even if it is five lines.
- **Taking the monkey back**: Saying "let me think about it" to a delegate puts the next move back on you. Ask what they recommend and agree on their next step.
- **Leaving freed time unassigned**: Time without a named purpose fills with reactive work, as [Birkinshaw and Cohen](https://hbr.org/2013/09/make-time-for-the-work-that-matters) found with some participants. Assign it before you free it.
- **Treating every task as expert work**: Most delivery tasks have a core that needs judgment and a larger part that does not. Split them and hand off the part that does not.
- **Doing it once**: A single clear-out drifts back within weeks. The weekly review is what makes the shift stick.
