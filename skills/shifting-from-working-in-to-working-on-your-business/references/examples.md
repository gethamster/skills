# Examples: Shifting from Working In to Working On Your Business

## A bookkeeping practice owner clears her inbox work

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: the owner of a small bookkeeping practice with three staff tracks one week. She finds that client email triage, month-end reconciliations she does herself and chasing missing receipts take most of her hours. She has not looked at pricing or her service list in a year.

**Walkthrough:**

She tags the week and sorts the Technician tasks. Chasing receipts is an off-load opportunity: a staff member can send the standard reminder sequence. Reconciliations for two simple clients are off-load opportunities once she writes a short checklist. Email triage needs a long-term redesign, so she puts "design a shared inbox with routing rules" on her first Manager block.

She books one half-day each week as time on the business, with the first agenda item being the inbox design and the second a review of which services are profitable. After a month, receipt chasing and the two reconciliations are off her plate and the inbox rules are live. The weekly review shows one reconciliation came back twice, both times because a bank feed broke, so she adds "what to do when the feed fails" to the checklist.

## A developer-founder keeps getting pulled back into code

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: the founder of a small software studio has two developers but still reviews every pull request and fixes urgent bugs himself. His "strategy afternoon" has been cancelled for three weeks in a row.

**Walkthrough:**

His log shows that urgent bug fixes arrive through direct messages to him. He agrees with the senior developer that she owns triage and first response, with the degree of initiative set to "act, but advise at once". Pull request review moves to the two developers reviewing each other, with the founder reviewing only changes to billing code.

The strategy afternoon gets a fixed agenda and moves to a day when the studio has no client calls. When a developer brings him a bug in the next week, he asks what she recommends and confirms her next step instead of taking the fix. At the weekly review he counts how many times he was pulled in and why, and the number falls over the following weeks.

## A salon owner redesigns before delegating

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a salon owner works the chair most days and does all the ordering, rota planning and supplier calls in the evenings. Every attempt to hand off ordering has ended with stock running out.

**Walkthrough:**

She sorts ordering as long-term redesign rather than an off-load opportunity, because nothing about it is written down. In her first two blocks on the business she writes a par-level list for each product and a weekly count sheet. Only then does she hand ordering to her senior stylist, with a rule that anything over the par list needs her approval.

The rota stays with her for now, and she writes down why: it depends on staff preferences she has not recorded anywhere. She puts documenting those preferences on the list for the next cycle, so the rota can follow ordering once the information exists outside her head.
