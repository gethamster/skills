# Examples: Rotating and Managing Board Membership Over Time

## Example: A DevTools Startup Managing a 30-Member TAB Through Its First Year

**Scenario:**

A developer tools company launched a 30-member Technical Advisory Board. After 6 months, engagement scores showed 8 members had effectively gone dormant (engagement below 0.4), and 5 members had become so dominant that calls felt repetitive. The board had also drifted toward backend-heavy representation as the product expanded into frontend tooling.

**Walkthrough:**

The TAB lead implemented the rotation system mid-stream. They assigned all 30 members to three cohorts of 10, with Cohort A's term ending in 6 weeks, Cohort B in 3 months, and Cohort C in 6 months. The 8 dormant members were placed in Cohort A for near-term graceful offboarding. Each got a personal message and an exit call invitation. Of the 8, 5 completed exit calls that yielded valuable meta-feedback about the TAB process itself (two said call scheduling was too rigid, one said they didn't feel their feedback was being used).

Meanwhile, the lead activated their recruitment pipeline, specifically targeting frontend developers and DevOps engineers to correct the backend skew. They used alumni referrals from the 5 offboarded members to source 4 high-quality candidates. Within 8 weeks, the board was back to 30 members with better role diversity, higher average engagement scores, and a clear rotation calendar for the next 12 months.

The robots.txt tester audit analogy proved apt: just as you'd discover stale rules blocking important content, the audit revealed 'blocked paths' — dormant members occupying slots that could surface fresh, diverse signal. The quarterly health scorecard became a standing agenda item in their product leadership meeting.

## Example: Re-Engaging an Alumni Member for a New Research Phase

**Scenario:**

A platform company had rotated out a senior infrastructure engineer 8 months ago. Their new product initiative focused on infrastructure-as-code, and they needed that specific expertise back on the board.

**Walkthrough:**

The TAB lead checked the alumni CRM tags and found the former member had contributed heavily to infrastructure pain themes during their original term. They sent a personal re-invitation explaining the new research focus, noting that the member's earlier insights had directly influenced the new initiative (citing specific product decisions). The member accepted a new 6-month term.

Critically, the re-invitation came after the mandatory 6-month gap. The member returned with fresh perspectives — their own stack had evolved, they'd changed companies, and they brought new pain points that wouldn't have surfaced if they'd simply stayed on the board continuously. The gap period had been the key ingredient: it prevented staleness while preserving the relationship.
