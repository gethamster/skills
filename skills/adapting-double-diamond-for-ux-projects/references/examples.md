# Examples: Adapting the Double Diamond UX Framework for Design Projects

## A feature request that arrives as a solution

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a product manager asks the UX team to "add advanced filters to the search page" of an online marketplace. The team works in two-week sprints and the request is on the next quarter's roadmap.

**Walkthrough:**

The designer writes down the assumed problem: buyers cannot find relevant listings. They agree with the product manager to spend one sprint on a short first diamond before any design work.

Discover combines search analytics, a review of recent support tickets and a handful of interviews with buyers who searched recently. Analytics show many searches end with no click. Interviews show buyers type product names that sellers describe differently.

Define produces a short journey map of a failed search and a problem statement: buyers searching by common product names miss listings that use different terms, so they leave without results. The product owner and lead engineer review it.

Develop explores three directions: synonym matching, suggested search terms, and the requested filters. Low-fidelity prototypes of each go into two small test rounds.

Suggested search terms perform best, and filters help only a small group of expert buyers. The team hands off the suggestion design with its reasoning and keeps filters as a later option.

## A new internal tool with an unknown audience

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: an operations department wants a tool for scheduling field technicians. There is no existing product and the users are technicians and dispatchers the design team has never met.

**Walkthrough:**

The first diamond is sized larger because almost everything is unknown. The designers shadow dispatchers for several shifts and ride along with technicians.

Define produces two journey maps (dispatcher and technician) and personas, because the team keeps disagreeing about who the main user is. The problem statement centres on last-minute changes that dispatchers cannot communicate reliably.

Develop and Deliver run alongside sprints, with designers a sprint ahead. Test rounds happen in the depot with real dispatchers.

After release, the team tracks how often jobs are reassigned by phone instead of in the tool, which was the behaviour the design aimed to change.

## Running the diamonds inside a tight deadline

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a team has four weeks to redesign an account settings page before a regulatory change takes effect.

**Walkthrough:**

The first diamond takes three days: a review of settings-related support tickets, analytics on the current page and a few quick interviews. The problem narrows to users not finding where to change their communication preferences.

Develop takes a week, with two layouts sketched and tested with a small group each.

Deliver takes the remaining time: one direction is refined, tested again at higher fidelity and handed off with a list of edge cases. The team notes what it did not have time to research and adds it to the backlog.
