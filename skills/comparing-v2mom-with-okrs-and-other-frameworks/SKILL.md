# V2MOM vs OKRs and Other Goal-Setting Frameworks

> Decide when V2MOM, OKRs, a balanced scorecard or Hoshin Kanri fits your planning problem, and how to combine V2MOM with OKRs without duplication.

## Overview

Teams looking at V2MOM are usually also looking at OKRs, and sometimes at a balanced scorecard or Hoshin Kanri. All of them connect goals across an organization, so they are easy to confuse. They were built for different problems, though, and choosing well starts with naming the problem you have. The [V2MOM Framework](https://tryhamster.com/methods/v2mom-framework) method page describes V2MOM itself; this skill is about the comparison and the choice.

V2MOM, as Marc Benioff describes it in [his write-up](https://www.salesforce.com/blog/how-to-create-alignment-within-your-company/), answers five questions about what you want, what matters, how you will get it, what stands in the way and how you will know. Its distinctive features are the ranked values and methods and the explicit obstacles section. OKRs, which [What Matters describes](https://www.whatmatters.com/faqs/okr-meaning-definition-example) as a goal-setting method created by Andy Grove at Intel and brought to Google by John Doerr in 1999, pair a few objectives with specific, time-bound key results on a quarterly cycle. The balanced scorecard, introduced by Robert Kaplan and David Norton in [HBR in 1992](https://hbr.org/1992/01/the-balanced-scorecard-measures-that-drive-performance-2), is a measurement system that looks beyond financial results. Hoshin Kanri is a lean strategy deployment approach built around a back-and-forth negotiation between levels.

The output of this skill is a decision: which framework, or which combination, fits your organization now, and why. It includes a short diagnosis of the planning problem, the reason the chosen framework addresses it, and a plan for how any combined frameworks will divide the work. A good decision also says what would make you revisit it.

No framework fixes problems it was not designed for. [Cascade's V2MOM guide](https://www.cascade.app/blog/the-v2mom-framework) notes that V2MOM will not fix a toxic culture or inexperienced leadership, and the same holds for OKRs and scorecards. Choosing a framework is a way to structure planning, and it works only where leaders are willing to make and publish real choices.

## How It Works

Start with the problem, not the framework. Planning problems tend to fall into a few types. Some organizations lack shared direction: teams do not agree on where the company is going or which priorities win. Some lack execution focus: direction is clear, but goals are vague and progress is not tracked. Some lack a balanced view of performance: leaders watch revenue but miss the customer, process and learning signals that predict it. Some lack a working dialogue between levels: plans come down from the top and never get tested against what teams can deliver.

Each framework maps to one of these. V2MOM is strongest on shared direction, because it makes leaders write down and rank their values and methods, name obstacles and have every level translate the plan into its own. OKRs are strongest on execution focus, with measurable key results reviewed each quarter and, as What Matters notes, kept separate from compensation so people set ambitious targets. The balanced scorecard is strongest on measuring performance from several angles at once. Hoshin Kanri's catchball, which [Businessmap describes](https://businessmap.io/lean-management/hoshin-kanri/what-is-catchball) as goals passing down and feedback passing back up until levels agree, is strongest on the dialogue between levels.

| Framework | Main question it answers | Distinctive feature |
|-------|-------|-------|
| V2MOM | Where are we going and what wins when priorities collide? | Ranked values and methods, explicit obstacles |
| OKRs | What will we achieve this quarter and how will we measure it? | Objectives with measurable key results, regular scoring |
| Balanced scorecard | How are we performing across more than finances? | Measures from several perspectives at once |
| Hoshin Kanri | Do the plans at each level fit together and are they feasible? | Catchball negotiation between levels |

The frameworks also combine. The most common pairing is V2MOM for annual direction and OKRs for quarterly execution. In that setup, V2MOM methods become the source of OKR objectives, and V2MOM measures inform the key results. The risk is duplication: two documents saying the same thing in different formats. Avoid it by giving each a clear job and linking them rather than copying text between them.

Finally, size and stability matter. A small team with shared daily context may not need any formal framework. Visible.vc's [overview of V2MOM](https://visible.vc/blog/v2mom-salesforce/) suggests a long-established business running steadily may not need a V2MOM at all. Match the weight of the framework to the size of the alignment problem.

## Step-by-Step Guide

### Step 1: Diagnose the planning problem

Interview a handful of leaders and team leads about where planning breaks down today. Ask where decisions get stuck, where teams contradict each other and where results surprise people. Sort the answers into direction, execution focus, balanced measurement and dialogue between levels. Name the one or two types that dominate.

### Step 2: Map the problem to a framework

Use the diagnosis to shortlist frameworks. A direction problem points toward V2MOM. An execution problem points toward OKRs. A measurement blind spot points toward a balanced scorecard, and a gap between top-down plans and team reality points toward catchball. Write one sentence explaining why the shortlisted framework fits.

### Step 3: Check the fit with culture and capacity

Ask whether leaders will rank priorities and publish them, which V2MOM requires. Ask whether the organization can sustain a quarterly scoring rhythm, which OKRs require. Check who will run the process and how much time it will take from managers. Remove any option the organization cannot realistically sustain.

### Step 4: Decide whether to combine

If the diagnosis showed two problems, consider pairing frameworks. Give each one a distinct job and time horizon, such as V2MOM for the year's direction and OKRs for each quarter's execution. Define how they link: which V2MOM section feeds which OKR field. Decide where each document lives so readers can move between them.

### Step 5: Pilot with one group

Run the chosen setup with one department or leadership team for a cycle before rolling it out. Watch for duplication, confusion about which document to update and time spent on process. Collect feedback from authors and readers. Adjust the design before extending it.

### Step 6: Set a review point

Write down what would make you change the decision, such as rapid growth, a change in strategy or persistent duplication. Put a date on the calendar to review the choice after a full cycle. Share the reasoning with the organization so people understand why the framework was chosen.

## Best Practices

- Diagnose before choosing. Picking a framework because a well-known company uses it skips the question of whether you share their problem.
- Give each framework in a combination a distinct job and time horizon. Overlap produces two documents nobody fully maintains.
- Link documents instead of copying text between them. A V2MOM method can point to the OKR that tracks it, so updates happen in one place.
- Keep the lightest framework that solves the problem. Extra process has a cost in manager time that grows with the organization.
- Revisit the choice as the organization changes. The framework that fits a team of forty may not fit a company of several hundred.

## Common Mistakes

- **Choosing by popularity**: adopting a framework because it is fashionable leads to process without purpose. Start from the diagnosed problem.
- **Running V2MOM and OKRs as duplicates**: writing the same goals twice in two formats doubles the work. Split direction from execution and link the documents.
- **Expecting a framework to fix culture**: no template will make leaders rank priorities they are unwilling to rank. Address the leadership problem directly.
- **Rolling out to everyone at once**: a design flaw then affects every team. Pilot with one group first.
- **Never revisiting the choice**: organizations grow and change. Schedule a review after a full cycle.
