# Examples: Applying JTBD Insights to Product Strategy

## Accounts payable: choosing a segment and a strategy

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: an accounts payable startup has three needs-based segments. "Drowning in reconciliation" is the largest and already has several capable competitors. "Worried about paying the wrong party" is smaller, has the highest opportunity scores and is poorly served by everyone.

**Walkthrough:**

The team picks the worried segment first, because its needs are severe and unaddressed. The outcomes show an underserved segment willing to value a better job, so the team chooses a differentiated strategy: get the confirm step done significantly better and charge for it.

It groups the segment's top outcomes into one theme, "confirm payee and amount before paying", and compares concepts. A payee verification check addresses three of the theme's outcomes, while a new approval screen addresses one, so verification goes first. Each roadmap item lists the outcomes it targets, and the release will be judged on whether respondents in that segment report higher satisfaction on them.

## Recipe app: positioning from the forces

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a recipe app team has chosen "Cooking for mixed tastes" as its target segment. Interviews with people who switched from other apps show a strong push (arguments over dinner), a clear pull (one plan everyone accepts) and a real anxiety (another app that goes unused).

**Walkthrough:**

The roadmap theme is "decide on a meal everyone will eat", built from the segment's define-step outcomes. Marketing uses the forces for positioning: the message leads with ending the nightly negotiation and answers the anxiety with a short first-week plan that shows value quickly.

A requested feature, a cooking-technique video library, maps to outcomes that scored as overserved. The team records it on its list of declined requests with that reason.

## Platform team: turning outcomes into key results

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a platform team has found that finding the owners of affected services is the most underserved step in releasing a change safely.

**Walkthrough:**

The team writes a key result straight from the outcome statement: reduce the time it takes to identify who owns each service affected by a change. Roadmap items are concepts meant to move it, such as an ownership registry and automatic reviewer suggestions.

It measures the time before the first release and again afterwards with a short survey of engineers. The first concept moves the outcome only slightly, so the team adjusts rather than declaring the theme done because the feature shipped.
