## Example: B2B SaaS project management tool (mid-stage startup, 40-person team)

**Scenario:**

The product team pulled the top 12 outcomes into a working document and identified three themes: "Real-time blocker detection" (4 outcomes, total opportunity 56), "Cross-project visibility" (3 outcomes, total opportunity 41), and "Automated status reporting" (5 outcomes, total opportunity 53). ## Example: Enterprise healthcare analytics platform (large org, 200-person product org)

**Scenario:**

A healthcare analytics company surveyed 400 users across four segments: hospital administrators, clinical researchers, compliance officers, and IT directors. They scored 80 outcome statements across 12 job steps. The executive team wanted a single company-wide product manager roadmap that served all segments.

**Walkthrough:**

The product strategy team ran the opportunity sort at both the aggregate and segment levels. The aggregate top 10 was dominated by outcomes from the "interpret analysis results" job step. But the segment-level view revealed a critical divergence: compliance officers had their top outcomes in the "ensure audit readiness" step, scoring 15-16 on opportunity, while those same outcomes scored only 9 in the aggregate because other segments rated them as low importance. The team proposed a dual-lane roadmap: Lane 1 addressed the aggregate top themes ("Contextual result interpretation" and "Cross-dataset correlation") for the majority segments.

Lane 2 addressed "Continuous audit readiness" specifically for compliance officers, a segment the sales team had identified as a high-growth vertical with 40% higher average contract value. Each lane had its own now/next/later structure. " Engineering allocated 70% capacity to Lane 1 and 30% to Lane 2, reflecting the segment size ratio. Quarterly re-scoring showed that after two releases targeting Lane 1, three of the five targeted outcomes dropped below the opportunity threshold of 12, freeing capacity to shift toward Lane 2 themes.
