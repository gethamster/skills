# FAQ: Shape Up Hill Charts

## What is a hill chart in Shape Up?

A hill chart is a diagram that shows the status of each scope in a project as a dot on a hill. The left side is uphill, where the team is still figuring out its approach, and the right side is downhill, where only execution remains. The book's glossary defines it as a diagram showing the status of work on a spectrum from unknown to known to done. It replaces task counts and percent-complete figures with a judgement about remaining uncertainty.

## How often should the team update the hill chart?

The book does not set a schedule. Update when a scope's position really changes, and make sure updates are frequent enough that a stuck dot shows up within a few days. Keep every update as a snapshot, because the history of movement is the most useful part of the chart for anyone checking on the project.

## Who should move the dots?

The people doing the work on each scope. Singer's hill chart post stresses that the status is human generated, reflecting a real person's feeling about the work at that moment. If a manager moves dots, the chart stops being a signal from the team and turns into another report.

## When should a team start using hill charts?

After the team has mapped its work into scopes. The book's advice for teams new to Shape Up is to first learn to finish one integrated piece early, then map scopes, and only then track those scopes on the hill. A chart with dots for tasks or vague areas produces misleading signals.

## Can hill charts work outside Shape Up?

Yes, as long as the work can be divided into pieces that each have their own unknowns and their own done state. The metaphor applies to any creative work where figuring out the approach is a separate phase from carrying it out. Without a fixed time box, though, a stuck dot carries less urgency, so pair the chart with some deadline.

## What should happen when a dot is stuck?

Ask what is still unknown about that scope and what would get it over the hill. The answer is often a decision someone senior can make quickly, a detail the pitch missed, or a sign that the scope should be split. If the unknown cannot be solved in the time left, cut or rework that part instead of letting it run to the end of the cycle.
