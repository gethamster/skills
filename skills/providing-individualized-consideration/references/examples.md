# Examples: Providing Individualized Consideration and Coaching

## Two engineers, one skill gap

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a team lead has two engineers who both struggle with writing design documents, and the default plan is to send both to the same writing workshop.

**Walkthrough:**

The lead reviews each profile first. One engineer wants to move toward architecture and finds writing tedious but important; the other is anxious about being judged and avoids sharing drafts. For the first, the lead pairs design docs with ownership of a small architectural decision, so writing serves an aspiration. For the second, the lead offers private draft reviews before anything goes to the wider group, letting them build confidence at their own pace.

Both are held to the same document standard. Several weeks later, the lead checks whether each engineer is producing docs with less prompting and adjusts the support accordingly.

## Recognition that actually lands

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a support manager thanks the whole team in a weekly meeting, yet one high performer seems increasingly disengaged.

**Walkthrough:**

In a one-on-one, the manager asks what kind of acknowledgment feels meaningful. The team member says public shout-outs feel hollow, but being trusted with harder escalations would signal real confidence. The manager updates the profile and assigns the next complex escalation to them, stating specifically which past case earned it. Team-wide thanks continue for everyone else.

The manager watches whether the person volunteers for difficult cases more often, which is the sign the recognition matched what they value.

## Repairing a broken commitment

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a manager promised a designer an introduction to a senior mentor and forgot for two months.

**Walkthrough:**

The manager notices the gap while reviewing open commitments before a one-on-one. Rather than hoping it goes unmentioned, the manager opens the meeting by naming the miss and apologizing. The introduction is made that same day, and the manager sets a date to check how the first mentoring session went. From then on, every coaching promise goes into a tracked list reviewed before each meeting.

The designer later raises a new career goal unprompted, a sign that trust has recovered.
