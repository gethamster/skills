# Getting the Benefits of Transformational Leadership

> Coach each team member according to their own motivations, strengths and goals, and keep every coaching commitment you make.

## Overview

Individualized consideration is the part of transformational leadership that happens one person at a time. It means attending to each follower's needs, concerns, development and growth instead of managing everyone identically, as [Santa Clara University's overview of transformational leadership](https://scu.edu/business/blog/leadership-ethics/what-is-transformational-leadership) frames it. For background on the model and its other components, see the [transformational leadership method page](https://tryhamster.com/methods/transformational-leadership). This page covers the practical work: learning what drives each person, giving feedback and coaching that fits them, matching development to their goals, and keeping the promises you make along the way.

This is where many of the benefits of transformational leadership become personal. Vision and inspiration work at team level, but people commit through their individual relationship with you. [Rework's guide to the 4 I's](https://resources.rework.com/libraries/leadership/four-is-of-transformational-leadership) lists individualized consideration as one of the four specific behaviors that define how transformational leaders operate, and [Simply Psychology's explainer](https://simplypsychology.org/what-is-transformational-leadership.html) describes the style as fostering personal growth and built on vision rather than control. If every person gets the same check-in, the same training and the same praise, the growth part never happens.

The practice is associated with mentoring, coaching, support, open communication and tailored growth opportunities, according to the [Civil Air Patrol guide to transformational leadership](https://gocivilairpatrol.com/media/cms/Transformational_Leadership_B14231894F031.pdf). In concrete terms, the inputs are what you know about each person: motivations, strengths, weaknesses, aspirations and development needs. The outputs are a coaching approach, a development plan and a recognition style that differ from person to person, plus a record of commitments you made and kept.

The skill is aimed at managers with direct reports, team leads who coach without formal authority, and anyone running regular one-on-ones. It does not require a formal program. It requires attention, a habit of asking before prescribing, and a reliable way to track what you promised each person.

You will know it is working when team members can describe a development goal that is genuinely theirs, when feedback lands as useful rather than generic, and when people raise problems early because past commitments were honored. You will know it has failed when development plans read identically across the team, when one-on-ones collapse into status updates, or when people stop mentioning their goals because nothing happened the last time they did.

## How It Works

Individualized consideration runs as a loop for each person: learn, tailor, act, follow through, then learn again. Every pass sharpens your picture of the individual and makes the next round of coaching more precise.

**Learn the person.** Build a working profile covering the areas that [Rework](https://resources.rework.com/libraries/leadership/four-is-of-transformational-leadership) and [Simply Psychology](https://simplypsychology.org/what-is-transformational-leadership.html) both point to: motivations, strengths, weaknesses, aspirations and development needs. You fill it through conversation and observation, not a one-time form. Treat it as a hypothesis you revise when the person surprises you.

**Tailor feedback and coaching.** Personalized feedback and coaching replace identical development plans, per [Rework's 4 I's guide](https://resources.rework.com/libraries/leadership/four-is-of-transformational-leadership). The same observation, such as a missed handoff, may call for a direct note with one person and a question-led conversation with another. Asking questions that invite the person's perspective, for example "What approaches have you considered?", before prescribing a method keeps the coaching anchored in their thinking rather than yours.

**Match development.** Development opportunities should fit the individual's needs, goals and capabilities, as the [Civil Air Patrol guide](https://gocivilairpatrol.com/media/cms/Transformational_Leadership_B14231894F031.pdf) notes, and the environment should let people grow and learn at their own pace ([Simply Psychology](https://simplypsychology.org/what-is-transformational-leadership.html)). That rules out assigning the same course to the whole team and calling it development.

**Recognize individually.** Leaders should recognize and celebrate individual contributions and achievements ([Rework](https://resources.rework.com/libraries/leadership/four-is-of-transformational-leadership)). Your profile tells you what kind of recognition a given person actually values, whether that is public credit, a stretch assignment or a quiet specific thank-you.

**Follow through.** Coaching commitments must be completed, because broken promises damage trust ([Rework](https://resources.rework.com/libraries/leadership/four-is-of-transformational-leadership)). Sharing your own development areas and learning experiences can also model vulnerability and make the relationship two-way.

The table contrasts the two ways of managing across the four areas where the difference shows most.

| Area | One-size-fits-all | Individualized consideration |
|---|---|---|
| Feedback | Same format and tone for everyone | Adjusted to how each person uses feedback |
| Development | Generic training for the whole team | Growth matched to goals and capabilities |
| Recognition | Team-wide thanks only | Specific credit for each contribution |
| Commitments | Promises made, rarely tracked | Written down, owned and closed out |

Individualized consideration is not the same as lowering the bar. [Santa Clara University](https://scu.edu/business/blog/leadership-ethics/what-is-transformational-leadership) describes the style as raising performance expectations while investing in individual growth. The expectation stays high; the route to it is fitted to the person.

## Step-by-Step Guide

### Step 1: Map each person's motivations and goals

Before changing how you coach anyone, write down what you currently know about each team member's motivations, strengths, weaknesses, aspirations and development needs, the areas [Rework](https://resources.rework.com/libraries/leadership/four-is-of-transformational-leadership) highlights. Mark which entries you have heard from the person and which are your guesses. Use your next one-on-ones to test the guesses with open questions about what energizes them and where they want to be. Update the profile after each conversation.

The output is a short, living profile per person.

> **Pro tip:** If most of a profile is your guesswork, that person is the first priority for a listening conversation.

### Step 2: Ask before you prescribe

When someone brings a problem, resist handing over your preferred solution. Ask a question that invites their perspective, such as "What approaches have you considered?", which [Rework](https://resources.rework.com/libraries/leadership/four-is-of-transformational-leadership) offers as an example. Their answer tells you how they reason and how much support they need on this kind of task. Only then decide whether to coach, advise or simply confirm their plan.

This keeps coaching tailored instead of formulaic.

> **Pro tip:** Count how often you speak first in one-on-ones; if it is nearly always, flip the order.

### Step 3: Give personalized feedback

Deliver feedback in a way that fits how the individual takes it in, rather than using one script for everyone. Some people want the direct point first, others need context before the critique. Keep the substance and standard identical; adjust the framing, timing and level of detail. Personalized feedback and coaching, rather than identical plans, is central to the practice ([Simply Psychology](https://simplypsychology.org/what-is-transformational-leadership.html)).

Check afterwards whether the feedback changed anything, which tells you whether the framing worked.

### Step 4: Build a tailored development plan

For each person, choose development opportunities that match their needs, goals and capabilities, as the [Civil Air Patrol guide](https://gocivilairpatrol.com/media/cms/Transformational_Leadership_B14231894F031.pdf) recommends. Link each item to an aspiration from their profile so it is recognizably theirs. Set a pace the person can sustain, since the aim is an environment where they grow at their own speed. Review the plan together and let them edit it.

If two plans on your team look interchangeable, one of them is not tailored.

> **Pro tip:** Ask the person to explain their plan back to you in their own words; if they cannot, it is still your plan.

### Step 5: Recognize contributions individually

Name specific contributions and achievements for the person who made them, rather than folding everything into team-wide thanks. Recognizing and celebrating individual contributions is a named behavior in [Rework's guide](https://resources.rework.com/libraries/leadership/four-is-of-transformational-leadership). Use the profile to pick the form of recognition that person values. Be specific about what they did and why it mattered.

Generic praise teaches nothing and is quickly discounted.

### Step 6: Track and close coaching commitments

Every time you promise something in a coaching conversation, record it with a date. Review open commitments before each one-on-one and report progress without being asked. If you cannot deliver, say so early and explain why. Broken promises damage trust, per [Rework](https://resources.rework.com/libraries/leadership/four-is-of-transformational-leadership), and trust is what makes the rest of the coaching land.

A clean commitment record is the most visible signal that your attention is real.

> **Pro tip:** Open each one-on-one by reviewing your own open commitments before asking about theirs.

## Best Practices

- Keep a short written profile per person and update it after every one-on-one. Memory drifts toward the loudest team members, and a written record keeps quieter people from being coached by default.
- Hold the standard constant and vary the route. [Santa Clara University](https://scu.edu/business/blog/leadership-ethics/what-is-transformational-leadership) pairs raised performance expectations with investment in individual growth, so tailoring should never mean a lower bar for some people.
- Share your own development areas and what you are learning. [Rework](https://resources.rework.com/libraries/leadership/four-is-of-transformational-leadership) notes this models vulnerability, which makes it easier for others to admit gaps.
- Let people set their own pace within agreed goals. A supportive environment where individuals learn at their own pace ([Simply Psychology](https://simplypsychology.org/what-is-transformational-leadership.html)) produces development that sticks rather than compliance that fades.
- Attend to concerns and well-being, not only output. Individualized consideration covers needs and concerns as well as growth, so a one-on-one that only reviews tasks has skipped half the job.
- Use open communication as the default channel. Mentoring, coaching and open communication travel together in the practice ([Civil Air Patrol](https://gocivilairpatrol.com/media/cms/Transformational_Leadership_B14231894F031.pdf)), and people only share motivations when they expect honesty back.

## Common Mistakes

- **Managing every team member the same way despite clear differences in needs, motivations and aspirations.**: Build and use a profile for each person, and let it change how you give feedback, assign work and recognize effort. Consistency belongs in standards and fairness, not in identical treatment.
- **Offering generic training in place of development suited to what motivates the individual.**: Tie each development item to a goal the person has named. [Simply Psychology](https://simplypsychology.org/what-is-transformational-leadership.html) frames the style around personal growth, which a one-size course catalog rarely delivers.
- **Focusing only on performance while ignoring the person's growth, concerns and longer-term development.**: Reserve part of every one-on-one for development and well-being. Performance conversations still happen, but they sit inside a relationship that also looks ahead.
- **Making commitments during coaching conversations and then not completing them.**: Record every promise with a date and review the list before each meeting. If something slips, raise it yourself before the other person has to ask.
- **Treating the first read of a person as permanent.**: Revisit motivations and aspirations periodically, especially after role changes or setbacks. Goals shift, and coaching built on an outdated profile quietly becomes generic again.
