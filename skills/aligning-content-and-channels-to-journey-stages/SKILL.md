# Customer Journey Content Strategy by Stage

> Build a customer journey content strategy that matches each asset, message and channel to what customers need in each of the five stages.

## Overview

A customer journey content strategy assigns every piece of content to the stage where it helps the customer most. Someone in awareness is learning that a problem can be solved. Someone in consideration is comparing options. Someone about to buy needs to remove the last doubts. A retained customer needs to get more value from what they bought, and an advocate needs something easy to share. One asset rarely serves all of these, and content written for no stage in particular tends to serve none.

The [five-stage customer journey framework](../../methods/five-stage-customer-journey-framework/METHOD.md) gives the stages. This skill turns them into a working content plan: an audit of what exists, a map of which customer questions each stage raises, a choice of channel for each question, and a next step that moves the customer forward. The output shows where content is piling up, usually awareness, and where it is missing, usually retention and advocacy.

Intent is the organizing idea. Avinash Kaushik's See-Think-Do-Care model groups audiences by [how much commercial intent they have](https://www.kaushik.net/avinash/see-think-do-care-win-content-marketing-measurement/), from people with none to loyal repeat customers, and argues that content and measurement should differ for each group. The five stages map closely onto those clusters, and borrowing Kaushik's lens stops teams from judging awareness content by sales it was never meant to produce.

Use this skill when the content calendar is full but conversion or retention is flat, when sales or success teams build their own material because marketing content does not fit their conversations, or when you are planning content for a new product or segment.

## How It Works

Each stage raises a different set of customer questions, and content exists to answer them. In awareness the questions are about the problem: what is going wrong, is it common, can it be fixed. In consideration they are about options: what approaches exist, how do they compare, what would it take to adopt this one. In purchase they are about risk: what does it cost, what happens after signing, who else has done it. In retention they are about use: how do I do this, how do I get more out of it. In advocacy they are about sharing: what can I show a colleague, how do I refer someone.

Kaushik's clusters line up with this. See is the audience with [no commercial intent](https://www.kaushik.net/avinash/see-think-do-care-win-content-marketing-measurement/), Think has weak intent, Do has strong intent and Care is existing customers who buy repeatedly. His framework treats Care as its own audience with its own content, which is the part most content plans skip. The 5A path in Marketing 4.0 makes a similar point from the other side: its Ask stage is where consumers [seek further information before a purchase decision](https://www.markplusinc.com/en/featured-insights/publications/customer-journey-5a), which is where comparison and proof content belongs.

Channels follow the question. Awareness content has to go where people already spend attention, such as search, social feeds, communities and events. Consideration content is often pulled rather than pushed: comparison pages, demos, trials, case studies, reviews. Purchase content sits close to the transaction, in pricing pages, proposals and checkout. Retention content lives inside the product and in lifecycle email and help centers. Advocacy content is built to be forwarded: short case studies, referral links, templates a customer can show a colleague.

Every asset also needs a next step that fits the stage it serves. An awareness article that ends with "book a demo" asks for a jump most readers are not ready to make. A softer next step, such as a related guide or a comparison page, keeps them moving. The plan records that next step so gaps in the path become visible.

Measurement follows intent too. Awareness content is judged by reach and engagement, consideration content by movement to trial or demo, purchase content by conversion, retention content by feature adoption and fewer support requests, and advocacy content by shares and referrals.

## Step-by-Step Guide

### Step 1: Inventory existing content

List every asset the segment might see: articles, pages, videos, emails, sales decks, help articles, case studies. Record the channel, the owner, a rough measure of use and the call to action each one ends with. Include material that sales and success built outside marketing, because it often reveals what customers actually ask for. Keep the inventory in the same table format as the touchpoint inventory if one exists.

### Step 2: Write the customer questions for each stage

For the chosen segment, list the questions customers ask in each stage, using interview notes, sales call notes, support tickets and search queries. Write them in the customer's words. A good list has questions that clearly belong to one stage, such as "can I import from my current tool" for consideration. These questions are the backbone of the plan, so spend time on them.

### Step 3: Assign each asset to one stage and one question

Tag each asset with the stage and the question it answers best. Some assets will not answer any question on the list. Mark those as candidates to retire or rewrite. Some questions will have no asset, and those are your gaps. Resist tagging one asset to several stages to make coverage look better.

### Step 4: Choose the channel for each question

For each question, decide where the customer is when they ask it. Awareness questions happen in search and communities, consideration questions on comparison pages and in sales calls, retention questions inside the product. Place the asset in that channel rather than in the channel your team finds easiest to publish to. Note any question that currently has content but in the wrong place.

### Step 5: Set the next step for every asset

Give each asset one call to action that fits its stage and points to the next stage's content. Check the chain end to end: an awareness article should lead somewhere a curious reader would go, not straight to a sales form. Fix dead ends, where an asset offers no next step at all. The finished plan should let you trace a path from first article to renewal.

### Step 6: Fill the gaps in priority order

Rank the uncovered questions by how often they come up and by the stage metric they affect. Retention and advocacy gaps often rank high because they are neglected and affect existing revenue. Brief new assets with the stage, the question, the channel and the next step already decided. Retire or rewrite assets that answer no one.

### Step 7: Measure each stage by its own standard

Set a signal for each stage's content that fits its intent, such as engaged reach for awareness, progression to trial for consideration and feature adoption for retention. Review content performance by stage each quarter. When an asset underperforms, check first whether it sits in the wrong stage or channel before rewriting it.

## Best Practices

- **Write from the customer's question.** An asset that starts from a real question is easier to place, easier to measure and easier to find. Assets that start from what the company wants to say drift toward the purchase stage regardless of audience.
- **Give retention content a named owner.** Help articles, onboarding emails and in-product guides are content even when marketing does not produce them. Without an owner they go stale first.
- **Match the call to action to intent.** Low-intent readers respond to low-commitment next steps. Save demo requests for content aimed at people already comparing options.
- **Use advocate language in awareness.** The words customers use when they recommend you are often better headlines than the ones the team writes. Collect them from reviews and referral notes.
- **Keep one stage per asset.** Multi-stage assets try to satisfy everyone and end up vague. Write two focused pieces instead.
- **Retire content deliberately.** Old assets that answer nobody dilute search results and confuse customers. Remove or redirect them as part of each review.

## Common Mistakes

- **Pouring everything into awareness**: A plan that measures itself by traffic fills the top of the funnel and leaves customers without help later. Audit coverage by stage and fund the gaps.
- **Judging awareness content by sales**: An article for people with no commercial intent will rarely close a deal. Measure it by reach and progression to the next stage instead.
- **Asking for the sale too early**: Ending every asset with a demo request trains low-intent readers to leave. Use a next step that fits the stage.
- **Ignoring content sales and success already built**: Decks and help articles built by customer-facing teams show real questions. Fold them into the plan rather than duplicating them.
- **Publishing where it is convenient**: Content on the company blog does not help a customer who is stuck inside the product. Put the answer where the question is asked.
