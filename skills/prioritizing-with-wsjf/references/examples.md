# Examples: Prioritizing Work Using WSJF

## Ranking portfolio epics before committing capacity

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a portfolio team has five epics competing for next year's capacity, and two executives each insist their epic must go first.

**Walkthrough:**

The portfolio lead scores the five epics column by column with business owners and enterprise architects present. One executive's epic scores highest on value but is very large; the other's scores moderately on value but high on time sensitivity because of a partner contract window. Dividing gives the contract-driven epic a WSJF of roughly twice the other's, using made-up scores of 18 over 5 against 21 over 13. Instead of leaving the larger epic behind, the group identifies an MVP slice sized at 5 that keeps much of its value.

Both the contract epic and the MVP slice move into analysis, and the rationale sheet gives each executive a clear view of why.

## Getting an enabler onto the roadmap

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: an architect has argued for three planning cycles that a test-automation enabler should be prioritized, but it keeps losing to customer-facing features.

**Walkthrough:**

In the next WSJF session, the team scores risk reduction and opportunity enablement as a separate column instead of folding it into value. The enabler scores low on direct value, say 2, but high on risk reduction and enablement, say 13, because it shortens validation for every later feature. Its job size is small at 3, so its WSJF lands near the top of the list. Product management accepts the result because the rationale shows which downstream features it unblocks.

The enabler enters the next PI, and later cycles show those features scoring smaller job sizes.

## Re-scoring after a deadline moves

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a regulatory change is announced with an earlier effective date than expected, midway between planning cycles.

**Walkthrough:**

The product manager reconvenes a short session and re-scores only the time-sensitivity column for all features. The compliance feature's time sensitivity rises from 3 to 20 on the made-up scale, lifting its cost of delay and pushing it from sixth to first. Two features drop in rank, and the score sheet makes the reason obvious to their sponsors. The team also checks whether the compliance feature still fits one ART in one PI and finds it does.

The updated ranking goes to business owners before the next planning event, with the changed assumption flagged.
