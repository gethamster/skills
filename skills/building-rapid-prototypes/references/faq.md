# FAQ: Building Rapid Prototypes

## How rough can a design thinking prototype be?

Rough enough that you would not mind throwing it away, but clear enough that someone can use or react to it without your explanation. The d.school calls for prototypes that are understandable to another person without polishing details irrelevant to the learning goal. A useful check is to hand it to a colleague who has not seen the idea. If they can attempt the task, it is ready to test.

## What materials should I use for low-fidelity prototypes?

Use whatever is immediately available: paper, pens, sticky notes, slides, cardboard, tape, and simple household objects. The goal is speed, so avoid materials that require ordering, training, or specialist tools. Digital tools are fine when they are just as fast for your team, such as rough slides for an interface. Switch to higher-fidelity materials only when the question you are testing depends on realism.

## When should I use role-play instead of a physical prototype?

Use role-play when the idea is mainly an interaction between people, services, or environments rather than an object or screen. Services, handoffs, and support conversations are hard to capture on paper but easy to act out. Role-play reveals timing problems, awkward moments, and gaps in who does what. Combine it with simple props when a physical element is part of the experience.

## How many users do I need to test a rapid prototype?

Test with as few people as it takes to see a clear pattern, then stop and decide. For early rounds, a handful of sessions, for example three to five, often surfaces the biggest breakdowns. If results conflict or seem ambiguous, run another round rather than debating. The aim is fast learning, not statistical proof.

## What do I do if users like the prototype but cannot use it?

Trust the behavior over the compliments. Stated approval often reflects politeness toward the person testing, while hesitation and errors show where the design actually fails. Treat the usability problems as the finding, refine those parts, and retest. If the core idea is appealing but the execution keeps failing, try a different prototype form for the next round.
