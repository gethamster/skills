# FAQ: Building Rapid Prototypes

## What is the difference between a prototype and a minimum viable product?

A rapid prototype exists to answer a question and is usually thrown away once it has done so. A minimum viable product is a working release meant to deliver real value to real customers while you learn. Prototypes come earlier, cost far less and can be fake behind the scenes. Many concepts go through several rounds of prototypes before anyone builds something that could be called a product.

## How rough can a prototype be and still be useful?

It can be as rough as a hand-drawn sketch, as long as the people testing it can understand what it represents and react to it. The test is whether users can engage with the question you care about. If they spend the session asking what the drawing is supposed to be, raise fidelity a little. If they engage easily, stay rough.

## How many people should test each prototype?

There is no fixed rule, and a small number is usually enough to reveal major problems with a rough concept. Aim for enough people that you start hearing the same issues repeated, for example, a handful per round. Be careful about relying only on a small, convenient group, since the people who are easiest to reach may not represent everyone the design must serve. Spread testing across different kinds of users over several rounds.

## When should we move from low fidelity to high fidelity?

Move up when rough versions stop teaching you anything new, or when the remaining questions depend on detail that rough artifacts cannot show, such as exact wording, timing or integration with real operations. By then, the core value and flow should already be confirmed. Raising fidelity before that point usually means paying for polish on an idea that may still change.

## Can we prototype services and processes, not just screens?

Yes, and low-fidelity methods often work especially well for services. Role-play, storyboards, mock counters and scripted phone calls let you test handoffs, timing and emotions without building anything. Invite the staff who would deliver the service to take part, because they notice operational problems that designers miss.
