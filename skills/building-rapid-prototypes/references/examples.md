# Examples: Building Rapid Prototypes

## Paper prototype for a clinic check-in flow

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a small team is redesigning patient check-in at a community clinic after research showed patients arriving confused about which forms to complete.

**Walkthrough:**

The team's riskiest assumption is that patients will understand a single-page summary of what to bring and what to fill in. They sketch three versions of the summary on paper in an afternoon. At the clinic, they hand each version to a handful of waiting patients and ask them to explain what they need to do next. Two versions produce wrong answers about which form comes first, while the third, which uses numbered pictures, is understood by nearly everyone.

The team drops the two failing layouts and plans a clickable version of the third to test on a tablet.

## Role-playing a new service handoff

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a utility company wants to test a concept where call-center agents transfer billing disputes directly to a field technician instead of opening a ticket.

**Walkthrough:**

Rather than building software, the team sets up two desks and asks an agent and a technician to act out the handoff while a colleague plays a frustrated customer. They run the scene several times with different customer stories. The role-play shows that the technician lacks the account history needed to respond, so the handoff stalls every time. The team revises the concept to include a short shared summary and reruns the scene the same day.

Only after the revised handoff works in role-play do they commission a simple mockup of the summary screen.

## Fake-door test for a savings feature

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a personal finance app team believes users want an automatic round-up savings feature but is unsure anyone would switch it on.

**Walkthrough:**

The team adds a basic slide-based mockup showing a button labeled start saving spare change to a prototype of the home screen. In sessions with a few target users, they ask people to explore the screen and narrate what catches their attention. Most participants ignore the button, and those who notice it worry about losing control of their money. The team concludes that desirability is the real risk, not usability.

Their next prototype is a storyboard explaining the feature with clear limits, tested before any engineering work begins.
