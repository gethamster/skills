# FAQ: Structuring Roadmap Timeframes

## How far ahead should a product roadmap look?

Pichler finds a horizon of about twelve months works well, provided the product strategy covers at least that period. His broader rule is to look only as far ahead as you can realistically see without speculating. A brand-new product may need a much shorter horizon until users have responded to the first release.

## Should every goal be a quarter long?

No. Quarterly goals are a convenient default and fit well with quarterly OKRs, but Pichler's guideline is that roadmap goals should take between six weeks and four months. Size each timeframe to the outcome, and split or merge goals that fall outside that range.

## Should a product roadmap show dates?

On an internal roadmap, usually yes: Pichler argues that dates or narrow timeframes help capture deadlines and check whether the plan is realistic. On an external roadmap, he advises against specific dates and suggests coarse periods such as this year and next year, or no dates at all. The choice depends on who reads it and how they will use it.

## What is the difference between a roadmap and a release plan?

In Pichler's usage, a release plan forecasts how one major release is developed and usually covers the next three to six months, while the roadmap covers about a year and several releases. The release plan holds backlog items and tracks progress; the roadmap holds goals and high-level features. Derive the release plan from the roadmap and keep the two in sync.

## How does a now-next-later roadmap relate to timeframes?

A now-next-later roadmap, described by Janna Bastow of ProdPad, replaces the timeline with three horizons. Pichler mentions using such a grid to sequence releases on an external roadmap. It can serve as the external view of a GO roadmap, with the internal view keeping its dates.
