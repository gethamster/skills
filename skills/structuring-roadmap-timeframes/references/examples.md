# Examples: Structuring Roadmap Timeframes

## A seasonal deadline on an internal roadmap

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a team building a tax-filing app knows most of its users arrive in a short season each spring. Last year a key improvement shipped after the season had ended, and it made no difference until the following year.

**Walkthrough:**

The product manager marks the start of the filing season as a hard deadline on the internal roadmap and makes the goal before it, "help returning filers finish in one sitting and raise renewal", fixed in date. The team agrees that scope is the flexible corner: if progress is slow, features are cut, and the date holds.

The goals after the season get quarterly timeframes, and the last column only says "second half". The external roadmap shows the same sequence with no dates at all, just "for this filing season" and "later this year". When one feature turns out to be larger than expected, it is dropped from the seasonal goal without debate, because the flexible corner was agreed in advance.

## Breaking the quarterly habit

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a team building a photo-editing app plans every goal as exactly one quarter, because the company runs quarterly planning. A goal to reduce crashes on older phones is clearly a six-week job, and a goal to launch a collaboration mode clearly needs more than a quarter.

**Walkthrough:**

The team keeps quarterly reporting but stops forcing goals into it. The crash goal gets six weeks, followed by a goal to speed up exporting for the rest of that quarter. The collaboration goal is split into two: first let users share an edit link for comments, then let them edit together.

The date row now shows a mix of months and quarters. Leadership initially asks why, and the product manager explains that each timeframe matches the outcome's size. At the end of the quarter the crash goal has been met and measured, which would have been lost in a padded quarterly goal.

## Shortening the horizon for a new product

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a start-up is about to release the first version of a scheduling assistant. The founders ask for a year-long roadmap to show investors.

**Walkthrough:**

The product lead points out that the strategy has not yet been tested with real users, so anything beyond the first release would be speculation. Instead of a year of columns, the roadmap shows one detailed goal for the launch and two coarse goals labelled "after launch, subject to what we learn".

After the first two months of usage data, the team reviews the roadmap and fills in the next goal with a timeframe and metrics. The investors see a plan that changes when evidence arrives, which proves more convincing than a detailed year that nobody believes.
