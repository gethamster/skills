# Examples: Writing Benefit-Driven Headlines That Convert

## A time-tracking tool for design studios

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a time-tracking product for small design studios has the homepage headline "The Future of Creative Time Management." Visitors in comprehension checks cannot say what it does.

**Walkthrough:**

The writer picks the benefit studio owners mention most in interviews: knowing which projects lose money before the invoice goes out. Customer quotes use phrases like "we only find out we went over budget at the end."

Draft options include a category line ("Time tracking for design studios"), an outcome line ("See which projects are going over budget while you can still fix them"), and a question line about finding out too late. The clarity filter removes the original and a pun on "time well spent." The outcome line leads, the category line becomes the subhead's opening, and the team tests the outcome line against the plain category line.

## An email subject line from a headline table

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a meal-kit company is announcing a new family plan by email. The draft subject line is "Introducing FamilyFlex."

**Walkthrough:**

"FamilyFlex" is an internal plan name, so it tells the reader nothing when it appears alone in an inbox. The writer goes back to the benefit table, where the family plan's main benefit is that parents can pick different dishes for adults and children in one order.

The new options state that benefit directly, and one keeps the plan name at the end for recognition. The writer reads each option alone, as it will appear in the inbox, and cuts one that only makes sense after opening the email. Two options go into a subject line test with the send split evenly.

## A B2B security product for IT managers

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a device management product sells to IT managers at mid-sized companies. The landing page headline reads "Peace of Mind, Delivered."

**Walkthrough:**

The writer notes that IT managers search by category and judge products on specifics. The feature-led control ("Device management for teams of fifty to five hundred") states the category and the audience. The benefit option names the task that goes away, setting up a new laptop remotely on the day a hire starts.

Neither line is clever, and both pass the stranger test. The team runs the benefit headline with the category line as its subhead, and keeps the pure category line as the alternative for the next test.
