# How to Design Validated Learning Lean Startup Experiments

> Turn product assumptions into falsifiable hypotheses with preset thresholds so each experiment yields evidence you can act on.

## At a Glance

| Field | Value |
|-------|-------|
| Difficulty | Intermediate |
| Time to Learn | 2-4 hours to design one experiment brief |
| Outcome | A one-page experiment brief naming the riskiest assumption, a falsifiable hypothesis, the metric, the pass threshold, the audience and the decision each result triggers. |
| Prerequisites | A written product or business idea with a stated plan, Access to people in the target customer segment, A way to observe behavior, such as analytics, a sign-up flow or a payment step |
| Part of | [Lean Startup Framework](../../methods/lean-startup-framework/METHOD.md) |

## Overview

Validated learning is the unit of progress in Lean Startup, and it only happens when an experiment is designed to produce evidence the team cannot argue away afterward. For background on the framework itself, see the [Lean Startup Framework](https://tryhamster.com/methods/lean-startup-framework) method page. This page covers the design work that comes before anything is built: deciding what to test, how to phrase the test, and what result will count.

The starting premise comes from how the [Lean Enterprise Institute defines Lean Startup](https://lean.org/lexicon-terms/lean-startup): ideas for businesses and products are hypotheses that must be validated by rapid experimentation in the marketplace. That framing changes the job of a product team. A roadmap item stops being a commitment and becomes a claim about customers that can be right or wrong, and the experiment exists to find out which.

Designing a good experiment comes down to four decisions. First, write down the assumptions the plan depends on, covering customer, problem, solution, acquisition channel, pricing and revenue model, as [Umbrex's Build-Measure-Learn guide](https://umbrex.com/resources/frameworks/organization-frameworks/lean-startup-build-measure-learn-loop) recommends. Second, focus on the assumption that carries the biggest risk, which is the second step in [the Lean Startup Co. process](https://leanstartup.co/resources/articles/lean-startup-method). Third, rewrite that assumption as a falsifiable hypothesis that predicts observable customer behavior. Fourth, set the success metric and decision threshold before launch so the standard cannot drift once data arrives.

This design step matters because it is where the method most often breaks in practice. A review of Lean Startup research notes that critics see experimentation as a major problem area because practitioners may formulate weak hypotheses, and that [Felin and colleagues challenged treating a hypothesis as merely a guess](https://hilarispublisher.com/open-access/lean-startup-as-an-entrepreneurial-strategy-limitations-outcomes-and-learnings-for-practitioners-52478.html). The same review cites a survey of mobile startups in which [62% of respondents called the MVP a vital concept while 82% named defining and designing it a disadvantage](https://hilarispublisher.com/open-access/lean-startup-as-an-entrepreneurial-strategy-limitations-outcomes-and-learnings-for-practitioners-52478.html), a sign that teams accept the need to test but struggle to decide what to test and how.

The output of this skill is a short experiment brief: the assumption, the hypothesis, the metric, the threshold, the audience and the action each outcome triggers. That brief is the input to [building a minimum viable product](https://tryhamster.com/skills/building-minimum-viable-products) and to [running the Build-Measure-Learn cycle](https://tryhamster.com/skills/running-build-measure-learn-cycles). If you cannot fill in every line of the brief, the experiment is not ready to run.

## How It Works

An experiment produces validated learning when there is an unbroken chain from a business assumption to a decision. The chain has five links: assumption, hypothesis, metric, threshold, decision. Break any link and the result becomes an anecdote. The general sequence described by [the Lean Startup Co.](https://leanstartup.co/resources/articles/lean-startup-method) follows this chain: identify assumptions, home in on the riskiest one, determine how to test it, formulate a hypothesis about the test, run it, review results, incorporate the findings and iterate.

**Choosing what to test.** Risk has two dimensions. An assumption is worth testing first when it is highly uncertain and would sink the plan if false, which is how [Yu-kai Chou's Build-Measure-Learn explainer](https://yukaichou.com/gamification-analysis/lean-startup-ries-build-measure-learn-mvp) and the Lean Startup Co. frame the riskiest assumption. An assumption that is uncertain but cheap to be wrong about can wait. One that is consequential but already well evidenced needs no experiment. Teams drift toward comfortable tests because they are easy to pass, so the ranking has to be explicit and written down.

**Making the hypothesis falsifiable.** A falsifiable hypothesis names who, what behavior, how much and by when. A useful template: "We believe [segment] will [observable action] at [rate] within [time window], because [assumption]." For example, "We believe independent bookkeepers will connect a bank feed within their first session at a rate of at least 30% within two weeks." [Umbrex's guide](https://umbrex.com/resources/frameworks/organization-frameworks/lean-startup-build-measure-learn-loop) recommends converting each important assumption into exactly this kind of behavioral prediction. If no plausible result could prove the statement wrong, it is a belief, not a hypothesis.

**Fixing the standard in advance.** The metric and the decision threshold are set before launch so the team compares observed behavior with the original hypothesis rather than moving the goalposts, a point [Umbrex](https://umbrex.com/resources/frameworks/organization-frameworks/lean-startup-build-measure-learn-loop) makes directly. Without a success criterion, [the wondelai Lean Startup skill](https://tessl.io/registry/skills/github/wondelai/skills/lean-startup) warns, it becomes hard to say what results mean. A practical format is a three-way rule: a pass line, a fail line, and an inconclusive band between them that triggers a retest.

**Measuring behavior, not opinion.** The metric should count what customers do. The same wondelai skill notes that learning what customers want comes from experiments on real behavior rather than feature requests, surveys or focus groups, because people mispredict their own actions. Stated interest can shape a hypothesis; it cannot validate one.

**Using A/B tests when you have traffic.** When enough people flow through a product or page, a controlled split isolates cause and effect better than a before-and-after comparison. A [2025 debate article on Lean Startup](https://tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/13662716.2025.2476431) cites Koning, Hasan and Chatterji's 2022 study finding that A/B testing led to a 10-100% increase in customer visits to startup websites and that startups using it launched more new products. The same article cites Kotha and colleagues' 2023 ([source](https://tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/13662716.2025.2476431)) study linking Lean Startup training to greater venture growth. Treat these as initial evidence, not a guarantee: reviews describe experimentation and hypothesis design as [implementation-sensitive practices](https://hilarispublisher.com/open-access/lean-startup-as-an-entrepreneurial-strategy-limitations-outcomes-and-learnings-for-practitioners.pdf) whose outcomes depend on how rigorously they are applied.

## Step-by-Step Guide

### Step 1: Map the assumptions

Take the current plan, pitch or roadmap and list every statement that must be true for it to work. Group them under customer, problem, solution, acquisition channel, pricing and revenue model, the categories [Umbrex](https://umbrex.com/resources/frameworks/organization-frameworks/lean-startup-build-measure-learn-loop) suggests. Write each as a plain sentence starting with "We assume". Include assumptions that feel obvious, because obvious assumptions are the ones nobody has checked.

The output is a flat list, typically a few dozen lines for a new product.

> **Pro tip:** Ask each team member to write assumptions separately before merging lists. Silent listing surfaces beliefs that a group discussion would smooth over.

### Step 2: Rank by risk and evidence

Score each assumption on two axes: how uncertain it is and how badly the plan fails if it is false. The assumption scoring high on both is your test target, matching the riskiest-assumption step in [the Lean Startup Co. process](https://leanstartup.co/resources/articles/lean-startup-method). Note what evidence already exists for each, since a well-evidenced assumption does not need a new experiment. Resist choosing the assumption that is easiest to test.

If the top pick feels uncomfortable to test, that is usually a sign you picked correctly.

> **Pro tip:** Use a simple 2x2 of uncertainty against impact on a whiteboard. Only the top-right quadrant earns an experiment this cycle.

### Step 3: Write the falsifiable hypothesis

Rewrite the chosen assumption as a prediction of observable behavior from a named segment within a time window. Use a template such as "We believe [segment] will [action] at [rate] within [time]." Check falsifiability by writing down a concrete result that would prove it wrong; if you cannot, sharpen the statement. Keep one hypothesis per experiment so a result points to one cause.

This step addresses the weak-hypothesis problem that [reviews of Lean Startup practice](https://hilarispublisher.com/open-access/lean-startup-as-an-entrepreneurial-strategy-limitations-outcomes-and-learnings-for-practitioners-52478.html) identify as a major failure point.

> **Pro tip:** Replace vague verbs like "like", "want" or "engage with" with actions you can count, such as "pay", "return", "invite" or "complete setup".

### Step 4: Set the metric and decision thresholds

Pick the single behavioral metric that the hypothesis predicts, and define how it will be captured. Set a pass line, a fail line and an inconclusive band, for example pass at 30% or above, fail below 15%, retest in between. Record these before launch, as [Umbrex](https://umbrex.com/resources/frameworks/organization-frameworks/lean-startup-build-measure-learn-loop) advises, so nobody can reinterpret the standard after seeing data. Also set a minimum sample or duration so the test does not end the moment a number looks good.

For guidance on picking the metric itself, see [choosing actionable over vanity metrics](https://tryhamster.com/skills/choosing-actionable-over-vanity-metrics).

> **Pro tip:** Base thresholds on what the business needs to be viable, not on what you expect to see. A threshold set to match your forecast only tests your optimism.

### Step 5: Choose the smallest test that produces the behavior

Decide how the target customers will encounter something they can act on: a landing page with a real sign-up or payment step, a manual service, a prototype, or a split test. Pick the cheapest option that still produces the behavior named in the hypothesis. If the product already has steady traffic, an A/B test gives a cleaner causal read, and a [2025 debate article](https://tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/13662716.2025.2476431) cites research linking A/B testing to a 10-100% increase in startup website visits. Make sure the audience is the segment named in the hypothesis, not colleagues or friends.

Hand the brief to whoever builds the MVP with the scope limited to what the test needs.

### Step 6: Pre-commit decisions and log the result

Next to each threshold, write the action it triggers: persevere, pivot, or revise and retest. Get the decision owner to agree to these rules before launch. When the test ends, record the observed number, which band it fell into and the action taken, alongside the original brief. This log is what turns a data point into validated learning the whole team can see.

Feed the result into the next round of assumption ranking, since one answered question often promotes a new riskiest assumption.

> **Pro tip:** Keep every brief and result in one shared log. Reviewing past experiments quarterly shows whether your hypotheses are getting sharper or staying vague.

## Best Practices

- Test one assumption per experiment. When a test bundles several changes, a pass or fail cannot be traced to any single belief, and the team learns nothing it can act on.
- Write the threshold as a business requirement, not a forecast. Ask what level of behavior would make the plan viable, then set the pass line there, because a threshold matched to expectations only confirms what you hoped.
- Prefer paid or costly actions over free ones as evidence. A payment, a preorder or time spent on setup is harder to fake than a click, which is why observed behavior outranks stated interest in [the wondelai Lean Startup skill](https://tessl.io/registry/skills/github/wondelai/skills/lean-startup).
- Define an inconclusive band explicitly. Results that land between pass and fail are common, and naming a retest rule in advance stops teams from rounding ambiguous numbers toward the answer they wanted.
- Use A/B testing when traffic allows a controlled split. It separates the effect of your change from seasonality and channel noise, and [research cited in a 2025 debate article](https://tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/13662716.2025.2476431) found startups using it launched more new products.
- Keep the experiment brief to one page. If the assumption, hypothesis, metric, threshold, audience and decision do not fit, the experiment is too broad and should be split.

## Common Mistakes

- **Testing the comfortable assumption instead of the risky one, such as validating that users like a visual design before checking whether anyone will pay.** — Rank assumptions by uncertainty and impact and test the top one first, as [Yu-kai Chou's explainer](https://yukaichou.com/gamification-analysis/lean-startup-ries-build-measure-learn-mvp) stresses. If the riskiest assumption fails, the comfortable ones do not matter.
- **Writing a hypothesis that cannot fail, such as "Customers will find value in faster reporting."** — Name the segment, the countable action, the rate and the time window. Then write down the result that would disprove it; if none exists, rewrite the hypothesis.
- **Launching without a success criterion and deciding afterward what counts as good.** — Set the metric and thresholds before launch, as [Umbrex](https://umbrex.com/resources/frameworks/organization-frameworks/lean-startup-build-measure-learn-loop) recommends. Without them, as the [wondelai skill](https://tessl.io/registry/skills/github/wondelai/skills/lean-startup) notes, results are hard to interpret and easy to rationalize.
- **Treating survey answers or interview enthusiasm as validation.** — Use what people say to form hypotheses, then validate with what they do. Build the test around an action with some cost to the customer, such as signing up, paying or returning.
- **Stopping an A/B test the moment one variant pulls ahead.** — Fix the sample size or duration in the brief before launch and run to that point. Early leads often reverse, and peeking turns a controlled test back into guesswork.
