## Overview

Customer advocacy is the fifth stage of the [five-stage customer journey framework](../../methods/five-stage-customer-journey-framework/METHOD.md): satisfied customers recommending you, reviewing you, speaking for you and bringing in new customers. It is the stage where the relationship starts paying back into awareness and consideration, because a recommendation from a peer carries more weight than anything the company says about itself.

Advocacy cannot be manufactured. It grows out of the earlier stages, and programs only amplify what is already there. Bain's description of the Net Promoter system says promoters are the customers who [stay longer, buy more and generate most referrals](https://www.netpromotersystem.com/about/measuring-your-net-promoter-score/), which is another way of saying that advocacy depends on retention. If customers are not getting value, a referral bonus will produce either nothing or referrals from people who want the bonus.

The economic case for a program rests on the value of referred customers. A study of a German bank's referral program tracked about 10,000 customers for almost three years and found that [referred customers were at least 16% more valuable](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2012/04/Schmitt-Skiera-vandenBulte-2011-Referral-Programs-Customer-Value.pdf) than comparable customers acquired in other ways, with higher retention. The same authors found that the size of the advantage varied by segment and recommended a selective approach. That is a finding about one bank, not a benchmark, but it is a good reason to measure the value of referred customers in your own business before scaling a program.

This skill covers four forms of advocacy: referrals, public reviews, references and case studies for sales, and community participation. It shows how to find likely advocates, when to ask, what to offer, and how to measure the results.

## How It Works

An advocacy program has three parts: identifying who is likely to advocate, triggering the ask at a moment when they are most willing, and making the action easy.

Identification usually starts with a satisfaction signal. Net Promoter Score is the common one: customers rate how likely they are to recommend you on a zero-to-ten scale, and Bain classes those who answer [9 or 10 as promoters and 0 to 6 as detractors](https://www.netpromotersystem.com/about/measuring-your-net-promoter-score/). Behavioral signals work too and are often more reliable: heavy product use, renewal without negotiation, unprompted praise in support conversations, attendance at events. Combining a stated signal with a behavioral one filters out customers who say nice things but do not use the product.

Timing matters as much as selection. The best moments to ask are right after the customer gets value: a successful launch, a problem solved quickly, a renewal, a milestone in the product. An ask sent at a random time, or right after a support problem, reads as the company wanting something. Build triggers from events you can detect rather than sending a blanket campaign.

The ask should match the form of advocacy. Referral programs need a simple way to share and a clear incentive, and the incentive should be something the referrer is comfortable accepting. Two-sided rewards, where both the referrer and the new customer get something, reduce the awkwardness of recommending a paid product. Reviews need a direct link and a short request. References and case studies need a personal ask from someone the customer knows, and a clear statement of how much time it will take.

Measurement closes the loop. Track how many advocates you asked, how many acted, how many new customers came from referrals, and how those customers retain compared with others. The Schmitt, Skiera and Van den Bulte study is useful here as a design pattern: it compared referred customers with [similar customers acquired at the same time](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2012/04/Schmitt-Skiera-vandenBulte-2011-Referral-Programs-Customer-Value.pdf), which is the comparison that tells you whether a program adds value.

## Step-by-Step Guide

### Step 1: Confirm the earlier stages can support advocacy

Before building a program, check retention and satisfaction. If churn is high or satisfaction scores are poor, fix those first, because an advocacy program will surface complaints as fast as recommendations. Look at the share of customers who are clearly getting value, and at recent reviews. If the picture is weak, this skill can wait.

### Step 2: Decide which form of advocacy you need

Choose the advocacy that fits your growth problem. If buyers rely on peer recommendations, a referral program fits. If they read reviews before shortlisting, a review program fits. If deals stall at the reference-check stage, a reference program fits. Starting with one form keeps the program small enough to run well.

### Step 3: Identify likely advocates

Combine a stated satisfaction signal, such as a Net Promoter response, with a behavioral signal, such as active use or a recent renewal. Exclude customers with open support issues or recent complaints. Start with a short list you can contact personally. Record why each customer is on the list so you can check later which signals predicted action.

### Step 4: Build triggers around moments of value

List the events that show a customer just got value: a completed onboarding, a resolved ticket with a positive rating, a renewal, a product milestone. Tie the ask to those events so it arrives when the customer is most willing. Avoid asking immediately after a problem, even if it was resolved. Test the timing with a small group before automating it.

### Step 5: Make the action easy and the incentive appropriate

Give referrers a link or a short message they can forward, and give reviewers a direct link to the review page. For referrals, consider a two-sided reward so the new customer benefits too. For references and case studies, ask personally, explain the time involved and offer to write the first draft. Remove every step that is not essential.

### Step 6: Pilot with a small group

Launch the program to a small set of likely advocates and watch what happens. Measure how many act, where they drop off and what they say. Read the responses from people who decline, because they often reveal friction or reluctance you can fix. Adjust before expanding.

### Step 7: Measure the customers it brings in

Track referred customers separately in the CRM and compare their conversion and retention with similar customers from other channels. Track review volume and rating trends and reference-request turnaround. Report on the new customers the program brings in, not only on how many advocates signed up. If referred customers are not better than others, question the incentive design.

### Step 8: Thank and look after your advocates

Acknowledge every advocate personally, report back when a referral turns into a customer, and avoid asking the same people too often. Advocates who feel used stop advocating. Rotate reference requests across the list and keep a record of how often each customer has been asked.

## Best Practices

- **Ask after value, not on a schedule.** Event-based asks land when the customer has a fresh reason to be positive. Calendar-based campaigns land at random moments.
- **Use both stated and behavioral signals.** A high survey score from someone who rarely uses the product is a weak signal. Active use plus a positive response is stronger.
- **Keep the incentive modest and two-sided.** A reward the new customer also receives makes recommending feel like a favor rather than a sale. Large rewards attract people who care about the reward.
- **Protect your best references.** Heavy reference users burn out. Keep a log and spread requests across the list.
- **Measure the referred customers, not the program activity.** Signups and shares are easy to count and easy to inflate. Retention and value of referred customers show whether the program works.
- **Respond to public reviews.** Answering reviews, including negative ones, shows prospects how you treat customers and turns review sites into a managed touchpoint.

## Common Mistakes

- **Launching advocacy before retention works**: A referral program on top of a leaky product amplifies complaints. Fix the retention stage first and use the advocacy stage to scale what is already good.
- **Asking everyone at once**: Blanket campaigns reach unhappy customers and feel impersonal to happy ones. Start with a targeted list and event triggers.
- **Rewarding volume over quality**: Incentives that pay for any referral attract poor-fit customers who churn. Reward referrals that convert and stay, or keep incentives small.
- **Buying or gating reviews**: Paying for positive reviews or asking only happy customers to post can breach review platform rules and damages trust when discovered. Ask broadly and respond openly.
- **Reporting program activity as success**: Counting invitations sent tells you nothing about value. Compare referred customers with others acquired at the same time.
