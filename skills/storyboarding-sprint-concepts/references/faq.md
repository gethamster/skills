# FAQ: Sprint Storyboarding: Plan the Prototype Step by Step

## What is a design sprint storyboard?

It is a step-by-step comic strip of the customer's experience that serves as the plan for Thursday's prototype. GV describes it as the winning scenes from the sketches woven into "a step-by-step plan for your prototype" ([GV](https://www.gv.com/sprint/)). It shows each screen or scene in order, with the key words on each, from the opening scene to the end of the test.

## How many steps should a sprint storyboard have?

The checklist from Jake Knapp and John Zeratsky suggests a grid of about fifteen squares and a finished story of five to fifteen steps ([Design Sprint guide](https://www.character.vc/guide/design-sprint)). A shorter story may skip the context customers need; a longer one is hard to build in a day. If you run over, cut the steps that do not touch the sprint questions.

## Who draws the storyboard?

One person draws at the whiteboard while the group directs, because the checklist advises against writing together. The drawer can be the Facilitator or anyone with steady handwriting. The team makes the decisions, and one hand records them.

## Should the storyboard include error states and alternative paths?

Usually not. The storyboard plans one path through the target moment, which is what five interviews can test. If the team has chosen competing solutions, it runs a Rumble with separate prototypes; otherwise it commits to one path and notes other paths for later.

## How detailed should each frame be?

Detailed enough that a Maker could build it on Thursday without asking what goes on the screen. That usually means a rough layout, the headline, the main labels and any sentence the customer must read. Visual details such as colors and exact spacing can wait for the prototype.
