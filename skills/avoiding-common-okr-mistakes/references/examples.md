# Examples: OKR Mistakes to Avoid

## A draft full of tasks

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: an engineering team's draft objective is "Improve platform reliability." Its key results are to migrate the database, add monitoring to every service, and hold a reliability training session.

**Walkthrough:**

The audit flags all three key results as activities: each could be completed while reliability stays the same. The team asks what the work is meant to change and rewrites the key results around incidents that reach customers, time to recover from incidents, and the share of services meeting their reliability targets.

The migration, monitoring and training move into the team's plan as the work expected to move those numbers. At the first check-in, the team can already see whether incidents are falling, which the task version would never have shown.

## Sandbagging driven by pay

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a company links part of each manager's bonus to their team's average OKR score. Over several cycles, almost every team scores near 1.0 on every OKR.

**Walkthrough:**

The audit notes that the scores look like sandbagging and traces the cause to the bonus policy. Rewriting individual OKRs would not help while the incentive remains.

The OKR lead brings the pattern to leadership with the guidance from Google's experience and Doerr's advice. Leadership removes OKR scores from the bonus formula and keeps sales quotas separate. The next cycle, teams set some aspirational OKRs for the first time, and average scores on those fall to a range that reflects real stretch.

## Individual OKRs that became to-do lists

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a growing company asks every employee to write personal OKRs each quarter. Most individual OKRs read as lists of tasks, and managers spend days reviewing them.

**Walkthrough:**

The audit finds that individual OKRs mostly restate project work and rarely connect to team outcomes. Following the argument that OKRs work best at the team level, the company drops individual OKRs and keeps company and team OKRs.

Individuals still see how their work contributes, because each team's key results have named owners. Managers use one-on-ones for development goals instead. Planning time falls, and team OKRs get more attention in check-ins.
