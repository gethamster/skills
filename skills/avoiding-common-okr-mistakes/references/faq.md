# FAQ: OKR Mistakes to Avoid

## What is the most common OKR mistake?

Writing tasks as key results is one of the most frequent. A key result such as "launch the new page" can be completed without changing anything, so it scores well while telling you nothing. Google's re:Work guide suggests a quick test: key results with words like "consult," "help," "analyze" or "participate" describe activities, not outcomes.

## What is sandbagging in OKRs?

Sandbagging is setting targets you already know you can hit, so you can under-promise and over-deliver. Google's playbook gives a test: a team that can meet all of its OKRs without needing all of its people and budget is probably hoarding resources, not pushing, or both. Consistently perfect scores on aspirational OKRs are the usual symptom.

## Why shouldn't OKRs be tied to compensation?

Because people then set targets they are sure to reach, which removes the stretch OKRs depend on. Laszlo Bock has described how early Google tied product usage OKRs to pay and people gamed the system. John Doerr advises against tying OKRs to bonuses, with sales quotas as the exception.

## Are business-as-usual goals always a mistake?

The work is fine; the OKR is the problem. Maintenance matters, but an OKR that describes what the team would do anyway takes a slot from a goal that needs focus. Track steady-state health as KPIs, and promote one to a key result only when you want it to change significantly.

## How many OKRs is too many?

Google's re:Work guide recommends three to five objectives with about three key results each, and warns that more leads to over-extended teams. If the list covers everything the team does, it has become a task list. Cut to the few that would make the biggest difference.
