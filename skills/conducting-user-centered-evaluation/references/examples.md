# Examples: Conducting User-Centered Evaluation

## Think-aloud testing on a scheduling prototype

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: A clinic software team has a clickable prototype for booking follow-up appointments and needs to decide whether the new calendar view is ready to build.

**Walkthrough:**

The team defines the decision as keep or rework the calendar view, and writes three goal-based tasks. They recruit six front-desk staff from different clinics and run think-aloud sessions with one facilitator and one note-taker. Four of the six participants hesitate at the same date picker, and two book the wrong week because the default view starts on a different day than they expect. The team logs these as use errors, rates the date picker issue high severity, and reworks it.

A short second round with new participants shows the wrong-week error no longer occurs.

## Heuristic review before a user study

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: A small product team is about to recruit users to test a redesigned settings area but has limited budget for sessions.

**Walkthrough:**

Two designers who did not work on the redesign review it independently against a standard set of usability heuristics. They merge their lists and find a dozen issues, including inconsistent button labels and no confirmation after saving. The team fixes the obvious ones in a day. User sessions then focus on deeper questions, such as whether people understand what each setting does.

The review stretched a small session budget further by clearing noise first.

## Field evaluation of a warehouse scanning app

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: A logistics team's handheld scanning app tested well in the office, but early pilot users report frustration on the warehouse floor.

**Walkthrough:**

The team moves evaluation into the intended use environment and shadows workers during real shifts. They observe that workers wear gloves, which makes small targets hard to hit, and that poor signal in some aisles causes scans to fail silently. They log glove-related mis-taps as use errors and dropped scans as technical errors, assigning each to a different owner. Designers enlarge touch targets while engineers add offline queuing and a visible sync status.

A follow-up observation round confirms both error types drop.
