# Examples: Now Next Later vs Timeline Roadmap

## A growth-stage product team with slipping quarters

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a product team of six publishes a quarterly roadmap to the whole company. Over the last four quarters, most items in each column either slipped to the next quarter or changed scope during discovery, and the sales team has started quoting roadmap quarters to prospects.

**Walkthrough:**

The product lead audits the last four roadmaps against what shipped and finds only two dates with an external source: a data-residency requirement for one region and a partner integration tied to the partner's own launch. Everything else was dated only because it sat in a quarter column.

She recommends a hybrid. The roadmap moves to Now Next Later, with each initiative tagged to a company objective. The two real dates stay visible on their initiatives and on the release plan. She runs both views for one cycle, explains every item whose position changed, and retires the quarterly view once sales confirms the objective links answer most of their questions.

## An agency team that should keep its timeline

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a team inside a services firm builds fixed-scope software for clients under contracts with delivery milestones. The team lead has read about Now Next Later and asks whether to switch.

**Walkthrough:**

The audit shows that nearly every item on the roadmap has a contractual date, and the solution is usually specified before work starts. Scope changes happen through change requests, and discovery plays little part. The team's problem is capacity planning across contracts, which a dated plan handles well.

The recommendation is to keep the timeline for client work. The lead suggests one small change: an internal Now Next Later board for the team's own tooling improvements, where the work is exploratory and no client is waiting on a date.

## An enterprise platform team under executive pressure for dates

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a platform team serves several internal product groups. Executives want a dated roadmap so the product groups can plan around it, and the team's past dates have rarely held.

**Walkthrough:**

The platform lead does not argue against dates in general. He maps what each product group actually needs and finds that most need to know the order of upcoming capabilities and when an item is committed. Few of them need a date on every item. Two groups have real dependencies with fixed launch windows.

He proposes Now Next Later for the platform roadmap, a written rule that an item gets a date only when it reaches Now and engineering has estimated it, and a short dependency note on each initiative that another group relies on. The executives accept a two-cycle trial, with a review of how many committed dates held.
