# FAQ: Building a Realistic Sprint Prototype in One Day

## Can you really build a prototype in one day?

Yes, if it is a facade. GV's sprint page says that by focusing on the customer-facing surface of a product or service, a team can finish its prototype in one day ([GV](https://www.gv.com/sprint/)). The storyboard does the planning on Wednesday, and Thursday is spent building only what it shows.

## What tools should we use for design sprint prototyping?

Use tools that are rough, fast and flexible, and that the Makers already know. The checklist from Jake Knapp and John Zeratsky warns against everyday production tools, which are optimised for quality ([Design Sprint guide](https://www.character.vc/guide/design-sprint)). Knapp's early GV sprints used Keynote slides in place of polished mockups ([Knapp, 2012](https://jakek.medium.com/the-product-design-sprint-a-five-day-recipe-for-startups-84fde3e97d79)). Slides, a design tool with links, paper, props or a scripted role-play can all work depending on what the customer will touch.

## How realistic does the prototype need to be?

Realistic enough that customers react to the idea rather than to the fakeness. The checklist calls this "Goldilocks quality." Put the most realism into the frames that carry the sprint questions, and keep the words and data believable throughout.

## What does the Stitcher do?

The Stitcher joins the scenes built by different Makers into one continuous prototype and checks it for consistency and realism. They catch mismatched names, prices, styles and broken transitions, and usually lead the afternoon trial run. In a small team the Facilitator often takes this role.

## What if the prototype is not finished by the end of Thursday?

Cut scope rather than extend the day. Simplify frames that do not carry a sprint question, turn interactive screens into static ones, and make sure the path through the target moment works end to end. A shorter prototype that works is more useful on Friday than a longer one with gaps.
