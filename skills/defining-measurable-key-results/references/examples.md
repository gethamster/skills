# Examples: How to Write Key Results

## Replacing tasks with outcomes

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a product team's objective is "Get new sign-ups to their first useful result on day one." The first draft of key results reads: redesign the onboarding flow, add three tutorial videos, and run a user study.

**Walkthrough:**

All three drafts are activities. The team asks what each one is supposed to change. The redesign should raise the share of new sign-ups who complete setup on their first day. The videos should cut the number of setup questions reaching support. The study should tell them where people get stuck, which is useful as a means to the result.

The final key results measure day-one setup completion, setup-related support tickets, and the share of new accounts that use the core feature in their first week. The redesign, videos and study stay in the plan as the work expected to move those numbers.

## Catching a set that is not sufficient

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a sales team's objective is "Win our first customers in the healthcare segment." The key results cover qualified meetings booked and proposals sent.

**Walkthrough:**

The team runs the sufficiency test: could it hit both targets and still win no customers? Clearly yes. Meetings and proposals are leading indicators, which are useful for check-ins, but the objective is about wins.

They add a key result for signed contracts in the segment and keep the meetings key result as the early signal. They drop the proposals key result, because it adds little beyond the other two. The set now proves the objective, and the team can still see early in the cycle whether the pipeline is building.

## Pairing quantity with quality

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a content team sets a key result to double the number of help articles published this cycle. The support lead worries the team will publish thin articles to hit the count.

**Walkthrough:**

The team adds a quality key result: the share of help-article visits that end without the reader opening a support ticket. It also defines what counts as a published article, so short stubs do not count.

At check-ins, the two numbers are read together. When the count rises and the quality measure falls, the team slows down and improves existing articles instead. The pair keeps the team honest in a way either number alone would not.
