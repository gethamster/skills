# Examples: Comparing Transformational Leadership to Servant and Authentic Leadership

## Engineering team that stalls without its lead

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a team of eight engineers ships well under a charismatic lead, but work stalls every time she travels.

**Walkthrough:**

The lead writes the situation down and names the needed change as the team making decisions without her. Scoring the models, transformational leadership matches her current style, and its typical failure, dependence on one person, is exactly what the team shows. She keeps a transformational default for direction-setting but borrows servant-style one-to-ones that open with what each engineer needs to own more decisions. She sets a review signal: decisions made during her absence.

After one quarter she checks whether that number has gone up and adjusts if it has not.

## New manager inheriting a low-trust team

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a manager takes over a team of six whose previous leader announced several inspiring initiatives that quietly benefited his own promotion case.

**Walkthrough:**

The manager names the risk as the team hearing any vision as spin, a pseudo-transformational pattern. Comparing models, authentic leadership's focus on consistency between values and actions addresses that risk most directly. He chooses authentic as the primary model for the first months, explaining the reasoning behind each decision openly. He borrows one transformational behavior, a single shared goal restated in each planning session, but only once the team has seen him keep commitments.

His review signal is whether people start raising concerns in meetings rather than privately.

## Operations team lacking direction

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a supportive operations lead runs a team of ten that feels cared for but keeps missing shared targets.

**Walkthrough:**

The lead realizes she has been practicing a servant style almost exclusively, and the team's needed change is a clearer collective goal. Her likeliest failure is soft direction, not dependence or self-interest. She shifts to a transformational default, articulating what the team is working toward and why it matters. She keeps her servant-style development conversations as a borrowed behavior so people still feel supported.

Her review signal is whether team members can state the shared goal unprompted, checked at the next quarterly review.
