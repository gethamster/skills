# FAQ: Identifying Underserved Outcome Opportunities

## How many desired outcome statements should I include in the opportunity survey?

Include 15 to 60 outcome statements per survey. Below 15, you probably have not mapped the job thoroughly enough and will miss important opportunities. Above 60, survey fatigue degrades data quality as respondents start satisficing (picking middle-of-road answers to finish faster). If you have more than 60 outcomes, split them across two surveys targeting different respondent groups, or use a MaxDiff or best-worst scaling approach to reduce respondent burden while preserving discriminability.

## Should I run opportunity scoring before or after customer interviews?

Run qualitative interviews first, then opportunity scoring, then follow-up interviews. The initial interviews, covered in [conducting JTBD customer interviews](https://tryhamster.com/skills/conducting-jtbd-customer-interviews), generate the desired outcome statements that become survey items. The opportunity survey quantifies which outcomes matter most. Follow-up interviews after scoring add narrative context to the top-scoring outcomes.

Skipping the initial interviews means you will write outcome statements from internal assumptions rather than customer language, which produces surveys that miss real needs.

## What if most of my outcomes score in the appropriately served range (7-10)?

This is common in mature markets with established competitors. It means the aggregate market has decent solutions, but it does not mean there are no opportunities. First, segment your respondents by role, company size, or usage pattern and re-run the scoring per segment. Segment-level analysis almost always reveals pockets of underservedness hidden by the average.

5 can be a meaningful opportunity if no competitor specifically targets it. Third, consider whether your outcome statements are too broad. Broad outcomes tend to score in the middle because they average across diverse sub-experiences.

## Can I use opportunity scoring for outcomes I've written myself without customer interviews?

You can, but the results will be unreliable. Internally written outcomes tend to describe solution features in outcome clothing ('minimize the time to configure the dashboard' rather than 'minimize the time to understand current project status'). They also miss outcomes customers care about but you have never considered. The survey will still produce numbers, and those numbers will look authoritative, but they will reflect your assumptions about the job rather than the customer's actual experience.

If time constraints prevent full interviews, at minimum conduct 5-8 short calls focused on having customers describe the job in their own words before writing outcomes.

## How often should I re-run the opportunity scoring analysis?

Re-run annually at minimum, and immediately after major market events such as a new competitor launch, a significant product update by a dominant player, or a regulatory change that alters the job. Satisfaction scores are particularly volatile because they reflect the current solution landscape, which changes as competitors improve. Importance scores are more stable because they reflect the underlying job structure, but they can shift as customer expectations evolve. If you build a feature that addresses a top-scoring outcome, re-survey 3-6 months after launch to verify that satisfaction actually improved and that the outcome's score dropped, confirming your solution worked.

## Why does my opportunity landscape keep showing the same top outcomes as last year?

Three common causes. First, you may not have shipped anything that meaningfully addresses those outcomes, so satisfaction has not changed. Check your release notes against the top outcomes and ask whether your shipped features actually target those specific needs. Second, your improvements may not be visible or discoverable to customers, so satisfaction stays low even though the capability exists.

This is a UX and communication problem, not a product gap. Third, your sample may be drawing from the same customer cohort each time. If you are surveying your longest-tenured customers repeatedly, their ratings will stabilize. Include new customers and prospects in each wave to capture shifting market perceptions.

## How does opportunity scoring relate to learning how to become a product manager?

Opportunity scoring is one of the most transferable analytical skills in a product manager's toolkit. It teaches you to replace opinion-driven roadmap debates with quantified customer evidence, which is the core discipline of modern product management. Interviewers at product companies frequently ask candidates how they would prioritize features or decide what to build next. Being able to describe a structured method, surveying customers on importance and satisfaction, calculating opportunity scores, and ranking outcomes, demonstrates both analytical rigor and customer-centricity.

Practicing this skill on a real dataset, even a small one, gives you concrete examples to reference in interviews and portfolio presentations.
