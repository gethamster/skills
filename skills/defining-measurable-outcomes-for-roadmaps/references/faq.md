# FAQ: Defining Measurable Outcomes for Product Roadmaps

## How many outcomes should one team own at a time?

As few as possible. Roman Pichler recommends one product goal at a time for a roadmap timeframe and treats several goals in one period as an exception, because multiple goals dilute focus and make progress harder to read. If the business insists on more, give each a separate owner and accept that each will move more slowly.

## What is the difference between an outcome and a KPI?

A KPI is a metric the business tracks continuously, such as weekly active users or churn. An outcome is a specific change in a metric that the team commits to pursue in a period, with a baseline, a target and an owner. A declining KPI is often a good source of outcomes for a mature product, which is one of the two methods Pichler describes.

## How do I set outcomes for a new product with no baseline?

Start with learning goals. Teresa Torres suggests that when a team is working on an unfamiliar outcome, it first sets a goal to learn what moves the metric and only later sets a numeric target. Instrument the behaviors you care about from the first release so that a baseline exists by the next planning cycle.

## Should targets be commitments or stretch goals?

Say which one each target is. A committed target is one the team expects to hit and plans around. A stretch target signals ambition and is not treated as a failure when missed. Mixing the two without labels leads to sandbagging, which Torres warns about when reviews are tied to hitting outcomes.

## Can I write outcomes as OKRs?

Yes. Torres describes OKRs as one way to express an outcome, with the objective as a qualitative statement and the key results as the measures. The work is choosing the right outcome; the OKR is only the format. Pichler suggests treating a roadmap goal as the objective and its metrics as the key results.
