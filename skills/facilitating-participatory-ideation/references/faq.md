# FAQ: Facilitating Participatory Ideation

## How is participatory ideation different from a normal brainstorm?

A normal brainstorm usually involves only the project team. Participatory ideation brings in the people who experience the problem and those who deliver the current service as co-authors of ideas. That changes the facilitation, because power differences between users and staff have to be managed through mechanics like silent generation and ordered voting. It also changes the output, which should include ideas the team would not have produced alone.

## How many people should take part?

There is no fixed rule, and the right size depends on how many perspectives you need represented. As an example, a group of six to twelve lets everyone post and discuss ideas within one session. Larger groups can split into parallel tables working on different prompts, then share clusters at the end. Whatever the size, keep users a real share of the room rather than a single representative.

## What if participants strongly disagree about which idea is best?

Treat the disagreement as information. Write both positions down, ask which user need each serves, and check whether the dispute is about values or about facts. Factual disputes can often be settled by building cheap prototypes of both options and testing them, which the [IDEO process overview](https://umbrex.com/resources/frameworks/design-thinking-frameworks/ideo-human-centered-design-process) describes as resolving disagreements through evidence rather than opinion. Value disputes need to be escalated explicitly to whoever owns the tradeoff.

## Can participatory ideation work remotely?

Yes, if you rebuild the conditions of the physical room. Give everyone edit access to the same board, test it before the session, and create private spaces for silent generation before ideas are shared. Watch for participants who struggle with the tool and pair them with a helper. Remote sessions tire people faster, so shorter blocks with breaks usually work better than one long stretch.

## How do I know the session actually involved users rather than just hosting them?

Look at the shortlist. If users can point to concepts that came from their notes, and if the shortlist differs from what the team expected going in, participation shaped the outcome. If the winning idea matches the team's pre-session favorite and user votes were ignored, involvement was superficial. Inviting the same users back to test prototypes is another sign the involvement is real.
