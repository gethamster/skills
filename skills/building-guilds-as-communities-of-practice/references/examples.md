# Examples: Building Guilds as Cross-Cutting Communities of Practice

## Launching an accessibility guild

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a designer notices that several squads in different tribes keep asking the same accessibility questions in chat, and nobody owns the answers.

**Walkthrough:**

She writes a one-paragraph charter naming the topic and the audiences: designers, frontend engineers and product managers. She recruits a frontend engineer as co-convener and announces the guild in a company-wide channel, stating that joining and leaving are open to anyone. The first backlog is seeded with a dozen real questions pulled from chat history. Sessions run monthly for 45 minutes, and each ends with one change attendees can try in their squad.

After two months, around 30 people have joined the channel and a shared checklist written by the guild is linked from several squad wikis, while the decision to make it mandatory is left to the design chapters.

## Rescuing a guild that became a lecture series

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a data guild started strong, but after six months the same two senior engineers present every session and attendance has dropped to about five people.

**Walkthrough:**

The conveners run a health review and ask members what they would miss if the guild stopped. Answers point to peer questions in the channel, not the lectures. They replace the monthly talk with a monthly clinic where members bring a problem and others help live. The backlog is reopened for voting, and each session now picks the top two items.

Convenership rotates to a newer analyst paired with one of the original seniors. Within a quarter, channel questions rise and new presenters from four different squads appear.

## Closing a guild on purpose

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a guild formed around migrating to a new build tool has seen interest fall because most squads have finished the migration.

**Walkthrough:**

At its quarterly review the conveners find the backlog nearly empty and attendance down to the conveners themselves. They post a note proposing closure and ask whether anyone wants to refocus the guild on a broader developer tooling theme. Two members volunteer, so the old guild's material is archived with topic tags and a new tooling guild opens with fresh membership. The archive remains searchable, and teams still migrating keep finding answers there.

Nobody has to keep attending a meeting that no longer earns its time.
