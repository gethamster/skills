# How to Build Spotify Model Guilds That Last

> Start and sustain voluntary, organization-wide guilds that spread knowledge across squads and tribes without adding hierarchy.

## At a Glance

| Field | Value |
|-------|-------|
| Difficulty | Beginner |
| Time to Learn | 2-4 weeks to launch, then ongoing |
| Outcome | A self-sustaining guild with open membership, a live topic backlog, a predictable rhythm and published outputs that other teams reuse. |
| Prerequisites | A working grasp of squads, tribes and chapters, At least one topic that practitioners in several teams care about, Permission for people to spend some work time on shared learning |
| Part of | [Spotify Squads](../../methods/spotify-squads/METHOD.md) |

## Overview

A guild is the loosest structure in the Spotify arrangement. The [original Scaling Agile @ Spotify paper](https://storage.ghost.io/c/73/a9/73a90ce4-1663-4169-a7cb-efdf906b6b25/content/files/2022/12/Scaling-Agile-@-Spotify-with-Tribes--Squads--Chapters---Guilds-Henrik-Kniberg---Anders-Ivarsson-Oct-2012.pdf) and later retellings such as this history of the Spotify model describe a guild as an informal, voluntary community of interest that spans the organization and exists for knowledge sharing. That short definition is all the sources give you, and it is enough to shape the work. For background on where guilds sit among squads, tribes and chapters, see the [Spotify Squads method page](https://tryhamster.com/methods/spotify-squads).

The practical difference from a chapter drives most of your design choices. A chapter, as [Talkspirit's breakdown of the units](https://talkspirit.com/en/blog/spotify-squads) explains, gathers people doing the same job within one tribe and is led by a line manager. [USU's review of the model](https://usu.com/en/blog/the-spotify-model-magic-bullet-or-overrated) frames chapter meetings as the place where specialists coordinate technologies, tools, processes and methods. A guild has neither the tribe boundary nor the management line. It carries no authority over anyone's work, so the only reason people attend is that it is worth their time.

That constraint is the whole skill. You are building something people choose, which means you compete for attention with delivery work every single week. Topics must come from real problems, sessions must leave people with something they can use, and the rhythm must be light enough that skipping a session does not mean falling behind.

This page covers the doing: deciding whether a topic deserves a guild, finding a convener, opening membership without turning it into a mandate, building a topic backlog, choosing a cadence, capturing outputs and deciding when to reshape or close the guild. Anyone can run it. Guilds are often started by a practitioner with an itch rather than by a manager, and that origin tends to make them healthier.

The output is not a meeting series. It is a community with a visible membership list, a backlog of questions, a rhythm people can predict and a trail of reusable artifacts such as write-ups, recorded demos, starter templates and shared recommendations. You can tell it is working when people outside the core group cite guild material in their own squads. You can tell it has gone wrong when attendance shrinks to the organizers, when managers start assigning people to attend, or when the guild begins issuing rules that squads feel obliged to follow.

## How It Works

A guild runs on pull rather than push. Because the sources describe it as voluntary and aimed at knowledge sharing, there is no reporting line to lean on and no delivery goal to rally around. Every design decision should increase the value a member gets per hour spent. Three levers control that value: who can join, what gets discussed and how often the group meets.

**Membership.** Open membership is the default. Anyone in any squad or tribe who cares about the topic can join, lurk, contribute or leave. This is what lets a guild cross boundaries a chapter cannot: a chapter, per [Talkspirit](https://talkspirit.com/en/blog/spotify-squads), is bounded by one tribe and one discipline, while a guild can pull a backend engineer, a designer and a product manager into the same conversation about, say, accessibility. The cost of openness is a fluid roster. Accept it. Track an interested list and an active core, and expect the core to be small.

**Topics.** A guild dies fastest when it discusses what organizers find interesting rather than what members are stuck on. Keep a public backlog of questions, demos and problems, let members add and vote, and pick each session's agenda from the top. Good topics recur across teams: a problem three squads solved three different ways is ideal material. Avoid turning topics into decisions that bind squads. [USU's comparison](https://usu.com/en/blog/the-spotify-model-magic-bullet-or-overrated) places coordination of tools and methods inside chapters, so if a guild discussion surfaces a standard worth adopting, hand it to the relevant chapters or squads to decide.

**Cadence.** The sources set no cadence, size or roles for guilds, so these are your choices. A workable pattern pairs a light synchronous rhythm, for example a monthly 45-minute session, with an always-on asynchronous channel where questions get answered between sessions. The channel carries most of the value; the session gives it a heartbeat. Occasionally a larger event, for example a quarterly half-day of demos or hacking, re-energizes membership.

**Roles.** Nothing in the dossier prescribes guild roles. A practical minimum is one or two volunteer conveners who keep the backlog groomed, book sessions, recruit speakers and make sure notes get published. Rotate the role so the guild does not depend on one person's enthusiasm.

**Health loop.** Because the arrangement is, in the paper's own framing, a snapshot of a journey in progress, treat each guild as an experiment too. Review it on a fixed interval: who came, what was reused, what is left in the backlog. A guild that no longer earns attendance should be merged, refocused or closed openly, which frees people to start the next one.

## Step-by-Step Guide

### Step 1: Test whether the topic needs a guild

Start from the interest, not the structure. Write down the topic and list the squads and tribes where people already work on it. If everyone involved sits in one tribe and does the same job, the conversation probably belongs in that tribe's chapter instead. A guild earns its place when the interest crosses tribe or discipline boundaries and nobody owns the knowledge.

The output of this step is a one-paragraph statement of the topic and who it is for.

> **Pro tip:** Search your chat tools and wikis for repeated questions on the topic. Recurring unanswered questions from different teams are the strongest signal that a guild will get used.

### Step 2: Recruit a volunteer convener

Find one or two people who care enough to do the unglamorous work. The convener grooms the backlog, schedules sessions, recruits presenters and publishes notes. Keep the role explicitly voluntary and time-boxed, for example one quarter, so it can rotate. Agree with their squad how much time the role takes so it does not quietly eat delivery capacity.

> **Pro tip:** Pair a senior practitioner with a newer one as co-conveners. The senior brings credibility and contacts, the newer person gains visibility and eventually takes over.

### Step 3: Open membership and announce it

Publish a short charter: the topic, who might find it useful, how to join, how to leave and where the async channel lives. Make it clear that membership is opt-in and that nobody is expected to attend every session. Announce it in broad channels rather than through managers, so the invitation reads as an offer and not an assignment. Create a visible member list so people can find each other outside sessions.

> **Pro tip:** Say explicitly that lurking is welcome. Many members will only read the channel, and that is still knowledge sharing.

### Step 4: Build a member-driven topic backlog

Seed a shared backlog with questions and problems gathered in the first step, then invite members to add and vote. Each item should name a concrete problem, a demo or a comparison of approaches across teams. Pick session agendas from the top of the backlog instead of from the conveners' preferences. Retire items that nobody votes for after a while so the list stays honest.

> **Pro tip:** Ask every presenter to end with one thing attendees can try in their own squad next week. It keeps sessions practical.

### Step 5: Set a light cadence with an async backbone

Choose a rhythm that people can sustain alongside delivery, for example a monthly session plus a channel that is always open. Protect the session slot so it is predictable, and cancel openly when the backlog is empty rather than filling time. Use the async channel for questions, links and quick answers between sessions. Consider an occasional larger event, for example a quarterly demo afternoon, to recruit new members.

> **Pro tip:** Record sessions and post them with a short summary. Members in other offices or time zones then participate on their own schedule.

### Step 6: Capture and publish outputs

After each session, publish notes, recordings, code snippets or templates in a findable place. Tag outputs by topic so squads can find them months later. When a discussion produces a recommendation that some teams might want to adopt as a standard, hand it to the relevant chapters or squads rather than declaring it binding. The guild's authority is the quality of its material, not a mandate.

### Step 7: Review health and reshape or close

On a fixed interval, for example every quarter, look at attendance, channel activity, backlog depth and evidence of reuse in squads. Ask members directly what they would miss if the guild stopped. If interest has moved, refocus the topic, merge with a neighbouring guild or close it with a short farewell note. Closing a guild deliberately is a healthy outcome that frees attention for newer interests.

> **Pro tip:** Keep a simple log of review results. Trends over several reviews tell you more than any single dip in attendance.

## Best Practices

- Keep membership genuinely voluntary. The sources define guilds as [informal and voluntary](https://storage.ghost.io/c/73/a9/73a90ce4-1663-4169-a7cb-efdf906b6b25/content/files/2022/12/Scaling-Agile-@-Spotify-with-Tribes--Squads--Chapters---Guilds-Henrik-Kniberg---Anders-Ivarsson-Oct-2012.pdf), and that is what makes attendance a truthful signal of value. Once managers assign people, you lose the signal and gain resentful attendees.
- Draw members from across tribes and disciplines. Cross-boundary reach is what a guild offers that a tribe-bound chapter does not, so actively invite people from teams that have not joined yet.
- Let the backlog, not the organizers, set the agenda. Topics chosen by member votes stay tied to real problems, which keeps people coming back when delivery pressure rises.
- Route standards decisions to chapters or squads. [USU's review](https://usu.com/en/blog/the-spotify-model-magic-bullet-or-overrated) places coordination of tools and methods with chapters, so a guild should inform those decisions rather than make them, protecting squad autonomy.
- Make the async channel the primary venue. Most knowledge sharing happens in quick questions and answers between sessions, and a channel includes people who can never attend live.
- Publish everything in a findable, tagged place. A guild's long-term value is the reusable material it leaves behind, and unpublished discussions help only the people who were in the room.
- Rotate conveners on a fixed term. Rotation spreads the workload, develops new leaders and prevents the guild from collapsing when one enthusiast changes jobs.

## Common Mistakes

- **Treating a guild like a chapter with a manager and mandatory attendance.** — Chapters are tribe-bound and led by a line manager, as [Talkspirit describes](https://talkspirit.com/en/blog/spotify-squads), while guilds are voluntary communities of interest. Remove any reporting line or attendance tracking and let participation reflect value.
- **Letting the guild issue rules that squads feel bound to follow.** — Keep outputs as recommendations and examples. When something should become a standard, pass it to the relevant chapter or squad to decide so ownership of delivery decisions stays where it belongs.
- **Running sessions on topics the organizers find interesting rather than what members struggle with.** — Maintain a public, voted backlog and pick agendas from it. If the backlog is empty, cancel the session instead of inventing content.
- **Launching with a heavy cadence such as weekly hour-long meetings.** — Start light, for example monthly plus an async channel, and increase only if the backlog outgrows the slot. Heavy early cadence burns out conveners and teaches members that skipping is normal.
- **Keeping a dead guild alive out of habit.** — Review health on a fixed interval and close, merge or refocus when interest fades. A clean closure is better than a calendar invite nobody accepts.
