# Building PM Career Development Plans from Competencies

> Build a PM career development plan from competency gaps on Cabage's grid, using a PM growth plan template built on focus areas, real work and reviews.

## Overview

A PM career development plan turns the results of a competency assessment into specific work that moves a product manager toward the role they want. In the [Product Team Competencies Framework](../../methods/product-team-competencies-framework/METHOD.md), the plan answers a question Neal Cabage built the chart to help with: what an individual PM should be good at "if they want to focus in one direction or another" ([Cabage, Product Team Competencies](https://nealcabage.com/framework/product-team-competencies/)). The chart supports coaching and team development, and a development plan is where that coaching becomes concrete: product manager competency development for one person, written down and reviewed.

The plan starts with direction. PM career progression on Cabage's chart runs upward, from tactical boxes at the bottom toward strategic boxes at the top, and sideways, toward users and market on the left or business and technical work on the right. A PM who wants to lead a consumer product grows toward the upper-left. A PM who wants to lead a platform grows toward the upper-right. Productfolio's [summary](https://productfolio.com/product-team-competencies/) notes that the framework helps leaders advise junior PMs on which skills to acquire based on what they hope to become.

Marty Cagan's development plan gives the mechanics. In his [skills assessment article](https://www.svpg.com/developing-strong-product-managers/), the manager takes the areas with the biggest gaps, identifies actions for each, tries to focus on the top 3 areas, and meets with the PM no less than once a month to review progress. Once gaps close, he shows the PM how the importance ratings change for the next level so they can prepare for promotion.

Most of the growth in a good plan comes from doing the work. The Center for Creative Leadership's [70-20-10 rule](https://www.ccl.org/articles/leading-effectively-articles/70-20-10-rule/) describes leadership learning as 70% challenging experiences and assignments, 20% developmental relationships and 10% coursework and training. For a PM, that means each focus box needs an assignment in that box, a person to learn from, and only then reading or courses.

The output is short. One page lists the focus boxes, why each matters for the PM's direction and the team's gaps, the assignment, the mentor, any learning input, and review dates. A plan that fits on one page gets reviewed. A long plan gets filed.

## How It Works

The plan combines three inputs. The first is the PM's current profile from the team assessment, with agreed ratings for each important box. The second is the target: the next level's expectations in the leveling matrix, or a target profile for the role the PM wants. The third is the team's gaps, since a focus box that also fills a team gap gets the PM real assignments quickly.

Choosing focus boxes is the key decision. List the boxes where the target expects more than the PM does today and rank them by importance for the target role. Then pick a small number, following Cagan's suggestion to focus on the top 3 areas before moving to the next ones ([SVPG](https://www.svpg.com/developing-strong-product-managers/)). Prefer boxes that sit in the direction the PM wants to grow, and prefer boxes that the team needs.

Each focus box gets an assignment first. The assignment is real work in that box with a visible output: running the next competitive gap analysis, writing the business case for an upcoming investment, or owning a build-versus-buy decision. Assignments work because they produce evidence as well as learning. At the next assessment or promotion discussion, the output is the proof.

Relationships come next. Pair the PM with someone strong in the focus box, often a PM whose home quadrant is where the PM wants to go, or a partner in another function such as finance for Business Case work. The mentor reviews the assignment's output and gives feedback before it ships. Coursework and reading support the assignment. CCL describes that smaller share of formal learning as having an amplifier effect on the rest ([CCL](https://www.ccl.org/articles/leading-effectively-articles/70-20-10-rule/)).

Reviews keep the plan alive. A short monthly conversation checks whether the assignment is happening, what the PM learned, and whether the rating in the box has moved. Cagan notes that especially strong people managers discuss development weekly. When a focus box reaches the target, it comes off the plan and the next box on the list goes on.

## Step-by-Step Guide

### Step 1: Agree on direction

Ask the PM where they want to go: which level, and which side of the chart. Show them the chart with their current profile and the target region. If their goal and the team's needs point in different directions, say so openly and look for boxes that serve both. Write the direction in one sentence at the top of the plan.

### Step 2: List the gaps against the target

Compare the PM's agreed ratings with the target level or role expectations, box by box. List every box where the target expects more. Rank the list by how important each box is for the target role. Note which of these boxes are also team gaps.

### Step 3: Pick a few focus boxes

Choose a small number of boxes from the top of the list, in line with Cagan's advice to focus on the top 3 areas. Favor boxes that match the PM's direction and fill a team gap. Leave the rest of the list visible as the next set. More focus boxes than the PM can work on at once usually means none of them move.

### Step 4: Attach an assignment to each box

For each focus box, find real work in the next few months that exercises it and produces a visible output. Confirm the work exists and that the PM has room for it, reallocating other work if needed. Describe the expected output so progress can be checked. If no assignment exists, choose a different focus box.

### Step 5: Add a mentor and learning input

Name one person to review the PM's output in each box and meet with them during the assignment. Add reading or a course only where it directly supports the assignment. Keep the learning list short.

### Step 6: Write the one-page plan

Put the direction, focus boxes, reasons, assignments, mentors, learning inputs and review dates on one page. Use the same layout for every PM, so the page doubles as your PM growth plan template. Share it with the PM for edits so they own it. Store it where both of you can find it before each review.

### Step 7: Review monthly and rotate focus boxes

Meet at least monthly to discuss progress on each assignment, what feedback the mentor gave and whether the box's rating has moved. When a box reaches the target, replace it with the next one on the list. When all target boxes are covered, show the PM the next level's expectations, as Cagan recommends.

## Best Practices

- Start from the PM's direction. Cabage built the chart to show what a PM should be good at if they want to [focus in one direction or another](https://nealcabage.com/framework/product-team-competencies/), and a plan without direction becomes a list of weaknesses.
- Keep focus to a few boxes at a time. [Cagan's top 3 rule](https://www.svpg.com/developing-strong-product-managers/) is a good ceiling.
- Lead with assignments. Real work in the box builds skill and produces evidence for the next assessment.
- Match mentors to the target quadrant. Someone who already works in the upper-left is the best guide for a PM heading there.
- Use focus boxes that also fill team gaps. The PM gets real work sooner and the team benefits.
- Hold the monthly review even when nothing changed. A missed month usually means the assignment stalled, and the review is where you find out.

## Common Mistakes

- **Making the plan a reading list**: Courses without assignments rarely change how someone works. Put an assignment in every focus box.
- **Too many focus boxes**: Spreading effort across many boxes means none of them improves. Keep a few and rotate.
- **Ignoring the PM's direction**: Filling the team's gaps with a PM who wants to go elsewhere produces a plan they will not own. Look for overlap or be honest about the trade-off.
- **Assignments without room**: Adding a stretch assignment on top of a full workload sets the PM up to fail. Free up time or pick another box.
- **Reviewing only at annual review time**: Cagan argues that annual reviews are no substitute for ongoing development. Review the plan monthly.
