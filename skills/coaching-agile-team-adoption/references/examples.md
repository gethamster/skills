# Examples: Coaching Teams Through Agile Adoption and Transformation

## Example: 6-person startup engineering team with no prior agile experience

**Scenario:**

A seed-stage startup has 6 engineers who have been working in a 'just ship it' mode with tasks tracked in a shared Google Doc. The CTO wants to introduce some structure as the team grows. Nobody on the team has used Scrum or Kanban formally. The biggest complaint from the team is that priorities shift daily and nobody knows what to work on next. The coach has 8 weeks.

**Walkthrough:**

The agile coach spends 3 days doing one-on-ones with all 6 engineers and the CTO. The current-state map reveals that priority confusion is the overwhelming number-one pain point, mentioned by 5 of 6 engineers. Fear of agile is low because the team has no prior negative experience. The coach proposes a prioritized backlog in a simple tool (Trello board, Linear project) with a weekly 30-minute review where the CTO and team agree on the next week's priorities.

After 2 weeks, the team reports that priority thrash dropped significantly. They still occasionally get pulled into unplanned work, but the backlog gives them a reference point to push back. The coach then layers daily 10-minute stand-ups to surface blockers earlier, since the second pain point was engineers getting stuck waiting for each other. After another 2 weeks, the coach introduces 2-week sprints with lightweight planning.

By week 7, the team is running weekly backlog review, daily stand-ups, and bi-weekly sprints independently. The coach introduces a simple retrospective format and hands facilitation to a senior engineer. 2/5, and the team's first self-generated retrospective action item was to add a definition of done for pull requests.

## Example: 12-person enterprise team transitioning from waterfall

**Scenario:**

A financial services company has a 12-person team (8 developers, 2 QA, 1 BA, 1 PM) that has used waterfall with 6-month release cycles. Leadership mandated agile after a competitor shipped features faster. The team is skeptical because a previous 'agile transformation' 2 years ago resulted in the same waterfall process with stand-up meetings bolted on. The PM is worried about losing their role.

The BA is concerned estimation will become less rigorous.

**Walkthrough:**

The agile coach spends a full week on diagnosis, including individual conversations with all 12 team members plus the director who mandated the change. The current-state map reveals three key findings: the team's number-one pain is 6-month feedback loops that mean they build features customers no longer want, the PM fears role elimination, and the failed prior transformation left deep distrust. The coach starts by addressing the trust deficit head-on in a team meeting, naming the prior failure explicitly and asking the team to describe what went wrong. This surfaces that the prior effort prescribed Scrum roles and ceremonies without addressing the 6-month release cycle or getting leadership buy-in to change it.

The coach's first practice is a 2-week demo cycle: the team continues their current workflow but adds a stakeholder demo every 2 weeks showing whatever is in progress. This requires no process change, only visibility. After 4 weeks and 2 demos, stakeholders give feedback that redirects a feature, saving an estimated 3 weeks of wasted work. The team experiences the value of shorter feedback loops without any threatening role changes.

The coach then works with the PM to redefine their role as product owner, showing how their planning skills translate into backlog prioritization. Sprint planning is introduced in week 6, retrospectives in week 8. By week 12, the team is running 2-week sprints with planning, daily stand-ups, demos, and retros. 8/5.

## Example: Distributed B2C product team across 3 time zones

**Scenario:**

A consumer app company has a product team of 9 people spread across Portland, London, and Bangalore. They nominally do Scrum but the stand-ups happen at 6 AM Portland time with low attendance, sprint planning is dominated by the Portland contingent, and retrospectives have been dropped entirely because 'there is no good time.' The team ships regularly but collaboration across time zones is poor, leading to duplicate work and integration surprises.

**Walkthrough:**

The agile coach conducts asynchronous one-on-ones via video calls scheduled in each person's local business hours. The diagnostic reveals the core pain is not agile adoption, since the team already uses sprints and a backlog, but rather that the ceremonies are designed for a co-located team and exclude the distributed members. The coach's first intervention is restructuring the stand-up: replacing the synchronous meeting with an async written stand-up posted by each person at their local morning, plus a 15-minute synchronous overlap window three times a week where anyone can raise discussion items. After 2 weeks, the Bangalore and London team members report feeling significantly more included and blocker visibility improves.

The coach then restructures sprint planning into a two-phase process: async pre-planning where everyone reviews and comments on candidate stories, followed by a 60-minute synchronous session at the one overlapping hour across all three time zones. The coach reintroduces retrospectives in a similar async-plus-sync hybrid format. By week 6, the team has reduced duplicate work incidents from roughly 2 per sprint to zero for 3 consecutive sprints. The coach transfers facilitation of the async processes to a rotating 'sprint coordinator' role and exits after week 8 with a check-in scheduled at week 12.

## Example: Non-technical marketing team adopting agile for campaign management

**Scenario:**

A B2B SaaS company's 7-person marketing team wants to adopt agile to manage campaigns more predictably. They currently use a shared spreadsheet for tracking, have weekly status meetings that run 90 minutes, and frequently miss campaign launch dates because dependencies between content, design, and demand gen are invisible until the last minute. Nobody on the team has software development experience, and they are wary of developer-centric jargon.

**Walkthrough:**

' The one-on-ones reveal that invisible dependencies are the top pain point, followed by the 90-minute status meetings that everyone dreads. The coach introduces a Kanban-style board (not called Kanban, just 'our campaign board') with columns for Backlog, In Progress, In Review, and Done. Each campaign task is a card with the owner, due date, and dependencies marked explicitly. This replaces the spreadsheet.

After one week, the team can see where handoff bottlenecks cluster, predominantly between content and design. The coach then restructures the 90-minute status meeting into a 15-minute daily check-in focused only on blockers and handoffs, plus a 30-minute weekly planning session where the team reviews the board and adjusts priorities. The time saved is immediately appreciated. ' The team's first retro action item is to create a campaign launch checklist that formalizes the handoff sequence between content, design, and demand gen.

By week 6, the team has hit 3 consecutive campaign launch dates, reduced their total meeting time from 90 minutes per week to 45 minutes, and is running their own board reviews and retros without the coach.
