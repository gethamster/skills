# Examples: Setting Metrics for GO Roadmap Goals

## Making an activation goal precise

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a team building a project-planning tool has the goal "help new teams get their first project running and grow active teams". The first draft of the metrics row says "activation up".

**Walkthrough:**

The product manager asks what would be observably different if the goal were met. The answer is that more new teams would create a project, invite at least one colleague, and complete a task within their first week. The team defines activation as exactly that sequence, counted per team rather than per user, for teams created during the goal's timeframe.

The baseline from the last quarter is about one team in four. The target, agreed with the engineers, is one in three. Because teams need a week to count as activated, the result for teams created in the last week of the quarter can only be read a week after it ends, so the review is scheduled accordingly. The metrics cell reads: activated teams, one in four to one in three, read one week after quarter end, with a link to the full definition.

## A goal with no baseline

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a team maintaining an internal procurement app sets the goal "help buyers complete purchase requests without calling support". Nobody has ever measured how many requests lead to a support call.

**Walkthrough:**

Instead of inventing a target, the team makes measurement part of the goal. The first feature on the list is tagging support tickets by the request they relate to. The metrics cell states the measure, the share of purchase requests that generate a support ticket, and notes that the first month of the timeframe will establish the baseline.

At the midpoint review the baseline is known, and the team adds a target for the rest of the timeframe. At the end-of-quarter review they can say clearly whether the goal was met, which would have been impossible with the original wording.

## Measuring both halves of a compound goal

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a meal-kit subscription app has the compound goal "help subscribers cook more of the meals they order and reduce cancellations".

**Walkthrough:**

The team first proposes a single metric, cancellation rate. The product manager points out that this measures only the business half. They add a user measure: the share of delivered meals marked as cooked in the app, with a note that this relies on self-reporting.

Both measures get definitions and a reading date: cooked meals weekly, cancellations after one full billing cycle. At the review, cooking rates have risen but cancellations have not moved yet. Because both halves were measured, the team can see the user benefit arriving first and decide to keep the goal for one more timeframe instead of declaring it failed.
