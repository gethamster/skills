# Defining Target Markets for Expansion Strategies

> Define the target market for an expansion strategy: turn a candidate new market into a precise, sized definition backed by evidence of demand.

## Overview

Defining target markets is the step that turns an expansion idea into something a team can sell to. Both new-market quadrants of the [Ansoff Matrix](../../methods/ansoff-matrix/METHOD.md), market development and diversification, depend on it. "Expand into healthcare" or "go after mid-sized manufacturers" is a direction. A target market definition says exactly which buyers, with which need, in which circumstances, reachable through which channels, and how many of them exist.

A target market is commonly defined as a group of customers within the serviceable available market at which a business aims its product features, marketing and pricing, and choosing it is one of the final steps of the segmentation process ([Wikipedia: Target market](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Target_market)). In an expansion strategy, that choice is harder than in a home market because the team has little first-hand knowledge of the new buyers. Assumptions that are safe at home, about who decides, what they compare you with and how they buy, need to be checked.

Ansoff's original article offers a useful lens. He argued that describing a market by its missions, the jobs a product is meant to perform, is more useful than describing it by customer, "since a customer usually has many different missions, each requiring a different product" ([Ansoff, 1957](https://archive.org/details/strategiesfordiversificationansoff1957hbr)). Clayton Christensen and his coauthors make a similar point for innovation: the circumstances in which customers try to get a job done are more critical than buyer characteristics ([HBR](https://hbr.org/2016/09/know-your-customers-jobs-to-be-done)). A target market definition built around the job and the circumstance is easier to validate and easier to market to than one built on demographics alone.

The output of this skill is a one-page definition: the buyer and the user, the job and circumstance, the firmographic or demographic boundaries, the reachable size, the channels, the competing alternatives and the evidence of demand. It gives the market expansion strategy a shared, testable description of who it serves.

## How It Works

The definition has four layers, each narrowing the one before. The first is the job: the problem the buyer needs solved and the situation in which it becomes pressing. The second is the boundary: the characteristics that describe who has that job, such as industry, company size, region, role or life stage. The third is reach: how many of those buyers you can realistically contact through channels you can afford. The fourth is evidence: what you have observed that shows these buyers want your offer.

Demographic and geographic boundaries are necessary for targeting media and sales effort, but on their own they explain little. Critics of segmentation note that geographic and demographic segmentation can be overly descriptive and lack insight into the motivations that drive communications ([Wikipedia: Market segmentation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Market_segmentation)). Pairing the boundary with the job keeps the definition useful for both targeting and messaging.

Sizing works from the bottom up where possible. Count the organizations or people who match the boundary, then estimate how many have the job in a pressing form and how many you can reach. Top-down figures from industry reports help as a cross-check, but they usually describe a whole category, much of which will never buy from you. One common definition places the target market inside the serviceable available market, with the serviceable obtainable market as a subset of the target market ([Wikipedia: Target market](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Target_market)), which is the layering a bottom-up estimate follows.

Evidence comes from contact with buyers. Interviews reveal the job, the current alternatives and the buying process. Small tests, such as a landing page, a targeted ad, a pilot offer or outreach to a list, show whether buyers act. Record what would have disproved the definition as well as what supports it.

When more than one segment passes, the definition can name a primary target, where most resources go, and a secondary target served with less effort ([Wikipedia: Target market](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Target_market)). Keep the primary target narrow enough that one team can win it.

## Step-by-Step Guide

### Step 1: State the expansion hypothesis

Write one sentence naming the candidate market and why you believe it will buy, such as "operations managers at regional logistics firms need the same route planning our retail customers use." Note which Ansoff quadrant the move sits in, since diversification needs more evidence than market development. List what you already know and what you are assuming. This sentence is what the rest of the work tests.

### Step 2: Describe the job and the circumstance

Interview a handful of potential buyers and, where possible, the people who would use the product. Ask what they are trying to get done, when the problem becomes pressing, what they use today and what it costs them. Look for the circumstance that triggers a purchase. Write the job in the buyer's own words.

### Step 3: Draw the boundaries

Translate what you learned into characteristics you can target: industry, company size, region and role for business buyers, or location, life stage and behavior for consumers. Keep only the boundaries that separate buyers with the pressing job from those without it. Note the buyer, the user and anyone who can block the purchase. Avoid adding characteristics just because data exists for them.

### Step 4: Size the reachable market

Count how many organizations or people fit the boundaries, using directories, public registers, industry associations or platform audience tools. Estimate the share with the pressing job, based on interviews and tests. Then estimate how many you can reach through channels you can afford. Show the calculation and its sources so others can challenge it.

### Step 5: Map the alternatives and channels

List what buyers use today, including doing nothing, spreadsheets, in-house tools and direct competitors. Note why they might switch and what would stop them. Identify where these buyers look for solutions and whose advice they trust. This shapes both positioning and the channel plan.

### Step 6: Validate demand with small tests

Run one or two tests that ask buyers to act, such as booking a demo, joining a waitlist, paying for a pilot or responding to targeted outreach. Set the threshold for a positive result before starting. Compare results with a control where possible, such as your home market's response to similar offers. Update the definition with what you learn.

### Step 7: Write the one-page definition

Combine the job, the boundaries, the reachable size, the alternatives, the channels and the evidence into one page. Name the primary target and any secondary target. Date the document and name an owner. Share it with sales, marketing and product so everyone targets the same buyers.

## Best Practices

- Lead with the job and the circumstance. Characteristics say who to target, and the job says why they will buy.
- Size from the bottom up. Counting reachable buyers gives a smaller, more honest figure than a category-wide market estimate.
- Include the people who can block a purchase. In business markets, procurement, IT or compliance can stop a deal the user wants.
- Record disconfirming evidence. A definition that has survived attempts to disprove it is more reliable than one supported only by favorable anecdotes.
- Keep the primary target narrow. One clearly defined group that a small team can win is a better starting point than a broad market it cannot cover.
- Revisit the definition after the first customers. Early buyers often differ from the planned profile in useful ways.

## Common Mistakes

- **Defining the market by demographics only**: Buyers who share age, size or industry may have very different needs. Add the job and the circumstance to the definition.
- **Using a top-down market size as the target**: Category-wide figures overstate what you can win. Build a bottom-up estimate of reachable buyers.
- **Validating with friendly contacts**: Existing partners and friends tend to be encouraging. Include buyers with no relationship to you.
- **Treating interest as demand**: Positive interviews are a start. Look for buyers taking an action, such as booking time, joining a pilot or paying.
- **Writing the definition and never updating it**: New markets reveal themselves through early sales. Update the definition as evidence arrives.
