# FAQ: OKR Alignment Across Teams

## What are cascading OKRs?

Cascading is the top-down form of alignment: a company key result becomes an objective for a department or team, which then writes its own key results beneath it. What Matters describes it as useful when an organization is in a crisis or pursuing very specific objectives. Even when cascading, Doerr recommends letting employees write their own key results, so teams own the approach.

## Does every team OKR have to support a company OKR?

Google's re:Work guide asks for some connection between each team's OKRs and at least one organizational OKR, not a link for every objective. A team can also hold an objective that improves its own work. The link should be real; a stretched connection written to satisfy a template adds nothing.

## How should we handle work that needs several teams?

Give it a matching OKR or key result in every contributing team. Google's playbook says cross-team OKRs should include all the groups that must materially participate, with each group's commitment appearing in its own OKRs. That way a dependency cannot be forgotten by the team that has to deliver it.

## What if two teams' OKRs conflict?

Put the conflict on the alignment map and resolve it in the review. Often the teams can agree on a shared measure or a sequence. When it needs a priority decision neither team owns, escalate it to the leader who does, and record the decision and its date so the teams can plan around it.

## Should individuals have aligned OKRs too?

It is debated. Google's re:Work guide describes individual OKRs, while Marty Cagan recommends focusing on team objectives and Jeff Gothelf argues in Harvard Business Review that OKRs fall short when applied to individual contributors. Most organizations find alignment easier to manage at the company and team levels.
