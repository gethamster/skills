# Examples: OKR Alignment Across Teams

## A dependency with no owner on the other side

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a mid-sized software company's objective is to win larger customers. The sales team's draft includes a key result for signed deals that require single sign-on. The engineering team's draft makes no mention of single sign-on.

**Walkthrough:**

The alignment map shows the gap at once: the sales key result depends on work nobody has planned. In the alignment review, the engineering lead explains the team is already committed elsewhere. The two leads cannot resolve the priority between them, so they escalate to the product leader, who decides that single sign-on outranks one of engineering's aspirational OKRs.

Engineering adds a committed key result for shipping single sign-on by mid-cycle and drops the lower-priority aspirational OKR. Sales keeps its key result, now with a named engineering counterpart. Both teams' OKRs now show the shared work.

## Letting teams propose the approach

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a company objective is to improve retention in the first ninety days. Leadership is unsure what drives early churn and does not want to prescribe solutions.

**Walkthrough:**

Instead of cascading specific key results, leadership states the problem and asks three teams, onboarding, support and billing, to propose OKRs that would help. Each team drafts from what it sees in its own work. Onboarding targets setup completion, support targets repeat tickets from new customers, and billing targets failed first payments.

In the review, leaders check that the three together plausibly cover the main causes and that no two conflict. They accept all three, and agree to compare results at mid-cycle to see which lever moves early retention most.

## Too many layers

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a large organization cascades OKRs through company, division, department and team levels. Teams at the bottom receive their parent OKRs several weeks into the quarter.

**Walkthrough:**

The OKR lead maps how long each layer takes and finds that most of the delay comes from sequential approval. For the next cycle, the company publishes its OKRs and lets departments and teams draft at the same time, linking directly to company OKRs where the division layer adds nothing.

A single alignment review with division and department leads replaces the approval chain. Teams now finalize their OKRs within the first week of the quarter, and the division layer keeps OKRs only for the few goals that genuinely span its departments.
