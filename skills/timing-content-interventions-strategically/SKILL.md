## Overview

Timing is the third of the six pillars. In Inayatullah's framework it is the search for patterns in change: whether a system moves in a straight line, in cycles, like a pendulum between two poles, or in a spiral that combines progress with parts of the past. In a [Journal of Futures Studies interview](https://jfsdigital.org/2020/03/07/an-interview-with-the-unesco-chair-in-futures-studies-sohail-inayatullah/) he describes using macrohistory "as a diagnostic", asking which parts of an organization are exhibiting linear, cyclical or pendulum patterns. This skill applies that diagnostic to content: it reads the pattern of demand for each pillar topic and uses it to decide when to intervene.

An intervention is any deliberate change to a page or topic: publishing a new page, refreshing an existing one, consolidating pages, or retiring a page whose topic has gone. Many content calendars schedule interventions by capacity, such as one slot per week. Timing content interventions strategically means scheduling them by the topic's pattern. A seasonal topic needs its page ready before the season. A rising topic rewards early coverage. A declining topic may need consolidation instead of new pages. A topic that swings between two framings needs a page that can follow the swing.

Timing also protects against two habits Google warns about in its guidance on [people-first content](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content). One is writing about things "simply because they seem trending" and not because you would write about them otherwise for your existing audience. The other is "changing the date of pages to make them seem fresh when the content has not substantially changed". A good timing plan refreshes pages when there is something new to say, and publishes on trends only when they fall inside the topics the site has chosen to own.

The [Six Pillars Framework](../../methods/six-pillars-framework/METHOD.md) runs timing after mapping and anticipation. The map shows the current state of each topic, and the emerging issues list shows what might be starting. Timing turns both into a calendar.

## How It Works

The skill combines three signals for each pillar topic: the demand pattern, your own page's trajectory, and the competitive state of the results.

The demand pattern comes from Google Trends and Search Console. Google Trends divides search interest by total searches for the place and time and scales the result "on a range of 0 to 100", which makes it good for comparing shape over time and poor for estimating volume ([Google Trends FAQ](https://support.google.com/trends/answer/4365533?hl=en)). Search Console gives your own queries with real clicks and impressions, and its performance report holds [16 months of data](https://developers.google.com/search/blog/2018/01/introducing-new-search-console), enough for a year-over-year comparison of the same season. Together they show whether a topic is seasonal, rising, declining, or swinging.

Your page's trajectory shows whether your coverage is keeping up. A page whose impressions hold while its clicks fall may be losing ground in how results are presented. A page whose impressions fall while demand holds is probably being outranked. A page whose impressions and demand fall together is following its topic down.

The competitive state is read from the results themselves. Check which pages rank for the core queries, how recently they were substantially updated, and whether a competitor has recently published a much stronger page. A fresh, strong competitor page may call for a substantive refresh of yours. A results page full of outdated content may be an opening for a new page.

The three signals combine into a decision per topic. Seasonal topics get their page published or refreshed before demand rises, which means working backward from the season by the time it takes to write, review and get the page indexed. Rising topics inside your chosen territory get early, thorough coverage. Declining topics get consolidation into a broader page or a retirement with a redirect. Swinging topics get a page structured to cover both framings, updated when the swing turns.

The calendar that results is time-phased: each intervention has a date, a reason from the signals, and a check afterward. Inayatullah's timing pillar also pays attention to hinge periods, when the action of a few can make a large difference. For a content team, the equivalent is the window when a topic is forming and a strong page can shape how it is covered, and a calendar built this way puts limited writing time into those windows.

## Step-by-Step Guide

### Step 1: Build the pillar inventory with performance data

List each pillar topic with its pillar page and main cluster pages. For each page, pull clicks, impressions and position from Search Console over the full available history. Note when each page was last substantially updated. Keep the inventory in one sheet that the rest of the steps add to.

### Step 2: Classify each topic's demand pattern

Look at Google Trends for the topic's main queries over several years, and at your own Search Console data for the same period. Classify the topic as seasonal, rising, declining, swinging between framings, or stable. For seasonal topics, note when demand starts to climb and when it peaks. Record the classification and the evidence in the timing note.

### Step 3: Read each page's trajectory

Compare each page's impressions and clicks with the topic's demand. Mark pages that are losing ground while demand holds, pages following their topic down, and pages growing faster than their topic. Check for pages that compete with each other for the same queries. Each pattern suggests a different intervention.

### Step 4: Check the competitive state of the results

Search the core queries and look at the ranking pages. Note which were recently updated with real changes, which are outdated, and whether a strong new competitor page has appeared. Record anything that suggests an opening or a threat. Keep this check brief and repeat it before each planned intervention.

### Step 5: Decide the intervention for each topic

Combine the three signals into one decision per page: publish new, refresh substantially, consolidate, retire, or leave alone. Refresh only when there is new information, a better answer or a changed search intent to address. Retire pages whose topics have faded, redirecting them to the closest useful page. Leave stable, well-performing pages alone.

### Step 6: Build the time-phased calendar

Place each intervention on a calendar. For seasonal topics, work backward from the start of the demand rise by the time it takes to write, review and get the page indexed. Put rising topics early in the calendar and consolidation work in quieter periods. Record the reason for each date so the calendar can be defended and adjusted.

### Step 7: Set triggers and review each quarter

Define triggers that would change the calendar mid-cycle, such as a sudden change in demand, a strong competitor page, or a scenario indicator from the scenario planning skill. Each quarter, compare what happened with what the timing notes expected, update the notes, and adjust the calendar. Check that refreshed pages actually changed in substance.

## Best Practices

- **Compare shapes with Trends and volumes with Search Console.** Trends is scaled and relative. Search Console holds your real clicks and impressions. Use each for what it does well.
- **Work backward from the season.** A seasonal page published at the peak arrives late. Count back from the start of the rise by the time your process takes.
- **Refresh with substance.** Update a page when there is something new to say. Changing only the date does not help readers and is a practice Google warns against.
- **Stay inside your territory.** Rising topics outside the pillars you chose are distractions, however popular. Add them to the emerging issues list if they matter.
- **Retire pages deliberately.** Consolidating or redirecting a page on a faded topic keeps the cluster clean. Leaving it to decay leaves weak pages in the cluster.
- **Write down the reason for each date.** A calendar entry with its reason can be reviewed and changed. One without a reason tends to become a fixed habit.

## Common Mistakes

- **Scheduling by capacity alone**: A one-post-per-week rhythm ignores when topics are in demand. Schedule by the topic's pattern and fit capacity around it.
- **Reading Trends as volume**: Google Trends shows relative interest on a scaled index. Use Search Console or a keyword tool when you need volumes.
- **Chasing every trend**: Publishing on anything that spikes spreads the site across topics it cannot own. Publish on trends inside your chosen pillars and list the rest as emerging issues.
- **Cosmetic refreshes**: Updating dates or swapping a few sentences does not make a page current. Refresh when content changes in substance.
- **Never retiring anything**: Pages on dead topics dilute a cluster and compete with better pages. Consolidate or redirect them as part of the calendar.
