# Designing Competency-Based PM Interview Rubrics

> Design a competency-based PM interview with structured PM interview questions, a PM interview rubric and a hiring scorecard tied to Cabage's grid.

## Overview

A competency-based PM interview tests candidates on the specific boxes the role needs, using the same questions and the same rubric for every candidate. The [Product Team Competencies Framework](../../methods/product-team-competencies-framework/METHOD.md) provides the boxes. Neal Cabage names hiring as one of the chart's uses, helping teams work out what type of PM a given product role needs ([Cabage, Product Team Competencies](https://nealcabage.com/framework/product-team-competencies/)). The job description turns that into a handful of core boxes. This skill turns the core boxes into a structured interview.

Structured interviewing is well established. Google's re:Work [guide to structured interviewing](https://rework.withgoogle.com/intl/en/guides/a-guide-to-structured-interviewing-for-better-hiring-practices) defines it as using uniform methods to assess candidates applying for the same job, and describes four parts: vetted, role-relevant questions; comprehensive written feedback; standardized rubrics so reviewers share an understanding of what outstanding, solid, borderline and poor responses look like; and interviewer training and calibration. The guide says structured interviews result in increased predictive validity and decreased differences between demographic groups.

The grid makes structure easier to apply to product management. Without it, a PM loop can end up with several interviewers asking the same general questions about shipping something, and the panel collects repeated signal on a few skills and none on others. With it, each core box is assigned to one interviewer, and each interviewer knows exactly what they are there to test.

The PM interview rubric is the heart of the work. For each question, write down in advance what a poor, borderline, solid and outstanding answer contains, using the level expectations for the box. The rubric lets interviewers score answers against the same standard, and it lets the debrief compare evidence instead of impressions.

The output is an interview kit: an assignment of core boxes to interviewers, questions with planned follow-ups, a rubric per question, and a product manager hiring scorecard that collects ratings and written evidence per box. The same kit is reused for every candidate for the role and improved after each hiring cycle.

## How It Works

Start with the core boxes from the job description and the expectations for the level. Each core box needs at least one interviewer. Boxes in the same region can share an interviewer: a senior consumer role's upper-left boxes, such as Market Positioning and Opportunity Discovery, fit together in one conversation, while Build vs Buy belongs with a technical interviewer.

Two question types cover most boxes. Behavioral questions ask for past work (questions that start "Tell me about a time"), which suits boxes where the candidate should have direct experience, such as Competitive Gap Analysis for a senior hire. Hypothetical questions describe a situation (questions that start "Imagine if"), which suits boxes the candidate may not have owned yet but the role needs, or reasoning under ambiguity. The re:Work guide describes both types and recommends an initial prompt plus predetermined follow-ups designed to draw out the candidate's reasoning.

Rubrics come from the leveling matrix. A solid answer for a box is the expectation at the level you are hiring. An outstanding answer shows the next level's expectation. Borderline and poor answers describe what is missing. Writing rubrics this way ties the interview to the same standard used for promotion, so a new hire's first development plan starts from where the interview left off.

The scorecard collects one rating per box, plus written evidence. Evidence means the candidate's words and actions in the interview, written down separately from the interviewer's conclusion. The re:Work guide stresses recording comprehensive feedback so evaluators can review responses, and the scorecard is where that feedback goes.

Calibration keeps interviewers consistent. Before the loop goes live, interviewers score a sample answer together and compare. Where ratings differ, they discuss what the rubric says until they agree, and the rubric is clarified where it caused the disagreement. The re:Work guide also reports that pre-made questions, guides and rubrics save on average 40 minutes per interview ([re:Work](https://rework.withgoogle.com/intl/en/guides/a-guide-to-structured-interviewing-for-better-hiring-practices)), which helps win interviewer support for the extra preparation.

## Step-by-Step Guide

### Step 1: Take the core boxes from the job description

List the core boxes and the level. Add any secondary boxes that would change a hiring decision. Confirm with the hiring manager that nothing important is missing. Every box on the list must be tested somewhere in the loop.

### Step 2: Assign boxes to interviewers

Group boxes that fit into one conversation, usually by region of the chart. Assign each group to an interviewer who knows that work, such as a senior PM for strategic Market boxes or an engineering lead for Technical boxes. Make sure no core box is left out and no box is tested by several people by accident. Write the assignment down.

### Step 3: Write questions with follow-ups

For each box, write one main question and several predetermined follow-ups. Use behavioral questions where the candidate should have done the work and hypothetical ones where they may not have. Make the scenario realistic for your products. Keep the same questions for every candidate for the role.

### Step 4: Write the rubric for each question

For each question, describe what a poor, borderline, solid and outstanding answer contains. Use the level expectations from the leveling matrix for solid and the next level for outstanding. Name specific content, such as whether the candidate compared alternatives or used data. Avoid rubric lines that describe personality.

### Step 5: Build the hiring scorecard

Create a scorecard with one row per box, a rating from the rubric, and a space for written evidence. Ask interviewers to submit it before the debrief and before seeing others' ratings. Add one overall recommendation at the bottom. Keep the scorecard the same for every candidate.

### Step 6: Train and calibrate interviewers

Walk interviewers through their boxes, questions and rubrics. Have them score one or two sample answers and compare ratings. Clarify rubric lines that caused disagreement. Repeat calibration when new interviewers join.

### Step 7: Run the debrief by box

In the debrief, go box by box and read the evidence before discussing ratings. Where interviewers disagree, return to the rubric. Decide based on the core boxes, with secondary boxes as tiebreakers. Record why the decision was made.

### Step 8: Review the kit after each hiring cycle

After a hire, note which questions produced useful evidence and which did not. Retire or rewrite weak questions and refresh questions that candidates may have shared. After the new hire has worked for a while, compare their interview ratings with their first assessment to see whether the rubric predicted well.

## Best Practices

- Give every interviewer a clear set of boxes. A loop where each person knows what they are testing produces more signal than one where everyone improvises.
- Write rubrics before interviewing anyone. The [re:Work guide](https://rework.withgoogle.com/intl/en/guides/a-guide-to-structured-interviewing-for-better-hiring-practices) describes rubrics that set out outstanding, solid, borderline and poor responses in advance.
- Tie rubrics to level expectations, so a solid answer means the candidate meets the level you are hiring for.
- Match question type to the box. Use behavioral questions for boxes the candidate should have owned and hypothetical ones for boxes the role will stretch them into.
- Collect written evidence before ratings are discussed. It keeps the debrief grounded in what the candidate said.
- Weight core boxes over the rest of the chart. Cabage's framework [does not assume every PM can do everything](https://nealcabage.com/framework/product-team-competencies/), so candidates should not be penalized for boxes the role does not need.

## Common Mistakes

- **Everyone asking the same general question**: Several interviewers asking about a shipped project gives repeated signal on a few boxes and none on others. Assign boxes.
- **Rubrics that describe personality**: Lines such as confident or strategic mindset cannot be checked. Describe the content of a good answer.
- **Changing questions between candidates**: Improvised questions make candidates impossible to compare. Keep the kit fixed for the role and improve it between cycles.
- **Debriefing on overall impressions**: Starting with a yes or no invites anchoring. Go box by box through the evidence first.
- **Testing boxes the job description does not mention**: Candidates prepare for the role described. Testing unrelated boxes rejects people for things the role does not need.
