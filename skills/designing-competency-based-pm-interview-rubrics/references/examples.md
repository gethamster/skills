# Examples: Designing Competency-Based PM Interview Rubrics

## A loop for a senior consumer PM

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a consumer app is hiring a senior PM to fill a gap in strategic Users and Market work. The job description names four core boxes: Market Positioning, Opportunity Discovery, Jobs to be Done and Product Roadmap.

**Walkthrough:**

The hiring manager assigns Market Positioning and Opportunity Discovery to a senior PM, Jobs to be Done to the research lead, and Product Roadmap to an engineering manager. Each interviewer gets one behavioral question and one hypothetical question with follow-ups.

For Opportunity Discovery, the behavioral question asks the candidate to walk through an opportunity they identified and how they assessed it. The rubric says a solid answer compares several opportunities with evidence, and an outstanding answer also explains how the choice changed the roadmap. The debrief goes box by box, and the decision rests on the four core boxes.

## Calibrating a new panel

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a company has new interviewers for a platform PM role. In a calibration session, three interviewers score the same recorded sample answer on Build vs Buy and give three different ratings.

**Walkthrough:**

They read the rubric together. The borderline and solid descriptions both mention comparing options but do not say what separates them. The group adds that a solid answer includes the long-term maintenance cost and the effect on the team's roadmap.

A second sample answer gets matching ratings from all three. The rubric change is recorded so future interviewers use the clarified version.

## Retiring a question that gives no signal

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: after a hiring cycle, the hiring manager reviews the scorecards. One question on Feature Prioritization produced nearly identical answers from every candidate.

**Walkthrough:**

The question asked how the candidate prioritizes features in general, and every candidate described a well-known scoring model. The manager rewrites it as a hypothetical based on a real trade-off from the product, with follow-ups on what data the candidate would want and whom they would involve.

In the next cycle, answers differ enough to separate candidates, and the scorecards show more written evidence for that box.
