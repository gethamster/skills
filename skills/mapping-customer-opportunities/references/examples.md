# Examples: Mapping and Prioritizing Customer Opportunities

## Example: B2B SaaS. Project Management Tool for Small Agencies

**Scenario:**

A 4-person product team at a project management SaaS has conducted 12 customer interviews over three weeks with small creative agency owners (5-20 employees). Their current product outcome is 'increase activation rate from trial to paid within 14 days.' They have 47 raw data points extracted from interview notes and no existing opportunity map.

**Walkthrough:**

The team spends 45 minutes on silent affinity mapping and groups the 47 data points into 9 clusters. After discussion, they organize these into 4 top-level themes: 'Getting the team onboarded quickly' (3 sub-opportunities), 'Understanding project health at a glance' (2 sub-opportunities), 'Managing client communication without switching tools' (2 sub-opportunities), and 'Tracking time without interrupting creative flow' (2 sub-opportunities). They define scoring anchors for frequency (1 = 1-2 customers, 2 = 3-5, 3 = 6+), intensity (1 = mild friction, 2 = significant workaround, 3 = nearly a dealbreaker), breadth (1 = specific agency type, 2 = most small agencies, 3 = all agency sizes), and alignment (1 = loosely related to activation, 2 = moderately affects activation, 3 = directly blocks trial-to-paid conversion). Independent scoring reveals strong consensus on most items but a 2-point intensity gap on 'Importing existing projects from other tools', the product designer scored it 1 (mild inconvenience) while the customer success lead scored it 3 (she had watched three trial users abandon during import).

After the CS lead shared specific customer quotes about spending two hours trying to import and giving up, the team converged on intensity 3. Final rankings put 'Understanding which features to configure for my specific agency type' at the top (total: 11/12) and 'Importing existing projects from competing tools' at second (total: 10/12). Both directly connect to the activation outcome. The team selects these two and begins ideating solutions the following week, with the map pinned as a reference artifact.

## Example: B2C. Mobile Fitness App for Beginners

**Scenario:**

A product manager and designer at a fitness startup have conducted 8 interviews with users who downloaded the app but stopped using it within two weeks. The product outcome is 'increase 30-day retention from 18% to 30%.' They are working with 31 data points and have limited time, they need to complete the mapping in under 2 hours.

**Walkthrough:**

Working as a pair, they do a quick affinity sort in FigJam, producing 7 clusters. They collapse these into 3 top-level themes: 'Knowing what workout to do today without overthinking' (3 sub-opportunities including 'understanding which difficulty level matches my actual fitness,' 'not feeling judged for choosing easy workouts,' and 'getting a plan without answering 20 setup questions'), 'Feeling like I am making progress even when results are invisible' (2 sub-opportunities), and 'Fitting exercise into unpredictable daily schedules' (2 sub-opportunities). Since they are only two people, they each score independently, discuss the three cases where they diverge by more than one point, and converge in 15 minutes. The clear winner is 'Knowing what workout to do today without overthinking' with the sub-opportunity 'Getting a plan without answering 20 setup questions' scoring highest (frequency 3, intensity 3, breadth 3, alignment 3, total 12/12).

Six of eight interviewees mentioned the onboarding quiz as the point where they felt overwhelmed and disengaged. The team commits to this opportunity and immediately connects it to their [Opportunity Solution Tree](https://tryhamster.com/skills/building-opportunity-solution-trees) as the primary branch to explore. The entire process, extraction through prioritization, took 1 hour and 50 minutes.

## Example: Enterprise Platform. HR Software for Mid-Market Companies

**Scenario:**

A cross-functional team of 6 (PM, 2 designers, engineering lead, customer success manager, sales engineer) at an HR platform has accumulated 93 data points over 6 weeks of interviews with HR directors at companies of 200-2000 employees. Their product outcome is 'reduce time-to-hire by 20% for customers using the recruiting module.' The volume of data makes a flat affinity map unwieldy.

**Walkthrough:**

The team splits the 93 data points among three pairs, with each pair doing an initial affinity sort on their subset (approximately 31 data points each, 25 minutes). Pairs then present their clusters to the full group, and the team spends 30 minutes merging overlapping clusters and resolving disagreements about grouping. They arrive at 6 top-level themes with 22 sub-opportunities total. Because 22 items across 4 scoring dimensions with 6 raters is substantial, they use a shared spreadsheet where each person fills in scores on their own tab, then a summary tab automatically highlights divergent scores (defined as a spread of 2+ points).

Only 7 of 88 dimension-scores trigger the divergence flag, and the team resolves those in 25 minutes of focused discussion. The top-ranked sub-opportunity is 'Understanding where candidates are getting stuck in the pipeline without running manual reports' (total: 14/15), supported by 11 distinct data points from 8 different customers. The second-ranked is 'Getting hiring managers to complete their interview feedback within 24 hours' (total: 13/15). The PM notes that both sub-opportunities fall under the same parent theme ('Visibility into pipeline bottlenecks'), which suggests a solution might address both simultaneously.

They select the parent theme as their focus area and will explore solutions targeting both sub-opportunities. The full session, including data extraction done incrementally over prior weeks, took about 3 hours of synchronous team time.

## Example: Early-Stage Startup. Weekly Map Update After Limited Interviews

**Scenario:**

A solo founder building a bookkeeping tool for freelancers has conducted only 4 interviews so far. She is doing customer opportunity mapping for the first time and worries she does not have enough data to create a meaningful map. Her product outcome is 'get 50 freelancers actively using the tool weekly within 3 months.'

**Walkthrough:**

From 4 interviews she extracts 19 data points. She groups them into 5 clusters and names each as an opportunity statement. She does not build a second level of hierarchy yet because the clusters are already specific enough. She scores each opportunity herself on the 4 dimensions, noting that her frequency scores are all low (maximum 2, since she has only interviewed 4 people) and her confidence in intensity scores is uneven, two opportunities are backed by vivid customer stories while three are based on brief mentions.

Rather than forcing a precise ranking, she identifies the 2 opportunities with the highest intensity and marks them as 'explore further' while marking the other 3 as 'need more data.' Her top opportunity is 'Knowing whether I have set aside enough for quarterly taxes without doing math myself', only 2 of 4 customers mentioned it, but both described spending anxious hours on it every quarter. She commits to asking about tax anxiety in her next 4 interviews to validate whether this is a pattern or a coincidence. By week three, with 8 total interviews and 38 data points, her opportunity map has stabilized enough for confident prioritization and she selects her first target opportunity with genuine conviction.
