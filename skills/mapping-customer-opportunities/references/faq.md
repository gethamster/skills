# FAQ: Mapping and Prioritizing Customer Opportunities

## How many customer interviews do I need before I can start customer opportunity mapping?

You can start with as few as 5 interviews, though your confidence in the map will be low. The key insight is that you do not need a complete map to start, you need a map that is good enough to identify your first target opportunity, which you will refine with each subsequent interview. At 5-8 interviews, you will typically see 2-3 strong opportunity themes emerge with enough supporting data points to score meaningfully. By 12-15 interviews, most teams find that new interviews are reinforcing existing opportunities rather than revealing entirely new ones.

If you are still discovering major new themes after interviews, your interview guide may be too broad, consider narrowing the scope to a specific part of the customer journey.

## Should I do customer opportunity mapping before or after building an Opportunity Solution Tree?

Map opportunities first. The Opportunity Solution Tree requires prioritized opportunities as input, they form the second level of the tree, directly beneath your product outcome. Without a scored and prioritized opportunity map, you are guessing at which opportunities to branch into. That said, the two artifacts are tightly linked.

Once you select a target opportunity from your map, it becomes a branch on your [Opportunity Solution Tree](https://tryhamster.com/skills/building-opportunity-solution-trees), and from there you ideate solutions and design assumption tests. Think of the opportunity map as the input that feeds the tree.

## How do I handle opportunities that score identically after prioritization?

Tied scores are common and not a problem, they indicate that you have two opportunities of genuinely similar importance. When this happens, use a tiebreaker: which opportunity do you have more confidence in (more supporting data points)? Which one is more directly connected to your current product outcome? Which one would teach you the most about your customer if you explored it?

If the tie persists after tiebreakers, pick either one, the cost of choosing between two equally strong opportunities is much lower than the cost of not choosing at all. You can always pursue the other one next.

## How often should I completely rebuild the opportunity map versus incrementally updating it?

Incremental weekly updates (adding new data points, rescoring changed opportunities) should be your default cadence. A full rebuild, re-reading all data points, re-clustering from scratch, re-scoring everything, is appropriate in three situations: when your product outcome changes significantly, when you shift to a new customer segment, or when the map has grown unwieldy and needs pruning. In practice, most teams do a full rebuild every 3-6 months and incremental updates weekly. If you find your weekly updates are getting stale and feel rote, that is a signal that either your interview cadence has slowed or you are asking the same questions and getting the same answers, both of which need addressing.

## Why does my opportunity prioritization keep shifting every week?

Weekly oscillation in rankings usually means one of two things. First, you may have too few data points per opportunity, so each new interview swings the scores significantly, this stabilizes as evidence accumulates. Second, your scoring anchors may be ambiguous, causing you to score the same dimension differently each week based on your mood or the most recent interview. The fix for the first case is patience and continued interviewing.

The fix for the second is to write concrete anchor definitions with examples: 'Intensity 3 means the customer described a workaround that takes a significant amount of time per week OR explicitly mentioned considering cancellation.' Anchored scoring produces much more stable rankings over time.

## Can I use customer opportunity mapping for internal stakeholder requests and not just interview data?

You can include stakeholder input as a data source, but do not treat it as equivalent to customer data. A stakeholder request ('Sales says we need a better dashboard') is a secondhand signal that may or may not map to a real customer need. The best approach is to add stakeholder requests to your interview guide as hypotheses to validate: 'Sales thinks customers need a better dashboard, let us ask the next five customers about how they monitor their account health and see if the data supports it.' If customer interviews confirm the need, the opportunity earns its place on the map with primary evidence.

If they do not, the stakeholder request is a hypothesis that was not validated, which is valuable information in itself.

## How do I present the opportunity map to stakeholders who want to see a feature roadmap?

Frame the opportunity map as the evidence layer beneath the roadmap. Show stakeholders the top-ranked opportunities with their scores and supporting customer quotes, then connect each opportunity to the solutions you are exploring or building. The narrative becomes: 'We are building Feature X not because it was on a list, but because it addresses our highest-scored customer opportunity, one that multiple interviewed customers described as a significant pain point. Here are three quotes that illustrate the problem.'

This shifts the conversation from 'why isn't my favorite feature on the roadmap?' to 'what does the customer data tell us?' Over time, stakeholders learn to engage with the opportunity level rather than the feature level, which produces far more productive prioritization discussions.
