# Collecting Six Forces Model Data: A Research Process

> Collect six forces model data with a market research process that sources, checks and files evidence for every force before anyone rates it.

## Overview

Collecting six forces model data is the research phase of the [Six Forces Model](../../methods/six-forces-model/METHOD.md): gathering the evidence behind each force before the team rates it. Rivalry, new entrants, substitutes, buyer power, supplier power and complementary products each need their own facts, and most weak analyses fail here. The ratings look confident, but when someone asks why supplier power is "high," there is nothing to point to.

The skill is a market research process with a fixed shape. You start from the questions each force raises, check what the team already knows, fill gaps from secondary sources, and use primary research only for what public data cannot answer. The [SBA's guide to market research](https://www.sba.gov/business-guide/plan-your-business/market-research-competitive-analysis) draws the same line: existing sources save time but may not fit your audience, while asking people directly gives a more nuanced picture and is time consuming and expensive. That trade-off decides the order of work.

Each item of evidence is filed against a force, with its source, its date and a note on how much you trust it. The result is an evidence file. It is the input to rating, to synthesis and to the next update, so it needs to be readable by someone who did not do the research.

The skill covers competitive analysis data sources and industry analysis research methods at a practical level: public filings, government statistics, patent records, search trend data, customer and partner interviews, and short surveys. It does not cover the rating itself, which belongs to the force-specific skills, or the choice of tools and templates, which has its own skill.

A good evidence file has three properties. Every force has at least some evidence, even if it is thin. Every claim can be traced to a source a colleague can open. And gaps are written down, so that a rating built on weak evidence says so. When those hold, the rest of the analysis has a foundation.

## How It Works

The process runs from questions to sources. For each force, write the questions the rating depends on. For rivalry: how many competitors, how concentrated, how fast the market is growing, how differentiated the products are. For buyers and suppliers: how concentrated they are, what switching costs they face, whether they could integrate. For substitutes: what else meets the same need, at what price and performance. For complements: what customers use alongside the product, and who controls it. The factor lists in the [five forces article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porter%27s_five_forces_analysis) are a good starting checklist.

Secondary research comes first because it is cheaper and can be checked. Public companies describe their business, markets and main risks in their annual 10-K filings; the SEC's [guide to reading a 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/answers/reada10k.htm) explains that Item 1 covers products, services and markets and Item 1A covers the most significant risks. EDGAR's full text search covers [more than 20 years of filings](https://www.sec.gov/edgar/search-and-access), which makes it practical to search competitors' filings for mentions of a customer, a supplier or a new rival. The USPTO's [Patent Public Search](https://www.uspto.gov/patents/search/patent-public-search) shows who is filing in your space, a useful signal for entrants and substitutes. Government statistics such as the [Census Business Builder](https://www.census.gov/data/data-tools/cbb.html) give market size and business counts by location.

Primary research fills the gaps. Interviews with customers, former customers, partners, suppliers and salespeople answer questions no filing covers: why buyers switch, which complements they depend on, what a supplier could charge. [Nielsen Norman Group's interview guidance](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/user-interviews/) applies directly: ask open-ended questions, ask about specific past events instead of general habits, and avoid leading questions, because interviews collect self-reported data that is prone to poor recall and social-desirability bias.

Every item is checked before it is filed. Where possible, confirm a claim from a second, independent source. Record the date, because a market share from several years ago may describe a different industry. Label each item as fact, estimate or opinion. Search interest data is a good case for care: [Google Trends](https://support.google.com/trends/answer/4365533) scales results from 0 to 100 relative to all searches in that place and time, so it shows relative interest and should never be reported as search volume.

## Step-by-Step Guide

### Step 1: Write the research questions per force

Start from the scope of the analysis and list, for each of the six forces, the questions that the rating will depend on. Keep them concrete, such as "What share of revenue do the largest customers represent?" instead of "How powerful are buyers?" Use Porter's factor lists as a checklist so no major driver is skipped. Add questions about direction as well as level, since the analysis records where each force is heading. This list becomes the table of contents of the evidence file.

### Step 2: Inventory what the team already knows

Collect existing material before starting new research: past strategy decks, win-loss notes, CRM fields, customer interviews, partner agreements and analyst reports. Map each item to the questions from step 1 and note its date and origin. Mark the questions that are already answered well, answered weakly or not answered at all. This usually shows that some forces are well covered and others, often complements and substitutes, have almost nothing.

### Step 3: Source competitive analysis data from public records

Work through secondary sources force by force. Use 10-K filings and investor materials for competitors' markets, risks and customer dependence, EDGAR full-text search for mentions of specific companies, patent searches for signs of entry, and government statistics for market size and business counts. Trade associations, regulators and industry publications fill in the rest. Save the exact source, page and date for every item so it can be checked later.

### Step 4: Run targeted primary research

Plan primary research only for questions that remain open. Choose the people who can answer them: customers and lost customers for buyer power and substitutes, partners for complements, procurement staff or suppliers for supplier power, and your own sales team for rivalry. Write open questions about specific recent events, such as the last time the customer evaluated an alternative. Keep interview notes in a consistent format so answers can be compared across interviews.

### Step 5: Check and grade every item

Before an item goes into the file, check its source, its date and whether it agrees with other evidence. Where two sources disagree, record both and the likely reason. Grade each item on a simple confidence scale and label it as fact, estimate or opinion. Treat numbers from vendor marketing or unsourced blog posts as low confidence until confirmed elsewhere.

### Step 6: File the evidence by force

Put everything into one evidence file organized by force and by question. Each entry has the claim, the source, the date, the confidence grade and the name of the person who added it. Keep direct quotes from interviews separate from your interpretation of them. The rating skills will read this file, so write entries that make sense to someone who was not in the room.

### Step 7: Record gaps and hand off

End with a short gap list: questions that remain unanswered or rest on weak evidence, and what it would take to close each one. Note which forces are affected, so their ratings can carry a lower confidence. Hand the file to whoever runs the rating and synthesis, and agree on when it will next be refreshed. A dated file also becomes the baseline for the next update.

## Best Practices

- Start from questions. A list of questions per force keeps research focused and prevents collecting data that no rating will use.
- Prefer sources a colleague can open. A filing, a statistic or a dated interview note can be checked; "industry knowledge" cannot, so write it down as an opinion if you use it at all.
- Give complements and substitutes deliberate effort. They sit outside your category, so routine competitor tracking rarely covers them, and they are the forces most often left empty.
- Date everything. Industry structure changes, and the [Harvard strategy institute's five forces page](https://www.isc.hbs.edu/strategy/business-strategy/Pages/the-five-forces.aspx) notes that changes in regulation can change rivalry and entry barriers. An undated fact cannot be weighed against a newer one.
- Talk to people who left. Former customers and lost deals explain substitutes and buyer power better than happy customers do.
- Keep raw notes and interpretation apart. Raw notes let others check your reading, and they let the next update reuse the evidence.

## Common Mistakes

- **Collecting before defining questions**: Teams gather reports and data dumps and then try to fit them to forces. Write the questions first and collect only what answers them.
- **Treating one source as proof**: A single article or vendor claim becomes the basis for a rating. Confirm important claims from a second, independent source, or grade them low.
- **Reporting relative data as absolute**: Search trend indexes and survey percentages from small samples are presented as market facts. Label them for what they are, such as relative interest or a small sample.
- **Leading interview questions**: Asking "Would you switch if a cheaper option existed?" produces polite agreement. Ask what the person did the last time they looked at alternatives, as the [interview guidance](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/user-interviews/) recommends.
- **Hiding gaps**: A missing answer gets filled with a guess so the file looks complete. Write the gap down; the rating can then carry the right confidence.
