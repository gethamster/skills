# Applying the E-Myth Framework to Agencies

> Apply the E-Myth to agencies and service businesses: find where delivery depends on the founder and build systems so the agency can grow past it.

## Overview

Agencies, consultancies and professional service firms are the clearest case of the E-Myth. Michael Gerber's [technician manager entrepreneur framework](../../methods/technician-manager-entrepreneur-framework/METHOD.md) starts from the observation that "most businesses are started by Technicians", and EMyth's [about page](https://www.emyth.com/about-us) traces the idea to 1975, when Gerber was working at his brother-in-law's advertising agency helping small business clients build their sales process. Service firms are usually founded by a designer, developer, writer, lawyer or accountant who was good at the work and started taking clients. The founder's craft is the product, which is exactly the dependency the framework warns about.

In an agency, founder dependency shows up in specific places. Clients ask for the founder by name. Proposals and pricing wait for the founder. Quality depends on the founder reviewing everything before it ships. The team does good work but only as far as the founder's attention reaches. When the founder is at capacity, the agency is at capacity.

Gerber applied his model to specific service trades in books such as [The E-Myth Contractor](https://archive.org/details/emythcontractor00mich) and [The E-Myth Physician](https://archive.org/details/emythphysicianwh00gerb). The same logic carries over to agencies: separate the service the client buys from the founder who currently delivers it, write down how it is delivered, and move the founder's time toward running and directing the firm. The [StoryShots summary of The E-Myth Revisited](https://www.getstoryshots.com/books/the-e-myth-revisited-summary/) puts the organizing rule as building structure "based on functions, not personalities."

This skill adapts the general method to the shape of service work. Agency work is project-based, client-facing and judgment-heavy, so the systems look different from a production line: service playbooks, review gates, scoping templates and client handover rules. The founder's role shifts from doing the best work to designing how the best work gets done and deciding which clients and services the agency should pursue.

The output is a founder dependency map, documented playbooks for the core services, quality gates that replace the founder's personal review, and a transition plan for the founder's time. The general version of this work lives in the sibling skills; this page covers what is specific to agencies.

## How It Works

The dependency map is the starting point. For each service, list every step from first inquiry to final invoice and mark who does it. Steps only the founder can do are dependency points. Common ones in agencies are the sales call, scoping and pricing, creative direction, final review and senior client contact.

Services are then documented as playbooks. A playbook describes the phases of the engagement, the deliverables at each phase, the standard each deliverable must meet, the decisions the team may make and those that need escalation, and templates for recurring documents. This is the agency form of Gerber's Operations Manual, which StoryShots describes as documenting "the important innovations for each role" ([StoryShots](https://www.getstoryshots.com/books/the-e-myth-revisited-summary/)).

Quality gates replace founder review. Instead of the founder looking at everything, each gate sets explicit criteria that a senior team member checks before work moves on or goes to the client. The founder reviews a sample and the gate results. Where the criteria are hard to write, that is a sign the founder's standard has never been made explicit, and making it explicit is Manager work.

Client relationships move in stages. The founder stays visible at the start of an engagement and at key reviews, and a named account lead owns day-to-day contact. Clients hear about the change directly. StoryShots summarizes Gerber's hiring advice as "Hire ordinary people who can achieve extraordinary results with the right system" ([StoryShots](https://www.getstoryshots.com/books/the-e-myth-revisited-summary/)); in an agency, the playbooks and gates are that system.

The founder's freed time goes to the other two roles. Manager work includes maintaining playbooks, hiring and developing the team, and running delivery reviews. Entrepreneur work includes choosing which services and clients to focus on, productizing repeatable services and building referral partnerships. EMyth describes the goal as a business that "works without them rather than because of them" ([EMyth about page](https://www.emyth.com/about-us)).

## Step-by-Step Guide

### Step 1: List your services and their steps

Write down every service you sell and break each into steps from inquiry to invoice. Include sales, scoping, kickoff, delivery phases, review, handover and billing. Note which services bring in most of your revenue and which are most repeatable, since those are the best candidates for early systems.

### Step 2: Map founder dependency

For each step, mark who does it now and whether it could happen without the founder. Highlight every step where the answer is no. Add the informal dependencies too, such as the team waiting for the founder's opinion before starting. The highlighted steps are your dependency points and the core of the plan.

### Step 3: Choose the first service to systematize

Pick the service that is sold most often and delivered most consistently. Its playbook will be the easiest to write and will free the most founder time. Leave bespoke or experimental services for later, because they change too often to document well.

### Step 4: Write the service playbook

Document the phases, deliverables, standards, decisions and templates for that service. Capture the founder's judgment as explicit criteria wherever possible, for example what makes a strategy deck ready for the client. Test the playbook on a live project with a team member leading and the founder observing.

### Step 5: Set quality gates

At the key points in each playbook, define a gate with written criteria and a named checker who is not the founder. Start with the founder reviewing the gate results and a sample of the work. Reduce the founder's review as the gates prove reliable.

### Step 6: Hand over client relationships

Name an account lead for each client and introduce them while the founder is still involved. Agree what stays with the founder, such as quarterly reviews or major scope changes. Tell clients plainly who owns what, so they do not route around the new lead.

### Step 7: Move the founder's time to running the agency

Put Manager and Entrepreneur blocks on the founder's calendar with named outputs: playbook maintenance, hiring, service design, pricing and partnerships. Review the dependency map each quarter, remove the dependencies that have been resolved, and choose the next ones to tackle.

## Best Practices

- Systematize the service that sells most often first. It frees the most founder time and has the most stable process.
- Write the founder's standards down as criteria. Taste that exists only in the founder's head cannot be delegated.
- Keep the founder visible at key client moments during the transition. Clients accept a new lead more easily when the founder introduces them.
- Price and scope from templates. Custom pricing for every proposal keeps the founder in the sales bottleneck.
- Review the dependency map every quarter. New dependencies creep in as the agency adds services and clients.
- Consider productizing repeatable services. A fixed-scope offer is easier to document, sell and deliver without the founder.

## Common Mistakes

- **Hiring seniors to avoid writing systems**: A senior hire without a playbook becomes a second founder the agency depends on. Write the playbook either way.
- **Removing founder review before gates work**: Quality drops and clients notice. Keep the founder reviewing samples until the gate results are reliable.
- **Handing off clients without telling them**: Clients keep calling the founder, and the account lead has no authority. Make the change explicit.
- **Systematizing bespoke work first**: One-off projects change too much to document. Start with the repeatable service.
- **Filling freed time with more delivery**: The founder takes on a new client personally and the dependency returns. Assign the freed time to Manager and Entrepreneur work before it is freed.
