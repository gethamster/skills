# FAQ: Prioritizing and Slicing Releases on a Story Map

## How big should the first release slice be?

As small as possible while still letting your target users reach their goal. Jeff Patton's original article describes the first span as the smallest set of features necessary to be minimally useful in a business context. If the slice looks large, split cards into crude and elaborate versions, or look for workarounds, before accepting it.

## Should the first slice go to customers?

Not necessarily. Patton writes that the first span should always be the first release but need not be the first public one. Getting it working, even internally, lets testers see whether the product hangs together and lets architects check the technical foundation. Many teams show the skeleton to a few users before a wider release.

## How is slicing different from MoSCoW or other prioritization methods?

Slicing works within each column of the journey and checks the result end to end, which ranking methods applied to a list do not do. You can still use a scoring method to choose between cards inside a column or between candidate later slices. The map's contribution is making sure every release covers the whole story.

## Who draws the release lines?

The business owner accountable for the release, with the rest of the group asking questions. Patton's article describes this step as the point where the business people responsible for the release step forward. Developers contribute estimates and risks, and designers and testers check that the slice is usable.

## What do I do with cards below every line?

Leave them on the map. They record ideas the team has not committed to, and some will move up as you learn from users. Atlassian's guide shows teams labelling such slices as needs validation or not now. Remove cards only when the team agrees they no longer fit the product's goals.
