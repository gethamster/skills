# RACE Framework Journey Map: How to Build One

> Build a RACE framework journey map: a research-based RACE customer journey map of goals, questions, touchpoints and gaps for each of the four stages.

## Overview

A RACE framework journey map is a customer journey map whose phases are the four stages of the [RACE Framework](../../methods/race-framework/METHOD.md): Reach, Act, Convert and Engage. It shows one persona's path through those stages from the customer's side: what they are trying to do, what questions they have, where they meet your brand, how they feel, and where they get stuck. Your team's KPIs and owners sit underneath, so every insight connects to someone who can act on it.

Nielsen Norman Group defines a journey map as a visualization of the process a person goes through in order to accomplish a goal, and lists five elements: an actor, a scenario with expectations, journey phases, the actor's actions, mindsets and emotions, and opportunities ([NN/g: Journey Mapping 101](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/journey-mapping-101/)). A RACE customer journey map uses the RACE stages as the phases. That choice ties the map directly to the marketing plan, because each column already has a RACE objective and KPIs.

The map complements, and does not replace, the planning artifacts in this method. The [RACE funnel mapping](../mapping-customer-journey-stages-to-race/SKILL.md) skill produces an alignment matrix of journey stages, touchpoints and owners. The journey map adds the customer's perspective: the questions they ask in Act, the doubts that stop them in Convert, the moments after purchase that decide whether they come back. Those details come from research, which is what makes the map worth the effort.

Dave Chaffey's stage definitions give each column its purpose. Reach is building awareness and visibility to drive visits; Act, short for Interact, is persuading the visitor to take the next step on their journey; Convert is the sale, online or offline; Engage is the long-term relationship that builds loyalty and repeat purchase ([Chaffey: RACE marketing model](https://www.davechaffey.com/digital-marketing-glossary/race-marketing-planning-model/)). The output of this skill is one map per key persona, a prioritized list of opportunities, and a date for the next review.

## How It Works

The map is a grid. The columns are the four RACE stages, optionally split into sub-stages where your journey needs them. The rows describe the customer and your response.

| Row | What it captures |
|---|---|
| Customer goal | What the persona is trying to achieve at this stage |
| Questions and mindset | What they need to know or believe to move on, in their words |
| Touchpoints | Where they meet you, online and offline |
| Emotions and friction | How the stage feels, and what slows or stops them |
| Your KPIs and owner | How you measure the stage and who answers for it |
| Opportunities | Specific changes that would help the customer move on |

The top rows must come from research. NN/g says actors' actions should be rooted in data and that mindsets are ideally customer verbatims from research ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/journey-mapping-101/)). In practice that means a handful of interviews with recent customers and with prospects who did not buy, reviews of support tickets and sales call notes, and analytics paths. A map written in a workshop without customer input tends to describe the journey the team intended, which hides the real friction.

The bottom rows connect the map to RACE. For each column, bring in the stage KPIs and the latest numbers, so the team can see whether the friction customers describe shows up in the data. A drop between two stages in analytics, such as many visitors who view products but never add one to a basket, tells you where to look. The interviews tell you why.

Transitions deserve their own attention. Many useful findings sit between columns: the point where a Reach visitor decides whether to interact, or where an Act lead waits for a sales response. Smart Insights stresses that Act leads need managing separately from final conversion, through techniques such as retargeting and assisted selling ([Smart Insights: The RACE Framework](https://www.smartinsights.com/digital-marketing-strategy/race-a-practical-framework-to-improve-your-digital-marketing/)). Mark each transition on the map with what currently moves the customer on, if anything.

Opportunities close the loop. Each one should name the stage, the customer problem it addresses, the KPI it should move and an owner. Rank them by likely impact and effort, and carry the top few into the RACE planning template.

## Step-by-Step Guide

### Step 1: Choose the persona and scenario

Pick one persona or segment and one scenario, such as a first purchase of your main product. Write down the persona's goal and expectations, following the actor and scenario elements in [NN/g's guide](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/journey-mapping-101/). If several personas matter, map the most valuable one first. A map that tries to cover everyone ends up describing no one.

### Step 2: Set up the grid

Create a grid with the four RACE stages as columns and the rows listed above. Split a column into sub-stages if your journey needs it, such as "trial" and "upgrade" under Convert. Use your RACE funnel mapping to decide which of your journey stages belong in each column. Keep the grid readable on one screen or one printed page.

### Step 3: Gather research

Interview recent customers and prospects who did not buy, asking them to walk through what they did, what they wanted to know and what nearly stopped them. Review support tickets, sales notes and reviews for recurring questions. Pull analytics paths and stage-by-stage numbers. Collect direct quotes, because they carry more weight in the map than summaries.

### Step 4: Fill in the customer rows

For each stage, write the customer's goal, their questions and mindset, their touchpoints and the friction they meet, using quotes and evidence from the research. Include offline touchpoints such as phone calls or showroom visits, which [Chaffey](https://www.davechaffey.com/digital-marketing-glossary/race-marketing-planning-model/) builds into RACE. Mark assumptions clearly where you lack evidence. Keep each cell short.

### Step 5: Add KPIs, owners and data

Under each stage, add the stage KPIs with current values, and the owner. Compare what customers told you with what the numbers show. Where a stage looks healthy in the data but painful in interviews, or the reverse, note it for investigation. Mark the transitions between stages and what currently moves customers across them.

### Step 6: Identify and rank opportunities

List the opportunities the map reveals, each tied to a stage, a customer problem and a KPI. Rank them by expected impact and effort. Pick a small number to act on first, with owners and dates. Record the rest in a backlog so they are not lost.

### Step 7: Review with the teams who own each stage

Walk marketing, sales and customer success through the map. Ask each team to check the stages they own against what they see every day, and correct the map where they have better evidence. Agree the first opportunities and add them to the RACE planning template. Share the map where every team can find it.

### Step 8: Revisit the map on a schedule

Update the map at each planning cycle, and sooner when something changes the journey, such as a new channel or product. Repeat a few interviews each time to check whether the friction you fixed has gone. Keep dated versions so the team can see how the journey changed.

## Best Practices

- Base every customer row on research. [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/journey-mapping-101/) recommends that mindsets be customer verbatims, which keeps the map honest.
- Map one persona at a time. Different personas often take different paths through Act and Convert, and one blended map hides both.
- Interview people who did not buy. They explain the friction in Act and Convert that customers who did buy got past.
- Include offline moments. Calls, visits and events are part of the journey, and Chaffey designed RACE to cover them ([Chaffey](https://www.davechaffey.com/digital-marketing-glossary/race-marketing-planning-model/)).
- Put the numbers under the stories. Pairing KPIs with quotes shows whether a complaint affects many customers or a few.
- End with owners. Every opportunity on the map needs a name and a date, or the map becomes a poster.

## Common Mistakes

- **Mapping from the inside**: A map built from the team's assumptions describes the intended journey. Talk to customers before filling in the customer rows.
- **Stopping at the purchase**: Leaving Engage thin misses the moments that decide repeat purchase. Map onboarding, support and renewal with the same care.
- **Too much detail**: A map that needs a scroll bar in every cell will not be read. Keep cells short and link to evidence.
- **No link to KPIs**: Without stage KPIs, the map cannot show which friction matters most. Add them under each stage.
- **Making it once**: Journeys change as channels and products change. Put the next review on the calendar before you finish.
