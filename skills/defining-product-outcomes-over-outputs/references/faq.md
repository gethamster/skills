# FAQ: Defining Product Outcomes Over Outputs

## How do I define a product outcome when leadership only communicates in outputs?

Start by accepting the output they've given you, then work backward to understand the 'why' behind it. Ask: 'What business problem will this feature solve? How will we know it was successful?' Their answers reveal the implicit outcome they care about.

Then propose: 'It sounds like the real goal is [behavior change]. If we focus on that outcome, we might discover that [their feature] is the right solution, or we might find something that works even better. Can we commit to the outcome and let the solution emerge from discovery?' Most leaders will agree when you frame it as reducing risk rather than rejecting their idea.

If they insist on the output, negotiate for the outcome as an additional success criterion.

## How long should a product outcome last before we revisit it?

A standard cadence for most teams is used for discovery cycles. This gives enough time to conduct discovery, ship experiments, and observe behavioral change, most product outcomes require 4-8 weeks of experimentation before you see meaningful movement. Revisiting earlier than 6 weeks usually means you haven't given the outcome enough time. Revisiting later than a certain timeframe usually means you're holding onto a stale outcome.

The exception is when you hit your target early, in that case, raise the bar or move to a new outcome. Always have a formal review at quarter boundaries, even if you choose to continue pursuing the same outcome.

## Should I define a product outcome before or after building my opportunity solution tree?

Before. The product outcome is the root node of your [opportunity solution tree](https://tryhamster.com/skills/building-opportunity-solution-trees), without it, you have no basis for organizing or prioritizing opportunities. In practice, you'll sometimes start customer interviews before your outcome is fully defined, and that's fine, early conversations can actually help you identify which behaviors matter. But formalize the outcome before you start structuring your tree and making prioritization decisions.

Otherwise, you'll collect a grab bag of interesting insights with no evaluative framework for deciding which ones to pursue.

## How do I handle a product outcome that isn't moving despite weeks of discovery work?

First, diagnose where the breakdown is occurring. Check three things: Are you actually shipping solutions, or is discovery work stuck in the research phase? Are the solutions you've shipped reaching enough users to move the metric? Is the metric instrumented correctly, and are you looking at the right time window?

If you're shipping and the metric isn't moving, revisit your opportunity solution tree, you may be working on opportunities with low leverage. Pull your customer interview notes and look for opportunities you've been ignoring. If multiple well-targeted solutions fail to move the metric, the causal link between the behavior and the business metric may be weaker than you assumed. In that case, return to Step 2 and re-evaluate your candidate behaviors with your new data.

## Can two teams share the same product outcome?

Generally no, shared outcomes create diffusion of responsibility and make it impossible to attribute impact. If two teams are both trying to move the same metric, they'll either duplicate efforts or avoid risky experiments because each team assumes the other will move the number. The better approach is to decompose the shared metric into distinct sub-behaviors that each team owns exclusively. For example, if both teams care about retention, one might own 'first-week activation behaviors' and the other 'recurring engagement patterns in weeks 2-8.'

Each team gets a metric they can independently influence and be accountable for, while both contribute to the shared business metric.

## What's the difference between a product outcome and an OKR key result?

A product outcome is a specific type of key result, one focused on measurable customer behavior change rather than output delivery. Many OKR key results are actually outputs ('Launch feature X'), milestones ('Complete migration by Q2'), or activity metrics ('Conduct 20 user interviews'). A product outcome as a key result would be 'Increase the percentage of users who [behavior] from X% to Y%.' You can use product outcomes within an OKR framework, they make excellent key results because they're measurable, time-bound, and oriented toward impact rather than activity.

The outcome replaces output-focused key results, not the OKR structure itself.

## Why does my product outcome keep drifting back toward an output during team discussions?

This happens because output-thinking is the organizational default, it's concrete, familiar, and feels safe. Outputs give teams certainty ('we'll build X by date Y'), while outcomes introduce ambiguity ('we'll try to move metric X, but we're not sure how yet'). The drift usually surfaces when someone says 'but what are we actually building?' in a planning meeting. Counter this by keeping the outcome visible and by structuring conversations around opportunities rather than features.

When someone proposes a feature, redirect with: 'That's an interesting solution, which opportunity on our tree does it address, and how might it move our outcome?' Over time, the team internalizes this pattern. The first 4-6 weeks are the hardest; after that, outcome-thinking becomes habitual.
