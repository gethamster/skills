# FAQ: Running a Shape Up Betting Table Session

## Who should sit at the betting table?

The people with final authority over the product and over the teams' time. At Basecamp that was the CEO, the CTO, a senior programmer and the product strategist. Keep the group small; the book stresses that because the highest people are there, no second approval step is needed.

## How long should a betting table session last?

Short. The book says Basecamp's call rarely went longer than an hour or two, because everyone had read the pitches beforehand. If sessions run much longer, check whether unshaped ideas or design debates are creeping in.

## What happens to pitches that are not chosen?

They are let go. There is no backlog of passed pitches. Anyone who still wants one can track it their own way, rework it, and lobby for it at a later table. The book's view is that important ideas come back on their own.

## How is the betting table different from backlog grooming or sprint planning?

It chooses among a few shaped, de-risked options instead of ordering a long list, and it commits a whole cycle of uninterrupted team time to each choice. It also assigns specific people to each bet. Nothing unfinished rolls in automatically; old work only returns as a new pitch.

## Can the betting table change a pitch?

It can reject a pitch, or ask whether a smaller appetite would change the decision, but it should not redesign the solution in the meeting. The book warns against doing design work at the table. If the solution needs to change, send it back to the shaper.

## Do we need a betting table if there is only one candidate project?

Not necessarily. In Ryan Singer's recent case study, leadership agreed on one candidate before it was framed and shaped, so once it was shaped no further bet was needed. The table matters most when several shaped options compete for limited team time.
