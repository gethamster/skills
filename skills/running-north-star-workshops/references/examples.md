# Examples: Running a North Star Framework Workshop

## A first workshop for a subscription software team

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a team-collaboration product has grown to several product teams, each reporting its own success metric. The head of product sponsors a first North Star workshop with eight people: product, design, engineering, analytics, finance, marketing, sales and customer success.

**Walkthrough:**

The facilitator, a product operations lead with no team of her own to defend, rehearses the exercises the week before and sends a one-page pre-read. In the room, naming the game causes the first argument: marketing assumes an attention game, while customer success says customers buy the product to get work done. The group settles on the productivity game, which rules out time-in-product candidates straight away.

The statement exercise takes longer than planned. The group agrees that growth depends on teams finishing shared projects with less back-and-forth, and lists three inputs in words before anyone proposes a formula. After the silent drafting round, the board holds four candidates. The group eliminates two, keeps "teams completing a shared project each week" and "projects with at least two active collaborators," and records two open questions for analytics. Two refinement sessions go on the calendar for the following week.

## A workshop that stalls on measurement

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a small online learning company runs its workshop with six people. Halfway through, the data lead objects to every candidate because the event tracking cannot support it yet.

**Walkthrough:**

The facilitator parks each measurement objection on a separate sheet labelled "how would we measure this" and brings the group back to the statement. The team agrees in words that value comes from learners applying a lesson at work, which none of its current metrics capture.

In the convergence round, the group keeps a candidate built on learners completing a practical exercise, even though the event does not exist yet. The data lead takes the parked sheet as an action item and estimates the tracking work before the first follow-up. The workshop ends with a concept everyone supports and a concrete plan to make it measurable, instead of a weaker metric chosen only because it was easy to count.

## A multi-product company that wants several North Stars

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a company sells three add-on modules to the same small-business customers. Each module lead arrives expecting a separate North Star.

**Walkthrough:**

During the game discussion, the facilitator asks who the customer is for each module. The answers are the same business owners, buying the modules as one package. The group runs the warm-up on a familiar consumer app, which shows how one North Star can sit above several inputs.

When the group drafts its own, the module leads each propose an input rather than a separate North Star. The session ends with one candidate metric for the whole package and three inputs, one per module, plus an open question about whether one of the modules serves a different buyer. The sponsor agrees to settle that question with customer interviews before the second session.
