# FAQ: Aligning Teams Around Customer Journey Ownership

## What does a journey owner actually do?

A journey owner keeps an up-to-date understanding of the journey, tracks its metrics, surfaces problems, and coordinates the teams whose work shapes it. They make the case for improvements in planning forums and escalate when they need resources from other teams. They usually do not build the changes themselves. Their accountability is for the journey as a whole.

## Should journey owners be full-time roles?

It depends on size and maturity. Many organizations start by adding journey ownership to an existing role, such as a product manager or service lead. Larger organizations often create dedicated journey manager roles or a journey management office. Whichever model you choose, the ownership work needs to be recognized in the person's objectives.

## How many journeys can one person own?

Nielsen Norman Group advises that a journey manager own a single customer journey, because managing several well is not feasible. In practice, owners of broad levels, such as a lifecycle stage, oversee several journeys through the journey owners below them. The warning sign is an owner who cannot name the current top issue in each journey they own.

## Where should journey owners report?

They need a line of sight into every team that affects their journey, so reporting to a senior leader with influence across those teams tends to work best. Common homes include the CX function, product, or a dedicated journey management office. The right answer depends on where the organization's decisions about roadmaps and budgets are made.

## How do we handle conflicts between journeys?

Write an escalation path before conflicts happen. Owners of journeys under the same stage escalate to the stage owner, and conflicts between stages go to the lifecycle owner or executive sponsor. Each side brings evidence, and the decision and reasoning are recorded. Reviewing the effect afterwards shows whether the decision worked.
