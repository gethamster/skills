# Creating Circular Consumer Journey Maps

> Create a circular customer journey map from real buyer data, using the four CDJ phases and a loyalty loop instead of a straight funnel.

## Overview

A circular customer journey map shows the path a buyer takes as a loop instead of a line. It uses the four phases of the [McKinsey Consumer Decision Journey](https://tryhamster.com/methods/mckinsey-consumer-decision-journey): initial consideration, active evaluation, the moment of purchase and postpurchase. The loop matters because, as [the original McKinsey article](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/growth-marketing-and-sales/our-insights/the-consumer-decision-journey) puts it, the postpurchase experience shapes the buyer's opinion for every later decision in the category, so the journey is an ongoing cycle. A satisfied customer can take the loyalty loop, skipping straight from trigger to purchase next time.

A linear funnel map cannot show two things the circular map makes visible. The first is that brands can be added during evaluation, not only removed, so the set of options widens and narrows over time. The second is the return path: what happens after purchase feeds the next round of consideration, for the buyer and for the people they talk to. A map that ends at the sale leaves out the phase that decides whether the next sale is cheap or expensive.

The map is a shared tool for a cross-functional team. Marketing, sales, product and support each own parts of the loop, and the map lets them see how their touchpoints connect. It is only useful if it is built from evidence about real buyers, kept to one clear point of view, and updated as behavior changes.

This skill covers building the map. Measuring each phase in depth is covered by the sibling skills on consideration sets, active evaluation, purchase triggers, loyalty loops and touchpoints, and the map is where their findings come together.

## How It Works

The map borrows its structure from standard journey mapping and its shape from the CDJ. The Nielsen Norman Group's [Journey Mapping 101](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/journey-mapping-101/) describes the common components: an actor, a scenario with the actor's expectations, journey phases, the actor's actions, thoughts and emotions in each phase, and opportunities for improvement with internal owners. It recommends one point of view per map, with actions rooted in data. A circular CDJ map keeps all of these and arranges the phases in a loop.

Around the loop, each phase gets its own layer of information. For initial consideration, record the trigger and the brands the buyer recalls. For active evaluation, record the touchpoints used and the brands added and dropped, marking each touchpoint as consumer-driven or company-driven. [McKinsey found](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/growth-marketing-and-sales/our-insights/the-consumer-decision-journey) that two-thirds of touchpoints in evaluation were consumer-driven, so a map showing only your own channels will look emptier than the buyer's real experience. For the moment of purchase, record what tipped the decision and what nearly stopped it. For postpurchase, record onboarding, use, support and the moments that lead to advocacy or to shopping around again.

Two paths leave the postpurchase phase. One goes back to initial consideration: the customer's next need starts a new journey, with their experience shaping which brands they recall. The other is the loyalty loop, which goes directly to purchase. The map should show both, with evidence for how many customers take each path and what separates them.

Emotions and thoughts are what make the map useful beyond a list of touchpoints. A buyer who feels uncertain during evaluation needs different help from one who feels overwhelmed by options. Capture these from interviews in the buyer's own words, and mark the high and low points, since the low points are usually where the opportunities are.

The final layer is opportunities and owners. For each weak point, write what could change and which team owns it. Without owners, a journey map becomes a poster. With them, it becomes a working plan that can be checked against the next round of research.

## Step-by-Step Guide

### Step 1: Choose the Actor and Scenario

Pick one buyer type and one buying scenario for the map, such as a first-time buyer replacing an existing product. Write down what triggers the journey and what the buyer expects to achieve. If you need several buyer types, plan several maps instead of merging them, since a merged map describes nobody. Keep the scope narrow enough to research properly.

### Step 2: Gather Evidence

Collect data for all four phases: interviews with recent buyers, including some who chose a competitor, consideration and satisfaction surveys, web and search analytics, sales notes and support records. Ask interviewees to walk through their journey in order and to describe what they thought and felt at each point. Mark which findings come from many sources and which from one. Note gaps in the evidence so the map does not fill them with assumptions.

### Step 3: Draw the Loop

Lay out the four phases in a circle: initial consideration, active evaluation, moment of purchase and postpurchase. Draw the return path from postpurchase to initial consideration and the loyalty loop from postpurchase directly to purchase. Keep the shape simple enough to read at a glance. The information goes in layers around it.

### Step 4: Fill in Each Phase

For each phase, add the buyer's actions, the touchpoints they used, the brands in their set, and their thoughts and emotions. Mark touchpoints as consumer-driven or company-driven, and show where brands were added or dropped. Use the buyer's words for thoughts and emotions. Mark the high and low points of the experience.

### Step 5: Quantify the Loops

Add evidence for how buyers move through the loop: how many recall your brand at the start, how many add or drop it during evaluation, how many complete the purchase, and how many take the loyalty loop versus re-entering evaluation. Where you lack exact data, mark the estimate as an estimate. These numbers show which phase loses the most buyers and whether the loyalty loop is growing.

### Step 6: Identify Opportunities and Owners

For each low point or weak phase, write the opportunity in a sentence and name the team that owns it. Rank the opportunities by how many buyers they affect and how much they change the outcome. Keep the list short enough that owners can act on it. Link each opportunity to a metric so you can tell later whether it worked.

### Step 7: Review and Update

Present the map to the teams that own each phase and correct it with their knowledge, while keeping it anchored in buyer evidence. Set a date to refresh it with new research. Update it after major product, pricing or competitive changes. Keep old versions so you can see how the journey has shifted, and follow the [Nielsen Norman Group's advice](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/journey-mapping-101/) to base the actor's actions on data rather than on internal opinion.

## Best Practices

- Keep one actor and one scenario per map. Mixing buyer types produces a journey nobody actually takes, and the opportunities it suggests fit nobody well.
- Show both exits from the postpurchase phase. The loyalty loop and the return to consideration are the two outcomes the CDJ cares about most, and a map that shows only one hides the choice the business is trying to influence.
- Include touchpoints you do not own. Reviews, peers and comparison sites often carry more weight in evaluation than company channels, so leaving them off understates what shapes the decision.
- Record where brands enter and leave the set. This is the behavior the funnel cannot show, and it is often where challenger brands win and incumbents lose.
- Write opportunities with owners and metrics. A map that ends with observations rarely changes anything, while one that ends with assigned actions can be checked in the next review.
- Mark evidence strength. Distinguish findings supported by many interviews and data from single anecdotes, so readers know which parts of the map to trust.

## Common Mistakes

- **Building the map from internal opinion**: A map drawn in a workshop without buyer research reflects what the team believes. Gather evidence first and use the workshop to interpret it.
- **Drawing a circle around a funnel**: Arranging funnel stages in a circle does not make a CDJ map. Show additions during evaluation and the two exits from postpurchase, or the map is still a funnel.
- **Stopping at the sale**: Maps often end with the purchase because that is where marketing's responsibility used to end. Include onboarding, use, support and advocacy, since they decide the next journey.
- **Leaving out emotions**: A list of touchpoints without the buyer's thoughts and feelings shows what happened but not why. Capture both from interviews.
- **Treating the map as finished**: Buyer behavior, touchpoints and competitors change. Schedule updates and keep versions, so the map remains a working tool.
