# Examples: Identifying User Activities and Building the Story Backbone

## Example: E-Commerce Mobile App (Small Startup Team)

**Scenario:**

A 5-person startup is building a mobile app for buying vintage clothing. The team includes a product manager, two engineers, a designer, and a part-time marketer. They have 30 minutes for backbone creation during a sprint planning session. Their target user is a fashion-conscious millennial browsing on their phone during commute time.

**Walkthrough:**

" Each person silently writes activities on sticky notes for 5 minutes. They generate 18 cards total. After clustering duplicates, they have 11 candidates: Discover the app, Browse collections, Search for specific items, View item details, Save favorites, Check sizing, Add to cart, Enter payment, Confirm order, Track delivery, Leave a review. " The final backbone has 6 activities: Explore inventory, Search for items, Evaluate item, Purchase item, Track delivery, Share feedback.

They read it aloud as a story and it flows naturally. The designer notes that the user's biggest anxiety is at "Evaluate item" because sizing for vintage is unpredictable. This annotation is added to the card. The entire exercise takes 25 minutes.

## Example: B2B SaaS Onboarding Flow (Mid-Size Company)

**Scenario:**

A B2B project management tool company with 40 employees wants to map the onboarding journey for new team administrators. The product team of 8 people runs a dedicated 90-minute backbone workshop. Their target user is an IT administrator at a 200-person company who has been tasked with rolling out the tool to their organization.

**Walkthrough:**

" Silent brainstorming produces 26 candidate activities across 8 participants. After clustering, 14 distinct activities remain. The team notices that many activities map to their product's settings pages rather than user goals. " The team settles on 7 backbone activities: Evaluate the tool, Get organizational buy-in, Configure the workspace, Import existing data, Invite and onboard team members, Establish workflows, Monitor adoption.

The customer success lead flags that "Get organizational buy-in" often happens before the admin ever signs up, confirming it belongs on the backbone. She also notes that "Monitor adoption" is frequently skipped, leading to failed rollouts, making it critical to keep visible. Validation against 5 recent onboarding support threads confirms the ordering and reveals that "Import existing data" is a much bigger pain point than the team assumed, which changes how they will prioritize tasks beneath that activity.

## Example: Consumer Health App (Large Cross-Functional Team)

**Scenario:**

A health tech company with 200 employees is redesigning their diabetes management app. The product team runs a half-day story mapping workshop with 12 participants: product managers, clinicians, engineers, designers, and a patient advocate. Their target user is a newly diagnosed Type 2 diabetes patient learning to manage their condition.

**Walkthrough:**

" Given the large group, they split into three sub-teams of 4 for silent brainstorming, generating over 40 candidate activities. " After clustering across all three groups, 16 candidates remain. The group uses dot voting to identify the 8 most essential activities. The final backbone: Learn about diabetes, Set up monitoring routine, Log daily meals and activity, Check blood sugar readings, Identify patterns, Consult care team, Adjust management plan, Track long-term progress.

The patient advocate validates this against her own experience and flags that "Learn about diabetes" is not a one-time activity but recurs throughout the journey. The team decides to keep it as the first backbone activity representing the initial learning phase, with a note that educational content surfaces contextually under other activities as well. Reading the backbone aloud, the group agrees it tells a coherent story. The clinician observes that the emotional weight shifts dramatically from left (fear, confusion) to right (confidence, routine), and these emotional annotations are added to each card for future design reference.

## Example: Internal Tool for Operations Team (B2B, No External Users)

**Scenario:**

An operations team at a logistics company wants to map the journey for their internal dispatchers who use a custom-built routing tool. The team of 4 (ops manager, two dispatchers, one developer) has 60 minutes. The user is a dispatcher managing 50-80 deliveries per shift.

**Walkthrough:**

" Because the dispatchers are in the room, brainstorming is grounded in reality from the start. They generate 12 candidate activities. The dispatchers immediately flag that the developer's suggestions ("Log into system," "Check system status") are system-level, not user-level activities. After merging, 8 activities remain: Review incoming orders, Plan delivery routes, Assign drivers, Communicate route details, Monitor active deliveries, Handle exceptions and re-routes, Close out completed deliveries, Report shift summary.

One dispatcher points out that "Handle exceptions" is not a sequential step but happens continuously throughout the shift. The team places it in the position where it most commonly first occurs (after monitoring begins) and annotates it as a recurring activity. The backbone is validated by walking through yesterday's actual shift log. The ops manager discovers that "Communicate route details" takes far more time than anyone on the product side realized, suggesting an automation opportunity.

The final backbone of 8 activities fits naturally because the dispatcher workflow is genuinely complex with distinct phases.
