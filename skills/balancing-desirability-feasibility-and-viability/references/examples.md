# Examples: Balancing Desirability, Feasibility, and Viability

## Choosing between three onboarding concepts

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a small software team has three onboarding concepts for new accounts: a guided setup wizard, a human-led kickoff call, and a template library.

**Walkthrough:**

The design lead reports that research participants struggled most with blank-page setup, so all three concepts address a real need. The engineering lead rates the wizard and template library as buildable with the current stack, but the kickoff call needs no engineering at all. The operations lead flags that kickoff calls do not scale: at the team's imagined growth rate they would need several extra staff. Rather than drop the call, the team reshapes it into a template library with an optional call for the largest accounts.

The action plan names viability of the optional call as the weakest lens and schedules a cost check after the first month.

## A loved feature that could not be built yet

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a clinic scheduling team finds that patients react strongly to a concept that predicts appointment delays in real time.

**Walkthrough:**

Desirability evidence is observed: patients in a prototype test changed when they left home based on the mock delay estimate. The engineering lead explains the clinic systems only update in batches, so real-time prediction is not feasible this year. The team asks what version would pass feasibility and lands on a simpler notification sent when the clinician starts running late, triggered by front-desk staff. Viability is checked with the clinic manager, who confirms staff can absorb the extra step.

The full prediction concept is parked with a revival condition tied to a system upgrade.

## A viable concept nobody needed

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a retail team favours a paid loyalty tier because it fits the revenue plan and reuses existing systems.

**Walkthrough:**

Feasibility and viability both score well on strong evidence, and the business lead pushes to advance it. The design lead points out that the desirability claim rests only on assumed evidence and that research showed customers valued faster returns, not perks. The team marks desirability as the weakest lens instead of a pass. They combine the paid tier with a faster-returns benefit and plan a small prototype test with existing customers.

The decision record notes that the original concept would have advanced on two lenses alone.
