# Writing Competency-Based PM Job Descriptions

> Write a competency-based job description for a PM role from Cabage's grid, turning product manager responsibilities into clear PM hiring criteria.

## Overview

A competency-based job description names the specific work a PM role will own and the evidence a candidate needs to show, instead of listing every skill a product manager could have. The [Product Team Competencies Framework](../../methods/product-team-competencies-framework/METHOD.md) makes this practical. Neal Cabage says his chart can be used to think through skills gaps on the team or "what type of PM is needed for a given type of product role that you're hiring for" ([Cabage, Product Team Competencies](https://nealcabage.com/framework/product-team-competencies/)).

The job description starts where the team assessment ends. If the greyed-out team chart shows a gap in strategic Users and Market work, as in Cabage's example, the next hire's home region is the upper-left, and the job description should say so in plain terms. A PM who would only deepen boxes the team already covers adds less, however strong they are.

The grid also gives the role requirements their structure. Pick the role's home region using Cabage's two readings, seniority for height and product type for left or right. Choose a handful of core boxes from that region and turn each one into a responsibility line and a requirement line. Everything else on the chart stays out of the ad or appears as a nice-to-have.

Marty Cagan's sample [Product Manager Job Description](https://www.svpg.com/product-manager-job-description/) is a useful reference for tone and for what every PM must bring. He writes that the PM is accountable for ensuring the solution is valuable and viable, and must contribute knowledge of the business's constraints, of users and customers and the data about how they engage, and of industry trends and the competitive landscape. He also states that the description intentionally has no requirement for specific domain knowledge. His qualifications are written as demonstrated abilities, which is the same idea as tying each requirement to evidence.

The output is a job description with four parts: a short summary that names the role's home region in ordinary language, responsibilities drawn from the core boxes, requirements phrased as evidence for those boxes, and a short list of secondary boxes. The same core boxes then drive the interview rubric, so the ad and the interview loop test the same things.

## How It Works

The PM hiring criteria come from three sources. The team gap assessment says which boxes the team needs covered. The role profile says which region of the chart this role type sits in. The leveling matrix says what good work in each box looks like at the level you are hiring. Together they give you a short list of core boxes, usually a handful, all in or near the role's home region.

Each core box becomes one responsibility. A box label is too abstract for an ad, so translate it into the work at your company: Competitive Gap Analysis becomes "Lead our competitive analysis for the analytics suite and turn it into roadmap proposals." Keep responsibilities concrete enough that a candidate can picture a week in the job.

Each core box also becomes one requirement, phrased as evidence. Instead of "strong strategic skills," write "You have led at least one market positioning effort for a product and can walk us through the decisions." Evidence-based requirements tell candidates what to bring to the interview and give interviewers something to check. Cagan's qualifications follow the same pattern, with lines such as "Demonstrated ability to learn multiple functional areas of business" ([SVPG](https://www.svpg.com/product-manager-job-description/)).

Product manager responsibilities that every PM shares, such as working with design and engineering on the roadmap and backlog, can be stated once in a general paragraph. They sit in the Core Product column of Cabage's chart and do not distinguish one role from another. The ad should spend most of its words on the boxes that make this role different.

Secondary boxes are optional. List a few as nice-to-haves only if they would genuinely change a hiring decision between two otherwise equal candidates. A long nice-to-have list turns the ad back into a wish list and discourages good candidates who meet the core requirements.

## Step-by-Step Guide

### Step 1: Start from the team gap

Pull up the latest team chart and identify the gap this hire should fill. Write one sentence describing it in terms of the grid, such as "nobody owns the strategic Market boxes." If there is no clear gap, question whether the role should be a backfill of a specific profile instead. Share the sentence with the hiring manager before writing anything else.

### Step 2: Set the role's home region and level

Place the role on the chart using seniority for height and product type for left or right. A senior consumer role sits upper-left, and a junior internal tools role sits lower-right, following Cabage's examples. Check the placement against the role profile if one exists. Name the level and its expectations from the leveling matrix.

### Step 3: Choose the core boxes

Pick a handful of boxes from the home region that the role must own from the first months. Favor boxes that fill the team gap. Mark two or three more as secondary if they matter. Leave all other boxes out.

### Step 4: Write responsibilities from the core boxes

Turn each core box into one line describing the real work at your company. Use the names of your products and the decisions the PM will make. Add one short paragraph for shared PM work in Core Product. Keep the list short enough to read in a minute.

### Step 5: Write requirements as evidence

For each core box, write one requirement describing what a candidate should have done and be able to discuss. Avoid trait words such as strategic, visionary or data-driven unless you attach the evidence. Drop requirements such as domain knowledge unless the role truly needs them, as Cagan does in his sample description.

### Step 6: Check the draft against the grid

Map every line of the draft back to a box. Lines with no box are either shared PM work or noise. If the responsibilities spread across the whole chart, the role is unfocused, so cut back to the home region. Confirm the draft with the hiring manager and one PM on the team.

### Step 7: Hand the core boxes to the interview loop

Pass the list of core boxes and requirements to whoever designs the interview rubric. Each core box should be assessed by at least one interviewer. Keep the job description and rubric together so later edits stay in sync.

## Best Practices

- Hire for the gap. Cabage's [example of a team lacking external market strategy](https://nealcabage.com/framework/product-team-competencies/) shows why the next hire should strengthen the grey region.
- Keep core boxes to a handful. A role that owns the whole chart attracts nobody in particular.
- Phrase requirements as evidence a candidate can show. [Cagan's sample description](https://www.svpg.com/product-manager-job-description/) uses demonstrated abilities throughout.
- Separate shared PM work from what makes the role different. Shared Core Product work gets one paragraph.
- Question domain requirements. Cagan leaves them out of his sample on purpose, and they narrow the candidate pool.
- Reuse the core boxes in the interview rubric so the ad and the loop test the same things.

## Common Mistakes

- **Copying a generic PM template**: Generic ads list every competency and describe no particular role. Start from your team's gap and the role's region.
- **Trait words without evidence**: "Strategic thinker" and "customer obsessed" cannot be tested. Say what the candidate should have done.
- **A requirements list covering the whole chart**: This describes a superhuman generalist and scares off strong specialists. Cut to the home region.
- **Hiring a copy of the strongest PM**: Writing the ad around the best current PM deepens covered boxes and leaves gaps open.
- **Ads that drift from the interview loop**: When the job description and rubric test different things, the loop cannot confirm what the ad promised.
