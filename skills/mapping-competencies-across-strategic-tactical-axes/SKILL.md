> Map product management competencies on Neal Cabage's strategic-tactical and external-internal axes to build a PM competency framework for your team.

## Overview

Mapping product management competencies is the first job in the [Product Team Competencies Framework](../../methods/product-team-competencies-framework/METHOD.md). Neal Cabage's chart already places the competencies for you: Strategic at the top, Tactical at the bottom, External on the left and Internal on the right, with five categories along the middle band (Users, Market, Core Product, Business and Technical) and three boxes above and three below each category ([Cabage, Product Team Competencies](https://nealcabage.com/framework/product-team-competencies/)). Your task is to turn that published grid into a PM competency framework that fits your company, without losing the structure that makes it useful.

This matters because every later use of the framework reads from the map. Assessments rate PMs against its boxes, job descriptions quote them, and interview rubrics test them. If one manager reads "Business Case" as a slide for leadership and another reads it as a full financial model, those downstream tools disagree without anyone noticing.

The work is mostly definition and pruning. You keep Cabage's axes and categories, write a short description of what each box looks like in your company's work, and mark boxes that your products do not need. A company with no product council, for example, can mark that box as not applicable instead of inventing a meaning for it. You add a box only when real, recurring PM work has no home on the chart.

The map also carries Cabage's two readings of the axes. Height tracks seniority, since he places junior PMs at the bottom and senior PMs, "especially team leads, Directors," at the top. Left and right track product type, since technical PMs on internal tools and platforms work on the right side and PMs on consumer-facing products on the left. Recording these readings on the map lets later skills place roles on it quickly.

The output is a single page: the grid, a definition per box, a relevance mark per box, and two short notes on how your company reads the axes. It takes about an hour with one or two reviewers, and it should be revisited when the product mix changes.

## How It Works

The chart works because the two axes separate kinds of work that a flat skill list mixes together, which is why it is a useful way to talk about strategic vs tactical PM skills. Strategic versus tactical separates deciding what to build and why from getting it built and measured. External versus internal separates work aimed at users and the market from work aimed at the business and the technology. A flat list such as the twelve competencies in Ravi Mehta's [Product Competency Toolkit](https://www.ravi-mehta.com/product-manager-skills/) groups skills by area of responsibility. Cabage's grid places them in space, so a region of the chart can be empty in a way a list cannot show.

The five categories run from the external side of the chart to the internal side. Users is the most external: jobs to be done, user interviews, prototyping and user requests. Market follows with positioning, opportunity discovery, competitive gap analysis, product-market fit, conversion optimization and A/B testing. Core Product sits in the middle with vision, roadmap, prioritization, backlog, requirements and UAT testing. Business covers the product council, portfolio planning, business cases, business analysis, internal tools requirements and reports. Technical is the most internal: innovation, technical partnerships, build versus buy, system analysis, model evals and data queries.

Some placements surprise people, and the surprises are worth keeping. Product-Market Fit sits on the tactical side of Market, next to conversion optimization and A/B testing. People management does not appear as a category at all. Read these as Cabage's choices and keep them, because changing them quietly makes it hard to compare your map with his or with other teams using it.

Local definitions do the practical work. A good definition names the output of the box and one example of it from your products: "Build vs Buy: a written recommendation comparing building a capability with licensing it, such as the decision on the search vendor." A definition that only restates the label adds nothing. Two reviewers who read the same definition should be able to agree whether a given piece of work counts.

Relevance marks keep the map honest. Cabage says the framework does not assume every PM should do everything, and Productfolio's [summary](https://productfolio.com/product-team-competencies/) notes that the skills that matter depend on product type and stage. Mark each box as core, occasional or not applicable for your company, so later assessments do not treat an irrelevant box as a gap.

## Step-by-Step Guide

### Step 1: Copy the published grid

Reproduce Cabage's chart exactly as he labels it: the two axes, the five categories in order, and the three strategic and three tactical boxes in each. Keep his labels even where they read oddly, such as "User Cases," and note your interpretation next to them. Starting from the published version keeps your map comparable with others. Do not reorder or merge boxes at this stage.

### Step 2: Write a local definition for every box

For each box, write one sentence naming the output and one example from your own products. Ask a PM to read the definitions and flag any they could not apply to real work. Rewrite those until the PM can say whether a recent piece of work belongs in the box. Leave boxes you do not understand for the review in Step 5 rather than guessing.

### Step 3: Mark relevance for your products

Label each box as core, occasional or not applicable, based on the product types your team owns. Use Cabage's product-type reading as a guide: consumer products lean on the left side, and internal tools and platforms lean on the right. A startup still searching for product-market fit will weight the Users and Market boxes heavily. Record the reason for every not-applicable mark.

### Step 4: Add boxes only for homeless work

List the recurring PM work at your company and check that each item lands in a box. If an item has no home, add a box in the category and half where it belongs and mark it as a local addition. Keep additions rare, because every new box is one more thing to assess. Work that belongs to another function, such as project management, should not be added just because PMs do it today.

### Step 5: Review with PMs and managers

Walk two or three PMs and a manager through the draft and ask where they would place their own last month of work. Disagreement about placement usually means a definition is unclear. Fix the definitions and keep Cabage's placement. End the review when the group can place work without debate.

### Step 6: Publish the map with its reading notes

Publish one page with the grid, the definitions, the relevance marks and two notes: how your company reads height (which levels sit where) and how it reads left and right (which products sit where). Link the page from wherever assessments, job descriptions and interview kits live. Date it so readers know which version they are using.

## Best Practices

- Keep Cabage's axes and categories unchanged. The seniority and product-type readings described on [his page](https://nealcabage.com/framework/product-team-competencies/) depend on the layout, and a rearranged chart loses them.
- Define boxes by their output. "A business case document approved by the portfolio group" is testable, while "strong business thinking" is not.
- Mark boxes your products do not need. Cabage states the framework does not expect every PM to do everything, so an irrelevant box should never count as a gap later.
- Use product type to decide emphasis. Cabage's [account of the role](https://nealcabage.com/product-management/) contrasts B2C discovery with B2B and internal users who tell you what they want, which shifts weight between the left and right of the chart.
- Record local additions separately. Anyone comparing your map with the original should see immediately which boxes are yours.
- Revisit the map when your product mix changes, since a new platform or consumer line changes which boxes are core.

## Common Mistakes

- **Replacing the grid with a generic skills list**: Teams sometimes import a long list from elsewhere and lose the two axes. Keep Cabage's grid as the base and translate other lists into its boxes.
- **Moving boxes to where they feel important**: Moving Product-Market Fit above the band because it sounds strategic makes your map disagree with the source. Keep the placement and explain it in the definition.
- **Definitions that restate the label**: "Backlog Grooming: grooming the backlog" helps nobody decide whether work counts. Name the output and give an example.
- **Treating every box as required**: A map without relevance marks turns every empty box into a gap. Mark what your products need before anyone is assessed.
- **Adding boxes for other functions' work**: Adding project or people management boxes because PMs fill in for those roles makes the map describe the current workaround instead of the PM role.
