# RACE Funnel Mapping: Align Journey Stages to RACE

## Overview

RACE funnel mapping is the step that connects the customer journey you actually have to the four stages of the [RACE Framework](../../methods/race-framework/METHOD.md). Most teams already describe their customers' path in their own words: discovery, research, trial, purchase, onboarding, renewal. Mapping those stages to Reach, Act, Convert and Engage gives each one a RACE objective, a set of KPIs and an owner, and it shows where the RACE digital marketing funnel has no coverage at all.

The RACE stages have clear definitions to map against. Dave Chaffey describes Reach as building awareness and visibility to drive visits, Act (short for Interact) as persuading visitors to take the next step on their journey, Convert as the sale online or offline, and Engage as the long-term relationship that produces repeat purchase and advocacy ([Chaffey: RACE marketing model](https://www.davechaffey.com/digital-marketing-glossary/race-marketing-planning-model/)). Your journey stages rarely line up one to one with these, which is why the mapping is worth doing carefully.

Real journeys also loop. [McKinsey's 2009 article on the consumer decision journey](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/growth-marketing-and-sales/our-insights/the-consumer-decision-journey) described a circular process with four phases, initial consideration, active evaluation, closure and postpurchase, plus a loyalty loop in which a satisfied customer can buy again without restarting the journey. A good RACE mapping keeps those loops visible, for example by showing that a satisfied Engage customer's referral is a Reach touchpoint for someone else.

The output of this skill is an alignment matrix: rows for your journey stages, a column showing the RACE stage each belongs to, and columns for the customer's goal, the touchpoints you run there, the KPI that measures it, and the owner. It is a planning artifact, lighter than a full journey map. The research-based visual map is covered in [RACE framework journey map](../creating-race-customer-journey-maps/SKILL.md), and the matrix produced here is often its starting point.

## How It Works

The mapping works at two levels: stages and touchpoints.

At the stage level, you match each of your journey stages to the RACE stage whose job it performs. A common default is awareness to Reach, consideration to Act, decision to Convert, and post-purchase to Engage. The default breaks in predictable places. A free trial may belong to Act (the prospect is interacting) or to Convert (the trial is the product decision), depending on your model. A long B2B evaluation can span Act and Convert. Where a journey stage spans two RACE stages, split it or record the overlap explicitly, so no KPI is counted twice.

| Your journey stage | Customer goal | RACE stage | Example touchpoints |
|---|---|---|---|
| Discover | Notice the problem and possible solutions | Reach | Search results, social posts, press, referrals |
| Research | Compare options and build confidence | Act | Guides, product pages, webinars, newsletter |
| Decide and buy | Choose and complete the purchase | Convert | Pricing page, checkout, sales call, proposal |
| Use and return | Get value, buy again, recommend | Engage | Onboarding, support, loyalty offers, reviews |

It helps to compare your stages with other intent-based models. Avinash Kaushik's See-Think-Do framework groups audiences by intent (See for the largest relevant audience, Think for those considering, Do for those ready to buy) and later added a cluster for existing customers ([Kaushik: See-Think-Do](https://www.kaushik.net/avinash/see-think-do-content-marketing-measurement-business-framework/)). Asking "what intent does a person have at this point?" is a good tiebreaker when a journey stage could belong to two RACE stages.

At the touchpoint level, you list every place the customer meets you and assign it to a stage. Smart Insights notes that Act interactions can take place over several channels and touchpoints, and that the leads they create need managing separately from final conversion, through techniques like retargeting and assisted selling ([Smart Insights: The RACE Framework](https://www.smartinsights.com/digital-marketing-strategy/race-a-practical-framework-to-improve-your-digital-marketing/)). Touchpoints that serve two stages, such as an email newsletter that nurtures prospects and retains customers, should be listed in both with a note on which audience each version targets.

With stages and touchpoints placed, the gaps show themselves. Look for journey stages with no touchpoints, RACE stages with no KPI, touchpoints nobody owns, and transitions where you have no way to move a customer on, such as a lead that is captured but never followed up. Each gap becomes a line in the RACE planning template.

## Step-by-Step Guide

### Step 1: Write down the journey you actually have

List the stages your customers go through, in their order and in plain language, from first contact to repeat purchase. Use evidence: analytics paths, CRM stages, sales notes and customer interviews. Where journeys differ a lot between segments, map the most important segment first. Note any loops, such as customers who return directly to purchase.

### Step 2: Restate the RACE stage definitions for your business

Write one sentence per RACE stage describing what it means for you, using [Chaffey's definitions](https://www.davechaffey.com/digital-marketing-glossary/race-marketing-planning-model/) as the base. Decide specifically what counts as an interaction in Act and what counts as a conversion in Convert, including offline outcomes. Agree these definitions with sales and customer success. Every later disagreement about the mapping goes back to them.

### Step 3: Build the alignment matrix

Create a table with one row per journey stage and columns for the customer goal, the RACE stage, touchpoints, KPI and owner. Assign each journey stage to a RACE stage. Where a stage spans two, split it into sub-stages or mark the overlap and decide which RACE stage owns the KPI. Keep the matrix to one page.

### Step 4: Inventory touchpoints and place them

List every current touchpoint across marketing, sales and service, online and offline, and add each to the right row. Include owned channels such as your site and email, paid media, and earned media such as reviews and press. Mark touchpoints that serve more than one stage. Note which touchpoints have tracking and which do not.

### Step 5: Find gaps, overlaps and broken transitions

Scan the matrix for empty rows, stages without KPIs, touchpoints with no owner, and handoffs with no mechanism, such as leads from Act that never reach sales. Pull funnel data to see where people drop between stages, for example with a [funnel exploration in Google Analytics](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/9327974). Mark each finding with its likely impact. Do not try to fix everything at once.

### Step 6: Assign owners and actions

Give every RACE stage an owner and every gap an action with a date. Actions can be new touchpoints, better tracking, or a clearer handoff between teams. Add the actions to the RACE planning template so they compete for budget with everything else. Share the matrix with every team that owns a touchpoint.

### Step 7: Revisit the mapping when the journey changes

Review the matrix at each planning cycle and whenever something shifts the journey: a new channel, a pricing change, a new product or a new segment. Check whether new touchpoints have been added without being mapped. Keep dated versions, so you can see how the journey and your coverage changed over time.

## Best Practices

- Map the journey from evidence. Analytics paths and customer interviews show the journey customers take, which is often different from the one the team assumes.
- Define Act and Convert for your business before mapping. Many arguments about where a touchpoint belongs are really arguments about these two definitions, which [Chaffey](https://www.davechaffey.com/digital-marketing-glossary/race-marketing-planning-model/) keeps deliberately separate.
- Keep loops visible. McKinsey's [consumer decision journey](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/growth-marketing-and-sales/our-insights/the-consumer-decision-journey) shows that postpurchase experience feeds the next decision, so connect Engage back to Reach in the matrix.
- Include offline touchpoints. Sales calls, events and stores belong in the stage they serve.
- Give every stage one owner. Shared ownership of a stage usually means no one follows up on its gaps.
- Keep it to one page. A mapping that needs a slide deck to explain it will not be used in planning.

## Common Mistakes

- **Forcing a one-to-one fit**: Journey stages that span two RACE stages get squeezed into one, and their KPIs become misleading. Split the stage or record the overlap.
- **Mapping only digital touchpoints**: Leaving out sales calls, events or stores makes Convert and Engage look emptier than they are. Map every channel that serves a stage.
- **Stopping at the purchase**: Many mappings end at Convert. Map onboarding, support, renewal and referral into Engage.
- **Mapping the ideal journey**: A mapping built from the intended journey hides the real drop-offs. Base it on data and interviews.
- **Never updating it**: Journeys change with every new channel or product. Revisit the mapping at each planning cycle.
