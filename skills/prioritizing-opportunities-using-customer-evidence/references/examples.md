# Examples: Prioritizing Opportunities Using Customer Evidence

## Walking down a tree for a language-learning app

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a trio at a language-learning app owns the outcome "more learners complete a lesson on at least three days a week." Its tree has three top-level opportunities: "I forget to practice," "Lessons feel too easy or too hard," and "I don't see myself making progress."

**Walkthrough:**

The trio compares only these three. Most interview stories mention forgetting to practice, and learners describe it as frequent but mild. Fewer stories mention difficulty, but those learners described quitting because of it. Progress comes up often and connects to the company's strategy of serving learners preparing for exams.

The trio discusses the trade-off and picks "Lessons feel too easy or too hard," because the pain is severe, current workarounds are poor, and a competitor has recently marketed adaptive lessons. It then compares the children of that branch, "The placement test put me in the wrong level" and "Some topics are much harder than others," and picks the placement test as the target. It writes three sentences explaining the choice and moves to solutions.

## Ignoring effort

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a B2B team compares "I can't tell which invoices are overdue" with "I have to reconcile payments from our bank by hand." The engineer argues the bank reconciliation opportunity is too hard.

**Walkthrough:**

The product manager points out that effort is a property of solutions, which the team has not explored yet. The trio assesses both on sizing and customer factors. Reconciliation affects fewer customers, but those customers spend hours on it every month and describe it as the reason they considered switching.

The trio picks reconciliation. During ideation, the engineer suggests a simple import of the bank's standard export file, which avoids the integration work everyone assumed would be needed. The opportunity that looked hardest turned out to have an easy first solution.

## Revisiting a choice

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a team chose "I can't find the setting I need" as its target, then ran assumption tests on three solutions.

**Walkthrough:**

All three solutions tested poorly, and follow-up interviews suggested customers rarely needed the settings at all. The trio treats the original choice as a two-way door. It walks back up to the parent, compares the remaining siblings again with the new evidence, and picks a different target. The rationale document gets a new entry explaining what changed.
