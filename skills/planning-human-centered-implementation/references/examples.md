# Examples: Planning Human-Centered Implementation

## Clinic appointment reminder redesign

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a community health network has tested a text-based reminder concept that patients strongly preferred over phone calls, and a sponsor wants it live across all clinics next month.

**Walkthrough:**

The team frames the decision as a pilot in two clinics for one quarter, with no-show rate and front-desk time as metrics. Desirability passes on the prototype evidence. Feasibility surfaces a problem: the scheduling system cannot send messages without a new integration, and compliance must review consent language. The team brings IT and compliance in early, shows them the prototype, and agrees a manual workaround for the pilot while the integration is scoped.

Viability is assessed against the made-up cost of the messaging service versus staff hours saved. After the pilot, the team checks whether patients without mobile phones were missed before recommending wider rollout.

## Internal expense tool that stalled at feasibility

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a product team designed a simplified expense submission flow that employees rated highly in testing, and finance leadership asked for an implementation plan.

**Walkthrough:**

The team sorts its evidence and finds strong desirability data but nothing on feasibility or viability. Interviews with finance operators reveal that the simplified flow removes fields auditors require. Rather than forcing the concept through, the team sends it back for revision with a new constraint and a How might we question about capturing audit data invisibly. The revised concept is retested with employees and auditors together.

Only then does the plan move to a pilot in one department with approval time and rejection rate as metrics. The team records that skipping the feasibility gate would have produced a rollout finance could not accept.

## Retail returns kiosk pilot that led to a revision

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a retailer pilots a self-service returns kiosk in three stores after it passes all three gates on paper.

**Walkthrough:**

Success metrics agreed beforehand include kiosk completion rate, staff time per return and customer satisfaction, with a written threshold for each decision. The pilot shows strong completion but reveals an unintended consequence: staff are pulled from the floor to handle kiosk exceptions at peak hours. Because the team defined assessment criteria in advance, the result is read honestly as revise rather than scale. The team works with store leaders to redesign exception handling and schedules a second pilot.

The new baseline and the exception-handling problem become the next challenge for the design team.
