## Overview

Selecting six forces analysis tools means deciding, before research starts, where evidence will come from, how each force will be rated and how the result will be shown. The [Six Forces Model](../../methods/six-forces-model/METHOD.md) asks the same questions every time: how intense is rivalry, how high are entry barriers, how strong are substitutes, how much power do buyers and suppliers hold, and how healthy are complements. A toolkit makes the answers comparable across forces, analysts and years.

Without one, each analyst uses their own sources, scale and format. Rivalry gets rated from a paid industry report, complements from a hunch, and one person's "high" means another's "medium." The final chart looks consistent but hides that inconsistency. A shared template and source list fix most of this with little effort.

The toolkit has three parts. The first is a source list per force: where to look for evidence, starting with free public sources. Many of the most useful competitive analysis tools are government services: [EDGAR full text search](https://www.sec.gov/edgar/search-and-access) for company filings, the USPTO's [Patent Public Search](https://www.uspto.gov/patents/search/patent-public-search) for signs of entry, and the [Census Business Builder](https://www.census.gov/data/data-tools/cbb.html) for market and business data. The [SBA's market research guide](https://www.sba.gov/business-guide/plan-your-business/market-research-competitive-analysis) lists further federal sources for demographics, economic indicators and employment.

The second part is the six forces scoring template: one row per factor, with fields for the rating, the evidence, the source, the date and the confidence. The factor rows come from the standard lists in the [five forces article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porter%27s_five_forces_analysis), plus rows for the complementary products force. The third part is a visual format for presenting the result, usually a one-page summary that shows each force's rating and direction.

The skill is deliberately lean. Paid databases and specialist software can help, but the value comes from consistency, and a spreadsheet with a good template beats an expensive platform used differently by every analyst.

## How It Works

Tool selection starts from the questions. For each force, list what the rating depends on and what kind of evidence would answer it: market shares for rivalry, patent filings and funding for entrants, customer interviews for substitutes and switching costs, filings and procurement data for buyer and supplier power, integration and partner data for complements. Then map each evidence type to a source.

Public sources come first because they are free and checkable. Company filings describe markets and risks; the SEC's [guide to the 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/answers/reada10k.htm) explains that Item 1 covers products, services and markets and Item 1A the most significant risks. Search interest data can help spot a rising substitute, but [Google Trends](https://support.google.com/trends/answer/4365533) scales values from 0 to 100 relative to all searches in that place and time, so the template should label it as relative interest. Paid sources fill the gaps that public ones leave, such as private-company revenue or detailed market shares. Interview notes and surveys are tools too, and the template needs a place for them.

The scoring template standardizes rating. Each factor has a written definition of low, medium and high. Each rating cites its evidence and source, with a date and a confidence grade. Forces are rated separately and never averaged; a summary row states the overall rating for each force with a one-line reason. For concentration, include a calculation using the [Herfindahl-Hirschman Index](https://www.justice.gov/atr/herfindahl-hirschman-index), which squares each firm's market share and sums the results.

The visual format presents the result. A common choice is a one-page diagram or table with the six forces around the industry, each showing its rating, direction and the main driver. Keep the format fixed across analyses so readers learn to read it, and link each element back to the template row that supports it.

Finally, test the toolkit on one force before rolling it out. Rating a single force end to end shows which sources are missing, which definitions are unclear and how long each step takes.

## Step-by-Step Guide

### Step 1: List the evidence each force needs

For each of the six forces, write the questions the rating depends on and the kind of evidence that would answer each one. Use the standard factor lists as a checklist, and add complement questions such as which integrations customers depend on. The result is a short table of force, question and evidence type. Every later choice should trace back to it.

### Step 2: Inventory existing tools and access

List the sources and tools the team already has: subscriptions, internal data, CRM fields, past research, interview archives. Map each one to the evidence types from step 1. Mark evidence types with no source. This step often shows that the team already has most of what it needs, apart from customer and partner evidence for substitutes and complements.

### Step 3: Choose sources to fill the gaps

For each gap, pick a source, starting with free public ones: filings, patent search, government statistics, search trend data, trade association material. Add a paid source only where a public one cannot answer the question, and write down which question it answers. Plan primary research, such as interviews or short surveys, for questions no database covers.

### Step 4: Build the six forces scoring template

Create one template, usually a spreadsheet, with a section per force and a row per factor. Give each row fields for the rating, the evidence, the source link, the date and a confidence grade. Write the definitions of each rating level in the template itself. Add a summary row per force and a separate field for its direction over the analysis horizon.

### Step 5: Choose the visual format

Design a one-page summary that shows all six forces with their ratings, directions and main drivers. Keep it readable in a few seconds and consistent across analyses. Link each element to the template rows behind it so readers can check the evidence. Avoid formats that average the forces into a single score.

### Step 6: Set a source citation rule

Agree on how every piece of evidence is cited: link or document reference, date accessed and the page or section. Require a citation for every rating, and a label when a value is an estimate or opinion. This rule makes the analysis checkable and makes the next update faster.

### Step 7: Test on one force and refine

Run one force, such as rivalry, through the full toolkit. Note missing sources, unclear rating definitions and fields nobody used. Fix the template and source list, then document the toolkit on a single page: sources per force, the template, the visual format and the citation rule. Share it with everyone who will contribute.

## Best Practices

- Choose tools from questions. A source that answers no rating question adds cost without adding evidence.
- Use public sources first. Filings, patent records and government statistics are free and checkable, and the [SBA guide](https://www.sba.gov/business-guide/plan-your-business/market-research-competitive-analysis) points to several federal sources.
- Put rating definitions in the template. Written definitions of low, medium and high are the main thing that makes ratings comparable across analysts.
- Keep forces separate in the template. A combined score hides the force that dominates.
- Record dates and confidence. Evidence ages, and the template should show how old and how reliable each item is.
- Keep the toolkit small. A short source list and one template that everyone uses produce better results than many tools used inconsistently.

## Common Mistakes

- **Buying tools before defining questions**: A subscription is chosen because it is popular, then turns out to cover only one force. List the questions first.
- **No home for primary research**: The template has fields for data but not for interview evidence, so substitutes and complements end up rated from opinion. Add rows for customer and partner evidence.
- **Undefined rating levels**: Analysts use the same scale with different meanings. Write the definitions into the template.
- **Treating relative data as absolute**: Search trend indexes get read as search volume. Label them as relative interest.
- **Visuals without evidence links**: The one-page chart is shared without the template behind it. Link each rating to its evidence so the chart can be checked.
