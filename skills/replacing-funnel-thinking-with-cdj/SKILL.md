# Replacing Funnel Thinking with the Decision Journey

> Replace funnel thinking with the Consumer Decision Journey: remap touchpoints, move budget, change metrics and reassign ownership by CDJ phase.

## Overview

Most marketing teams still plan around the funnel: awareness at the top, consideration in the middle, conversion at the bottom, with a budget line for each. The [McKinsey Consumer Decision Journey](https://tryhamster.com/methods/mckinsey-consumer-decision-journey) replaces that model with a loop of four phases in which buyers add brands during evaluation and loyal customers skip straight back to buying. Replacing funnel thinking means changing the plans, budgets, metrics and ownership that were built on the old model, not only the diagram.

[The original McKinsey article](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/growth-marketing-and-sales/our-insights/the-consumer-decision-journey) described the cost of not changing. Marketers who kept spending by funnel stage risked wasting money, because their messages would not reach buyers at the right time, and appearing out of touch, because they would be pushing products on buyers who wanted information and support instead. The article's authors argued that most marketers had focused on either end of the funnel, building awareness or generating loyalty among current customers, and needed to be much more specific about the touchpoints that influence buyers in each phase.

The change is organizational as much as analytical. Funnel stages map neatly onto separate teams: brand owns awareness, performance owns conversion, and customer success owns retention. The CDJ cuts across those lines, because the postpurchase experience feeds the next consideration set and evaluation touchpoints are spread across marketing, sales and third parties. A transition that changes the slides but not the budget or the owners changes nothing a buyer will notice.

This skill is a practical sequence for making the switch: audit what exists, gather evidence that buyers do not behave like the funnel, remap touchpoints, find the biggest gaps, move budget and metrics, and assign owners. It works best as a staged change, starting with the phase where the evidence is strongest.

## How It Works

The funnel and the CDJ disagree on three points, and each one implies a change in how a team works.

The first is the shape of evaluation. The funnel assumes buyers only narrow their options. The CDJ found that buyers often add brands while researching, as [McKinsey's data](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/growth-marketing-and-sales/our-insights/the-consumer-decision-journey) showed for car shoppers, who added an average of 2.2 brands to an initial set of 3.8. A plan built on the funnel invests little in evaluation touchpoints because it assumes the list is fixed by then. A CDJ plan treats evaluation as a phase where brands can still get in.

The second is who drives influence. Funnel plans are mostly lists of company-driven activity: ads, emails, sponsorships. The CDJ found that most evaluation touchpoints are consumer-driven, such as reviews, word of mouth and past experience. Moving to the CDJ therefore means shifting some effort from buying media to earning presence, which the McKinsey authors described as developing properties that attract consumers.

The third is what happens after the sale. The funnel ends at conversion and treats retention as a separate topic. The CDJ makes postpurchase a phase of the same journey, with a loyalty loop for active loyalists and a return to evaluation for everyone else. In a CDJ plan, onboarding and support belong to the same strategy as acquisition, and the share of active loyalists is a marketing metric.

Budget follows the diagnosis. The McKinsey article gives the example of skin care brands that were strong in initial consideration but weak in evaluation and closure. Those brands needed to shift focus from overall positioning to efforts at the moment of purchase, such as packaging and in-store activity. Another brand might need the opposite. The transition should fund the phase where your brand loses, and that phase differs by brand and category.

Ownership is the last piece. Edelman and Singer, in their [2015 update](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/growth-marketing-and-sales/our-insights/the-new-consumer-decision-journey), argue that journeys should be managed like products, actively measured and improved. In [Competing on Customer Journeys](https://hbr.org/2015/11/competing-on-customer-journeys) they propose a journey product manager accountable for a journey's business results. A team does not need that exact title, but it needs someone responsible for the whole loop, not just one stage of it.

## Step-by-Step Guide

### Step 1: Audit the Current Plan

List every activity, budget line, metric and owner in the current marketing plan, and note which funnel stage each one serves. Include sales, customer success and support activities that touch buyers. Total the budget and effort by stage. The audit usually shows heavy spending at the top and bottom of the funnel and little in the middle or after the sale.

### Step 2: Gather Evidence of Non-Linear Behavior

Collect evidence of how your buyers actually decide: consideration set surveys, interviews about their research, data on brands added during evaluation, and the share of repeat buyers who did not re-evaluate. Look for the behaviors the funnel cannot explain, such as brands added late or loyal customers skipping research. This evidence is what persuades stakeholders, so use your own data wherever you can instead of relying on general research.

### Step 3: Remap Activities to CDJ Phases

Assign each activity from the audit to the CDJ phase where it influences buyers: initial consideration, active evaluation, moment of purchase or postpurchase. Tag each one as company-driven or consumer-driven. Add the consumer-driven touchpoints buyers mention that the plan does not cover. The remapped plan shows where the team is present and where it is absent.

### Step 4: Find the Biggest Gaps

Compare the remapped plan with the evidence. Identify the phase where your brand loses the most buyers relative to competitors, and check how much budget and effort that phase receives. Look for influential consumer-driven touchpoints with no activity at all. Rank the gaps by the number of buyers affected and the effort needed to close them.

### Step 5: Move Budget in Stages

Shift part of the budget from phases where you already win to the phase where you lose most, starting with a change large enough to measure and small enough to reverse. Tie each move to a hypothesis and a metric. Tailor messages to the weak phase, as the [McKinsey article](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/growth-marketing-and-sales/our-insights/the-consumer-decision-journey) describes Hyundai doing when it built a campaign to get into more initial consideration sets. Review results before the next shift.

### Step 6: Replace Funnel Metrics with Phase Metrics

Set one or two metrics per phase: inclusion in the initial consideration set, the share of evaluators who add or keep your brand, completion at the moment of purchase, and the share of customers who are active loyalists. Keep the old funnel metrics visible during the transition so results can be compared. Report the phase metrics together so the team sees the whole loop.

### Step 7: Assign Ownership of the Loop

Name one person accountable for the whole journey and owners for each phase and major touchpoint, including ones that sit outside marketing. Rewrite briefs so each one names the phase it targets and the buyer behavior it is meant to change. Hold regular reviews where phase owners look at the loop together. Adjust the plan as the evidence changes.

## Best Practices

- Start with the phase where the evidence is strongest. A focused change in one phase, backed by your own buyer data, builds more support than a full reorganization announced at once.
- Move budget in stages with a hypothesis for each move. Staged shifts let you learn what works in your category and reverse a mistake before it becomes expensive.
- Include postpurchase in the marketing plan. Onboarding, support and advocacy shape the next consideration set, so leaving them to another department breaks the loop at its most valuable point.
- Keep the old metrics visible during the transition. Stakeholders trust new metrics more when they can see them next to the familiar ones, and the comparison helps explain what changed.
- Write briefs by phase and by buyer behavior. A brief that states which phase it targets and what buyers should do differently is easier to evaluate than one aimed at a funnel stage.

## Common Mistakes

- **Changing the diagram and nothing else**: Replacing the funnel slide with a loop while budgets, metrics and owners stay the same does not change what buyers experience. Tie the new model to budget and ownership from the start.
- **Moving the whole budget at once**: A large, sudden reallocation risks results before the team has learned what works. Shift in stages and measure each step.
- **Copying another company's allocation**: The right split depends on where your brand loses buyers, which differs by brand and category. Base the allocation on your own diagnosis.
- **Leaving postpurchase to another team**: If customer success and support are outside the plan, the loyalty loop has no owner in the strategy. Bring them into planning and metrics.
- **Relying on last-click attribution**: Last-click models credit the final touchpoint and hide the evaluation and consideration touchpoints the CDJ highlights. Use buyer research and phase metrics alongside attribution data.
