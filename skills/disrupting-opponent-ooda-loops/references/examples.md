# Examples: Disrupting an Opponent's Decision Cycle

## Outpacing an incumbent's planning cycle

**Scenario:**

A small analytics startup competes with a large incumbent that announces product changes only at an annual customer conference.

**Walkthrough:**

Illustrative scenario: the startup's team builds a timeline of the incumbent's past responses and sees that product direction changes only appear at the yearly event. They profile the incumbent's orientation and find it treats enterprise buyers as its only serious segment. The startup ships a series of small features aimed at mid-sized teams shortly after the conference, each one quiet and incremental. By the next conference the incumbent's roadmap still reflects the old picture, and its response targets the startup's first feature rather than the segment position it has since built.

The team records that response as confirmation that the incumbent's Decide phase is gated by the event, and plans its next sequence accordingly.

## Breaking a predictable pricing rhythm

**Scenario:**

A regional software reseller notices a rival always matches its promotions within days, which erases any advantage.

**Walkthrough:**

Illustrative scenario: the reseller's model shows the rival watches its public pricing page closely and reacts through a fast, pre-approved discount process. Price is where the rival's loop is fastest, so competing there only exposes the reseller's pattern. The team shifts to irregular, less visible moves: bundled onboarding for specific customer types and service guarantees negotiated in individual deals. The rival's orientation still treats price as the battleground, so it keeps cutting list prices while losing deals on service.

The reseller watches win and loss notes to confirm the rival has not yet reoriented, and prepares to vary its approach again once it does.

## Checking whether disruption actually happened

**Scenario:**

A product team believes it has out-cycled a competitor after a surprise launch, but leadership wants evidence before committing more budget.

**Walkthrough:**

Illustrative scenario: before the launch, the team had written down its prediction that the competitor would respond late and with a copy of its previous feature. After the launch, the competitor responds quickly with a well-targeted counter aimed at the new capability. The team compares the outcome to its prediction and concludes its estimate of the competitor's cycle was too slow, likely because a newly formed team inside the rival had shorter approval paths. They update the opponent model to include that sub-loop.

Instead of doubling down on the same play, they redirect effort toward a segment the new team does not cover.
