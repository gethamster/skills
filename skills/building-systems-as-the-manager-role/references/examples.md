# Examples: Building Business Systems as the E-Myth Manager Role

## Client onboarding at a small accounting firm

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a two-partner accounting firm onboards new clients differently depending on which partner signed them. Clients often send the wrong documents, and the partners spend evenings chasing paperwork.

**Walkthrough:**

One partner onboards the next new client while writing down every action and decision: which engagement letter to use, which documents to request for which client type, and when to chase. She turns the notes into a two-page system with a trigger (signed proposal), steps, a standard (all documents received and filed before the first working session), a list of exceptions and an owner.

The firm's administrator runs the next onboarding using only the document. She asks four questions, including what to do when a client has no prior-year return. Each question becomes an edit. By the third run she needs no help, and the partners start tracking how often a first working session is delayed by missing documents.

## A café standardizes opening and closing

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a café owner opens and closes the shop himself most days because staff who try it forget steps, such as starting the grinder calibration or locking the back door.

**Walkthrough:**

He treats opening and closing as two soft systems. For each, he writes a one-page checklist grouped by area (bar, kitchen, front, back office), with a short standard for each group, such as "grinder dialed in and first shot tasted". He adds an exceptions section covering a failed delivery and a broken card reader.

Two staff members each run both checklists for a week and mark every unclear line. After one revision, he hands opening to one and closing to the other, names himself as owner of the documents, and reviews them each quarter. His quantification is simple: the number of mornings the café opens late.

## Proposal writing at a design studio

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a design studio founder writes every proposal because she is the only one who knows how to price. Proposals take her a full evening each, and the studio sends them late.

**Walkthrough:**

She realizes pricing is the judgment-heavy part and the rest is assembly. She builds a template for the assembly (scope sections, timelines, terms) and writes a pricing guide that covers the common project types, the questions that change the price, and when a proposal must come to her for approval.

A designer uses the template and guide for the next proposal while the founder reviews the draft. The review turns up two gaps in the pricing guide, which she fills. After that, proposals for the common project types go out without her, and only unusual projects come back to her for pricing.
