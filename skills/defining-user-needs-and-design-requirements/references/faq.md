# FAQ: Defining User Needs and Design Requirements

## What is the difference between a user need and a design requirement?

A need describes what a person is trying to achieve and what stops them, independent of any solution. A requirement is a testable condition that a specific solution must meet to satisfy that need. Needs stay stable across many possible designs, while requirements get more concrete as the design takes shape. Keep them linked so that a change in one shows you what to revisit in the other.

## How many How might we questions should we write?

There is no fixed number in the sources; aim for enough variety that ideation has different angles to work from. For example, three to five questions per opportunity area, each emphasizing a different part of the need, is a workable starting point. Quality matters more than count: each question should invite many answers and trace back to an insight. Drop questions that everyone answers the same way.

## What should we do when research contradicts the original brief?

Reframe the problem anyway and bring the evidence to the sponsor rather than softening the finding. Show the original brief, the reframed statement and the specific observations that moved it. Sponsors usually accept a reframe that is traceable to users, while an unexplained shift looks like scope creep. If the brief has to stand for business reasons, record the unmet need so it is not lost.

## How do we know our needs are valid if we only talked to a small group?

Treat recurring patterns across participants as stronger than any single voice, and mark single-source findings as hypotheses. Look for agreement between what people said and what you observed, and check against internal evidence such as support logs. Then let evaluation confirm or overturn the needs, since the process is meant to be iterative. If a core need rests on thin evidence, schedule targeted research before building heavily on it.

## Who should take part in defining needs and requirements?

Researchers lead, but the session works best with people who will design, build, operate and support the solution. The standard behind human-centered design calls for multidisciplinary skills and perspectives, and each discipline catches different gaps. Engineers spot untestable requirements, operations staff spot undeliverable ones, and support leads know where users actually get stuck. Keep the group small enough to work through the evidence together.
