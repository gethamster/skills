# Examples: Defining User Needs and Design Requirements

## Expense reimbursement tool

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: an internal tools team receives a brief to 'add receipt scanning' to the expense system after research with field staff.

**Walkthrough:**

The team fixes the unit of analysis as field staff from incurring a cost to being reimbursed. Insights show people photograph receipts fine but lose track of claims after submitting, and several described chasing finance by email. The unmet need becomes 'needs to know when money will arrive and what could delay it.' The problem is reframed from receipt capture to confidence about reimbursement status.

The chosen opportunity area is visibility after submission, with the question 'How might we tell a claimant about a problem before they have to ask?' One requirement reads: a claimant whose claim is blocked learns the reason and the fix without contacting finance, tested by giving users a blocked claim and watching what they do.

## Clinic appointment reminders

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a community clinic wants to reduce missed appointments and assumes patients simply forget.

**Walkthrough:**

Observation at the front desk and home interviews show that many patients remember perfectly well but cannot rearrange childcare or transport in time. The team writes the need as 'needs enough notice and a low-effort way to move an appointment that no longer works.' The reframed problem shifts from reminding patients to making rescheduling easy. Opportunity areas include early change windows and rescheduling without a phone queue.

A design principle emerges: 'make changing plans as easy as keeping them.' Requirements cover the phone line and the paper appointment card as well as text messages, because the whole experience includes channels patients actually use.

## Warehouse software onboarding

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a B2B software company's brief asks for a better onboarding tutorial because new warehouse customers churn early.

**Walkthrough:**

The team separates two units of analysis, site managers configuring the system and floor staff using it daily, and runs definition for each. For floor staff, observation reveals they never see the tutorial; a supervisor shows them one screen and they improvise from there. The need becomes 'needs to learn the next task at the moment it comes up, without leaving the floor.' The reframed problem is about learning in the flow of work, not a better tutorial.

The How might we questions stay open, for example 'How might we let a new picker recover from a mistake without calling a supervisor?' Requirements carry insight IDs, and two unsupported ones from the sales team are marked as assumptions to test.
