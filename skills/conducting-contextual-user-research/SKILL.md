# Conducting human-centered design user research in context

> Plan and run in-context interviews and observation so a design team sees what people actually do, not only what they say.

## At a Glance

| Field | Value |
|-------|-------|
| Difficulty | Intermediate |
| Time to Learn | One to three weeks for a focused study, including planning and field visits |
| Outcome | A documented set of in-context observations and stories, tied to a clear scope, that is ready to be synthesized into insights and unmet needs. |
| Prerequisites | A defined design challenge or business decision the research must inform, Access to the people and settings where the activity happens, Basic interviewing and note-taking skills, Agreement from stakeholders on scope and consent handling |
| Part of | [Human-Centered Design \(HCD\)](../../methods/human-centered-design-hcd/METHOD.md) |

## Overview

Contextual user research is the fieldwork that opens a human-centered design project. You go to the places where people actually do the activity you are designing for, watch what they do, and hear how they explain it. [IDEO's Human-Centered Design Toolkit](https://cdn2.hubspot.net/hubfs/316071/Resources/Article/IDEO%20Human%20Centered%20Design%20Toolkit%202nd%20Ed.pdf) frames this work as part of the Inspiration phase, where the question is what people really need, answered through interviews, observation, immersion, shadowing, analogous research and desk research. For background on the method itself and how its phases fit together, see the [Human-Centered Design method page](https://tryhamster.com/methods/human-centered-design-hcd).

The reason to work in context, rather than only through surveys or conference-room interviews, is that people are unreliable narrators of their own routines. They skip the workarounds they no longer notice, the sticky note on the monitor, the colleague they always call before submitting a form. The same toolkit notes that [immersing yourself in their context reveals new insights and unexpected opportunities](https://cdn2.hubspot.net/hubfs/316071/Resources/Article/IDEO%20Human%20Centered%20Design%20Toolkit%202nd%20Ed.pdf). Seeing the environment lets you ask about things the participant would never think to mention.

The skill comes down to a handful of decisions, which a [pattern analysis of IDEO's HCD methods](https://ideal.umd.edu/assets/pdfs/2015_fuge_jmd_hcd_methods.pdf) summarizes as determining who to talk to, how to gather stories, and how to document your observations. In practice that means:

- scoping the research around a real decision and a defined unit of analysis
- building a plan that mixes direct fieldwork with existing evidence
- choosing participants deliberately
- running interviews and immersion in the natural setting, with a small team and clear consent
- recording what you see in a form others can use

The output is not a slide of quotes. It is a body of observations, stories and artifacts, each tagged to a participant and setting, that a team can later cluster into patterns and insight statements. Interpretation belongs to [synthesizing qualitative research into insights](https://tryhamster.com/skills/synthesizing-qualitative-research-into-insights). Fieldwork's job is to capture raw material faithfully enough that synthesis has something real to work with.

You can tell the research went wrong when the findings could have been written before the visits, when every observation is a complaint and none describe what already works, or when nobody can say which decision the study was meant to inform. Each of those failures traces back to a skipped planning choice, which is why most of this page is about preparation and recording rather than the conversation itself.

## How It Works

Contextual research works by combining several ways of seeing the same activity, then anchoring all of them to one scope. A [human-centered design toolkit from Umbrex](https://umbrex.com/resources/frameworks/design-thinking-frameworks/human-centered-design-toolkit) recommends a research plan that combines interviews, observation, shadowing, diary inputs, call-center themes, complaint data, sales feedback and operational facts. Direct fieldwork shows you behavior and context; the existing data tells you how common a problem is and where to look.

The anchor is the unit of analysis. Before any visit, the [Umbrex write-up of the IDEO process](https://umbrex.com/resources/frameworks/design-thinking-frameworks/ideo-human-centered-design-process) advises defining whether you are studying a customer segment, a use case, a service moment, a workflow or an end-to-end journey. That choice decides who you recruit, where you go and what counts as in scope when a participant drifts.

Each method reveals something different, so pick them for the question rather than habit:

| Method | What it reveals | When to use it |
|---|---|---|
| In-context interview | Motives, reasoning, stories behind behavior | You need the why behind a routine |
| Observation | What people actually do, including workarounds | Stated and actual behavior may differ |
| Shadowing | How a task flows across time, tools and people | The activity spans a shift or several handoffs |
| Immersion | What the experience feels like firsthand | The team lacks any felt sense of the context |
| Diary inputs | Patterns over days or weeks | Behavior is intermittent or private |
| Desk research | Existing data, prior studies, scale of issues | Before fieldwork, to target visits |

In the field, the setup matters as much as the questions. A [guide to HCD for sustainable trade](https://idhsustainabletrade.com/uploaded/2019/08/HCD-long-version-YIDH.pdf) recommends meeting interviewees in their natural context and keeping to a maximum of three researchers, one leading the conversation and the others mainly taking notes, because more people might intimidate. The same guide advises telling interviewees they do not need to answer questions they feel uncomfortable about, which protects participants and tends to produce more candid answers on the questions they do take.

What you record decides what synthesis can find. The [CCDF Human-Centered Design Toolkit](https://childcareta.acf.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/new-occ/resource/files/CCDF_Human_Centered_Design_Toolkit_0.pdf) asks observers to note frustrations, successful interactions and missed opportunities, not frustrations alone. It also recommends inviting a broad range of staff who may implement the eventual solution to join observation when possible, so the people who will deliver the change have seen the problem themselves.

Finally, the team reviews notes while the visits are fresh and flags what is recurring. The [Umbrex toolkit](https://umbrex.com/resources/frameworks/design-thinking-frameworks/human-centered-design-toolkit) points researchers toward recurring needs, tensions, barriers, contradictions and moments that matter disproportionately. At this stage you are marking candidates, not writing conclusions; the formal clustering happens afterward.

## Step-by-Step Guide

### Step 1: Clarify the decision and scope

Start by writing down the business or design question the research must inform, because fieldwork without a decision produces interesting stories that change nothing. The [Umbrex summary of the IDEO process](https://umbrex.com/resources/frameworks/design-thinking-frameworks/ideo-human-centered-design-process) advises defining the real question before research starts. Then choose the unit of analysis: a segment, a use case, a service moment, a workflow or an end-to-end journey. Write both on one page and get the sponsor to agree.

If the sponsor cannot name what they will do differently based on the findings, the scope is not ready.

> **Pro tip:** Phrase the decision as a choice the team will make, for example whether to redesign intake or the follow-up call, so fieldwork has a clear target.

### Step 2: Build a mixed research plan

List the sources you will combine: direct fieldwork plus existing evidence such as complaint data, call-center themes, sales feedback and operational facts, as the [Umbrex HCD toolkit](https://umbrex.com/resources/frameworks/design-thinking-frameworks/human-centered-design-toolkit) suggests. Review the existing data first, since it shows where problems cluster and which settings are worth visiting. Match each open question to a method using the table above. Decide on locations, visit length and who attends.

The output is a short plan naming questions, methods, sites and schedule.

### Step 3: Choose who to talk to

Deciding who to talk to is one of the core fieldwork choices named in the [pattern analysis of IDEO methods](https://ideal.umd.edu/assets/pdfs/2015_fuge_jmd_hcd_methods.pdf). Recruit against the unit of analysis, not whoever is easiest to reach. Include mainstream users alongside extremes, such as heavy users, people who abandoned the service, or people with unusual constraints, since extremes make hidden needs visible. Add people who serve users, like frontline staff, when the activity involves them.

Record why each participant was chosen so synthesis can weigh their input correctly.

> **Pro tip:** Keep a simple recruitment grid of the traits that matter and fill it as you book sessions, so gaps show before the last visit rather than after.

### Step 4: Set up the field team and consent

Assign roles before each visit: one lead who runs the conversation and one or two note takers. The [sustainable trade HCD guide](https://idhsustainabletrade.com/uploaded/2019/08/HCD-long-version-YIDH.pdf) recommends no more than three researchers so the participant is not intimidated. Prepare a plain-language consent explanation covering what you are recording, how it will be used, and that participants do not need to answer questions that make them uncomfortable. Where the solution will be delivered by staff, invite some of them to observe, as the [CCDF toolkit](https://childcareta.acf.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/new-occ/resource/files/CCDF_Human_Centered_Design_Toolkit_0.pdf) recommends.

> **Pro tip:** Brief observers from the implementing team to stay silent during sessions and save reactions for the debrief, so the participant is not steered.

### Step 5: Run in-context interviews and immersion

Meet people where the activity naturally happens, which the [sustainable trade guide](https://idhsustainabletrade.com/uploaded/2019/08/HCD-long-version-YIDH.pdf) recommends whenever possible. Ask them to show rather than tell: walk through the last time they did the task, using their real tools and documents. Follow up on what you see in the environment, such as notes, printouts or workarounds. Where a firsthand sense of the experience is missing, have team members try the activity themselves, since [IDEO's toolkit](https://cdn2.hubspot.net/hubfs/316071/Resources/Article/IDEO%20Human%20Centered%20Design%20Toolkit%202nd%20Ed.pdf) credits immersion with revealing unexpected opportunities.

The output of each session is a set of stories and observations, not answers to a questionnaire.

> **Pro tip:** When a participant generalizes with a phrase like usually or always, ask for the most recent specific instance instead.

### Step 6: Record observations the same day

Write up each session before the next one, while details are fresh. Use a consistent template so notes from different researchers can be compared: participant, setting, what they did, what they said, artifacts seen. Following the [CCDF toolkit](https://childcareta.acf.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/new-occ/resource/files/CCDF_Human_Centered_Design_Toolkit_0.pdf), capture frustrations, successful interactions and missed opportunities in separate fields. Keep direct quotes marked as quotes and your interpretations marked as interpretations.

> **Pro tip:** Photograph artifacts and settings, with permission, and file them against the session record so synthesis can see the context, not just read about it.

### Step 7: Debrief and flag candidate patterns

Hold a short team debrief after each day of fieldwork. Compare what each person saw and note where accounts differ, since those differences often point to things worth probing in later sessions. Flag recurring needs, tensions, barriers, contradictions and moments that matter disproportionately, the signals the [Umbrex toolkit](https://umbrex.com/resources/frameworks/design-thinking-frameworks/human-centered-design-toolkit) tells researchers to look for. Adjust remaining sessions to test emerging hunches.

When new visits stop producing new observations about the scoped question, hand the material to synthesis.

## Best Practices

- Tie every study to a named decision. The [IDEO process summary](https://umbrex.com/resources/frameworks/design-thinking-frameworks/ideo-human-centered-design-process) puts clarifying the real question first because it tells you what to observe and what to ignore in the field.
- Pair fieldwork with existing data. Complaint logs, call-center themes and operational facts, which the [Umbrex toolkit](https://umbrex.com/resources/frameworks/design-thinking-frameworks/human-centered-design-toolkit) includes in a research plan, show how widespread a problem is, while visits show why it happens.
- Keep the field team small with fixed roles. A lead plus note takers, capped at three per the [sustainable trade guide](https://idhsustainabletrade.com/uploaded/2019/08/HCD-long-version-YIDH.pdf), keeps the session conversational and ensures someone is always capturing detail.
- Record what works, not just what fails. Noting successful interactions alongside frustrations, as the [CCDF toolkit](https://childcareta.acf.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/new-occ/resource/files/CCDF_Human_Centered_Design_Toolkit_0.pdf) advises, stops the team from redesigning away behaviors people rely on.
- Bring implementers into observation. Staff who will deliver the solution understand the problem faster from one visit than from a findings deck, and they later defend the design with firsthand evidence.
- Separate observation from interpretation in notes. When quotes, behaviors and hunches share one undifferentiated paragraph, synthesis cannot tell what was seen from what was assumed.

## Common Mistakes

- **Starting fieldwork without a defined unit of analysis, so sessions wander across every part of the user's life.** — Pick a segment, use case, service moment, workflow or journey before recruiting, as the [IDEO process summary](https://umbrex.com/resources/frameworks/design-thinking-frameworks/ideo-human-centered-design-process) advises, and redirect sessions that drift outside it.
- **Sending a large delegation of stakeholders to every interview.** — Limit each visit to a lead and one or two note takers, since the [sustainable trade guide](https://idhsustainabletrade.com/uploaded/2019/08/HCD-long-version-YIDH.pdf) warns that more than three people might intimidate. Rotate stakeholders across sessions instead.
- **Interviewing people in an office about work they do somewhere else.** — Go to the natural setting, where tools, interruptions and workarounds are visible. If access is impossible, ask participants to bring or photograph their real materials and walk through a recent instance.
- **Logging only pain points.** — Add fields for successful interactions and missed opportunities, following the [CCDF toolkit](https://childcareta.acf.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/new-occ/resource/files/CCDF_Human_Centered_Design_Toolkit_0.pdf). A complaint-only record hides what the design must preserve.
- **Treating consent as a form to sign rather than a conversation.** — Explain in plain language what is recorded and why, and state that participants can skip any question. Signs it went wrong include guarded, one-word answers and participants glancing at the recorder.
