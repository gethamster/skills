# Examples: Conducting Contextual User Research

## Scoping research for a benefits application

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a public agency wants to reduce abandoned online applications for a support program and asks a small team to run field research.

**Walkthrough:**

The team first writes the decision: whether to redesign the eligibility questions or the document upload step. They set the unit of analysis as the end-to-end application journey for first-time applicants. Desk research on help-line themes shows most calls mention documents, so they target visits to community centers where people apply with caseworker help. They recruit a mix of successful applicants, people who abandoned midway, and two caseworkers.

The resulting plan fits on one page and the sponsor signs off before any visit.

## Shadowing a hospital ward handoff

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a product team designing a shift-handoff tool for nurses has only interviewed nurse managers so far.

**Walkthrough:**

Two researchers shadow a nurse through the end of one shift and the start of the next, with one leading brief questions and the other taking notes. They observe that critical details travel on handwritten notes tucked into pockets, which no manager had mentioned. They record a successful interaction too: nurses verbally confirm allergies at the bedside, a habit the tool must not replace. A ward clerk from the implementation team joins as a silent observer.

That evening the team writes up notes in a shared template, separating quotes, behaviors and interpretations.

## Adding diaries to intermittent behavior

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a team researching how small shop owners reorder stock finds interviews too thin because reordering happens irregularly.

**Walkthrough:**

The team adds a short diary study in which each owner logs every reorder over a few weeks with a photo of what prompted it. Researchers then visit selected shops to walk through specific diary entries in context. The visits reveal that reorders are often triggered by a supplier's sales rep dropping by, not by low stock alerts. Notes capture both the frustration of surprise shortages and the success of trusted rep relationships.

The team flags the rep relationship as a recurring pattern for synthesis.
