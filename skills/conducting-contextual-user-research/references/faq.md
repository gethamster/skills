# FAQ: Conducting Contextual User Research

## How is contextual research different from a regular user interview?

A regular interview often happens in a neutral room and relies on what people remember and choose to say. Contextual research happens where the activity occurs, so you can see tools, environments and workarounds and ask about them directly. The [sustainable trade HCD guide](https://idhsustainabletrade.com/uploaded/2019/08/HCD-long-version-YIDH.pdf) recommends meeting interviewees in their natural context for this reason. You still interview, but the setting prompts specific, grounded answers.

## How many people should be on a field research team?

Keep each visit small. The [sustainable trade guide](https://idhsustainabletrade.com/uploaded/2019/08/HCD-long-version-YIDH.pdf) suggests a maximum of three researchers, one leading the conversation and the others mainly taking notes, since more people might intimidate. If more stakeholders want exposure, rotate them across sessions rather than adding them to one.

## Should I use existing data or only fresh fieldwork?

Use both. The [Umbrex HCD toolkit](https://umbrex.com/resources/frameworks/design-thinking-frameworks/human-centered-design-toolkit) describes research plans that combine interviews, observation and shadowing with complaint data, call-center themes, sales feedback and operational facts. Existing data is best reviewed first, to decide where visits will be most informative. Fieldwork then explains the patterns the data only counts.

## When do I stop doing field visits?

Stop when new sessions within your scoped unit of analysis stop producing new observations, and when every participant type in your recruitment plan is covered. Daily debriefs make this visible because the list of new candidate patterns shrinks. If visits keep surfacing surprises, the scope may be too broad and worth narrowing before continuing.

## What should the handoff to synthesis look like?

Hand over session records in a consistent template, each tagged with participant, setting and why they were recruited. Include quotes, observed behaviors, photos of artifacts, and separate fields for frustrations, successful interactions and missed opportunities. Add the list of candidate patterns and tensions from debriefs, clearly labeled as hunches. Synthesis then clusters this material into insights rather than reconstructing what happened.
