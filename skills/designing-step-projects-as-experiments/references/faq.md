# FAQ: Designing Step-Projects to Validate Product Ideas

## How long should a step-project be?

Itamar Gilad's rule in the original GIST article is no more than 10 weeks per step-project. Early steps, such as a data check, interviews or a smoke test, are usually much shorter. If a step needs more time, split it into two steps that test different assumptions.

## What is the difference between a step-project and a sprint?

A sprint is a fixed time box for delivery work. A step-project is an experiment with a question and a threshold, and it may span several sprints or take only part of one. Its tasks are planned inside the team's normal sprints.

## Does every idea need an A/B test?

No. Gilad's guidance is to start with cheap validation and move to more expensive methods only if the idea still looks good. Small, low-risk, easily reversed changes may need little more than assessment and data, while new products and pricing changes need rigorous tests.

## What if the result is inconclusive?

Treat it as information about the test. Check whether the sample was too small, the measure was wrong, or the question was unclear. Usually the next step is a better-designed test of the same assumption.

## What happens to an idea after a successful step?

Its Confidence rises, its ICE score is updated, and it earns a larger next step: a more complete version in front of more users for longer. After enough steps, the final step is the launch itself.

## Who owns a step-project?

One named person who is responsible for making the step happen. Gilad suggests putting that person's name on the step's card on the GIST board, and notes it can be a designer, engineer, product manager, data analyst or product marketer.
