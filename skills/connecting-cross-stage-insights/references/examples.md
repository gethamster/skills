# Examples: Connecting Cross-Stage Journey Insights

## A price objection that started in evaluation

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a home solar installer loses many buyers after sending a formal quote. Sales attributes the losses to price. The insight team has a recall survey of recent buyers, website analytics and sales notes.

**Walkthrough:**

The team starts from the buying-stage loss and looks at what lost buyers did during evaluation. Lost buyers were much more likely to have used an online savings calculator from a comparison site, which showed a lower system price than the installer's typical quote. Won buyers more often came through a referral.

The chain reads: buyers formed a low price expectation from a comparison calculator during evaluation, and the formal quote broke it. The recommended action is to publish realistic price ranges earlier and explain what the calculator leaves out. The evaluation content team takes ownership, since the fix sits in their stage.

## A strong latent brand that stalls in evaluation

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a well-known university runs a postgraduate program with high name recognition but fewer applications than expected. It has brand tracking data, applicant surveys and interview notes with people who looked at the program and did not apply.

**Walkthrough:**

The team links the stages on a segment key, working professionals versus recent graduates. Working professionals consider the program in the latent stage at a high rate but drop it early in evaluation. Their interviews point to the lack of part-time scheduling details on the program pages, which made them assume it was full-time only.

The insight is written as a chain from latent trust to an evaluation information gap. The admissions and web teams share the fix, and the next applicant survey includes a question to test whether the change worked.

## Two chains, one survives

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: an electric bike brand sees buyers drop it at the buying stage in favor of a rival. The team drafts two explanations and tests both.

**Walkthrough:**

The first chain says buyers were put off by the brand's longer delivery time. The second says buyers who test-rode the rival in store formed a preference the brand's online-only model could not answer. Checking timing and lost-versus-won differences, the team finds delivery time was similar among won and lost buyers, while test rides were far more common among lost buyers.

The team reports the second chain as supported and the first as not supported. The recommended action is a test-ride program with partner shops, and the next wave of research will check whether it changes the buying-stage losses.
