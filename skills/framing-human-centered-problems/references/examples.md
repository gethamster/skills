# Examples: Framing Human-Centered Problems

## Reframing a hospital handover problem

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: A product team at a health software company interviews ward nurses about charting. Their first draft reads: nurses need a faster charting app.

**Walkthrough:**

The team notices the need names a solution and the user is generic. Returning to their notes, they find several nurses who wrote observations on scraps of paper during rounds and typed them up at shift end. The fieldwork lead points out that nurses said they did not trust notes written from memory. The team rewrites: a night-shift nurse charting between rounds needs to record observations at the bedside because she distrusts notes reconstructed hours later.

They add that it would be game-changing if records were made in the moment. A quick smoke test produces varied ideas, from voice capture to shared bedside cards, all about the same moment.

## Splitting one frame into two users

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: A city service team researches residents applying for parking permits and drafts a single POV covering all applicants.

**Walkthrough:**

The smoke test produces ideas with nothing in common, a sign the frame is too broad. Reviewing the research, the team sees two distinct groups: residents who renew every year and newcomers applying for the first time. Renewers mostly want to avoid repeating information the city already has. Newcomers are unsure which documents count as proof of address.

The team writes a separate POV for each, with its own insight and game-changer. Each statement now generates focused, varied ideas, and the team decides to ideate on the newcomer frame first because the insight surprised them most.

## Choosing between competing insights

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: A team designing onboarding for a budgeting tool has two candidate insights from interviews with freelancers who abandoned setup.

**Walkthrough:**

One insight is that setup takes too long, which everyone already believed. The other is that freelancers felt embarrassed entering irregular income into forms built for monthly salaries. The team asks which insight overturned an assumption, and only the second one did. They check the notes and find the embarrassment theme in several interviews, stated in the users' own words.

The final POV centers a freelancer with uneven monthly income who needs to see her real cash position without fitting a salary template. The game-changer line describes setup that feels designed for her income pattern, not a faster form.
