# Mapping the Complementary Products Force

> Map the complementary products force: list what customers use alongside your product, rate how necessary and healthy each is, and track who holds power.

## Overview

Mapping the complementary products force is the work that separates the [Six Forces Model](../../methods/six-forces-model/METHOD.md) from Porter's five forces. Complementary products are the goods, services and platforms that make your product more valuable when a customer uses them together. [Brandenburger and Nalebuff](https://www.strategy-business.com/article/12669) define a complementor as "someone who makes your products and services more rather than less valuable," and use Microsoft and Intel as their example. The [Wikipedia definition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complementors) adds that complementors add value to mutual customers.

The five forces mostly describe how value is divided. Complements affect how much value there is to divide, because they change whether customers want the product and how much it is worth to them. When complements are missing, even a strong product can stall. Ron Adner's [HBR article on innovation ecosystems](https://hbr.org/2006/04/match-your-innovation-strategy-to-your-innovation-ecosystem) describes high-definition televisions that failed at first because critical complements such as studio production equipment, signal compression and broadcasting standards "were not developed or adopted in time."

The map this skill produces answers four questions. Which complements do customers depend on to get full value? How healthy is each one: available, good enough, improving or declining? Who controls it, and how much bargaining power does that give them? And how does each complement act on the other forces? The last question matters because Porter sees complements as a factor that works through the other five forces rather than a force of its own, as the [five forces article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porter%27s_five_forces_analysis) reports. A good map records those effects explicitly.

The skill draws on market research techniques used elsewhere in the analysis, applied to a different set of players: customer interviews about what they use alongside the product, partner interviews about their plans, usage and integration data, and public information about complementors. The difference is the question behind them. Rivalry research asks who competes for the customer. Complement research asks who helps you win the customer, and on what terms.

## How It Works

The map starts with an inventory. List everything customers use with your product: hardware, software, integrations, content, services, installers, consultants, training, infrastructure. Customer interviews are the best source, because customers know what they actually rely on; usage and integration data add scale. Also list complements that customers want but cannot get, since a missing complement is often the most important finding.

Each complement is then rated on a few dimensions. Essentiality: how much of your product's value depends on it? Health: is it available where your customers are, is it good enough, and is it improving or declining? Control: do you own it, share it with rivals, or depend on a third party? The ratings use written definitions so different analysts reach the same answer.

Power comes next. A complement that customers cannot do without, supplied by one company, gives that company bargaining power over you; it can act like a powerful supplier even if no money changes hands. A complementor with its own customer base can also expand into your product and become a rival or a substitute. Record, for each critical complement, what the complementor gains from the relationship and what it could do if its interests changed.

The final pass links the complement map to the other forces. Complements that only work with your product raise switching costs and reduce buyer power. Complements that work equally with every rival neutralize each other. A complement ecosystem that is migrating to a substitute is an early warning for the threat of substitutes. These links are the main input the synthesis needs from this skill.

Cooperation is a legitimate response. Brandenburger and Nalebuff's [The Right Game](https://hbr.org/1995/07/the-right-game-use-game-theory-to-shape-strategy) argues early on that "Companies can succeed spectacularly without requiring others to fail," and their later [HBR article on co-opetition](https://hbr.org/2021/01/the-rules-of-co-opetition) covers cooperating with competitors to reach a common goal. For a missing complement, the right move may be to partner, co-invest or build it yourself.

## Step-by-Step Guide

### Step 1: Define the product and customer boundary

State which product and which customer segment the map covers, using the same scope as the rest of the analysis. Complements differ by segment: a large enterprise and a small team may rely on entirely different integrations and services. If segments differ a lot, map them separately. Write down what counts as part of your product, so complements are clearly outside it.

### Step 2: Build the complement inventory

List every product, service and platform customers use alongside yours. Interview recent customers about their setup and workflow, review integration and usage data, and ask sales and support teams what customers ask for. Add complements customers want but cannot get. Group items into categories, such as integrations, services, content and infrastructure.

### Step 3: Rate essentiality, health and control

For each complement, rate how necessary it is to your product's value, how healthy it is (availability, quality, direction) and who controls it. Write the rating definitions first and apply them consistently. Cite the evidence for each rating: interview notes, usage data, public information about the complementor. Mark low-confidence ratings.

### Step 4: Assess the power of critical complementors

For complements rated highly necessary, look at the complementor. Is it one company or many? Does it depend on you, or do you depend on it? Could it change terms, favor a rival or build your product's functionality itself? Record the answers and the evidence. A critical complement from a single powerful provider deserves particular attention.

### Step 5: Map the trajectory of the complementary products force

For each important complement, note where it is heading over the analysis horizon: growing, stable or declining, and whether it is shifting toward a rival or a substitute. Watch complementors' announcements, partnerships and product changes. A complement that is healthy today but losing investment is a future gap.

### Step 6: Link complements to the other forces

For each important complement, write how it affects buyer power, supplier power, rivalry, entry and substitutes. Exclusive complements can raise switching costs; shared ones can level the field; a complementor's move into your market can create a new rival. This step turns the complement map into input the synthesis can use.

### Step 7: Review with partner-facing teams and hand off

Review the map with the people who manage partners, integrations and services, and ask what it misses. Correct errors and add missing complements. Summarize the result: the overall strength of the complementary products force for you and for your rivals, the key dependencies, the gaps and the options for strengthening the ecosystem. Hand it to the synthesis step.

## Best Practices

- Start from the customer's workflow. Customers know which complements they rely on; internal partner lists often miss informal or third-party ones.
- Record missing complements. Adner's [HDTV example](https://hbr.org/2006/04/match-your-innovation-strategy-to-your-innovation-ecosystem) shows that a missing complement can stall a product that is ready.
- Compare your ecosystem with rivals'. The complementary products force affects competitors differently, and that asymmetry is often where strategy starts.
- Watch complementors' incentives. A complementor's interests can change, and a partner today can become a rival or gatekeeper tomorrow.
- Tie each complement to another force. Following Porter's view that complements work through the other forces, a complement with no effect on any force probably does not belong on the critical list.

## Common Mistakes

- **Listing partners instead of complements**: A partner program list includes resellers and co-marketing partners that add no value to the product itself. Keep the map to products and services customers use together with yours.
- **Assuming complements are always allies**: A powerful complementor can take value from you or enter your market. Rate its power and incentives as well as its usefulness.
- **Scoring the force in isolation**: A single number for "complement strength" hides the effects on switching costs, buyer power and substitutes. Record those links.
- **Ignoring gaps**: Only mapping complements that exist misses the ones customers need and cannot get. Ask customers what is missing.
- **Mapping once**: Ecosystems change quickly as complementors grow, merge or change strategy. Revisit the map when a major complementor moves.
