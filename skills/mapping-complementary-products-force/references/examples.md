# Examples: Mapping the Complementary Products Force

## Integrations for a team chat tool

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a company makes a team chat tool for small engineering teams and wants to map its complementary products force before a pricing change.

**Walkthrough:**

Customer interviews and integration data produce an inventory of about 20 complements, grouped into code hosting, issue tracking, calendar and video. Rating essentiality shows that code hosting and issue tracking integrations drive most daily use. Health is good for both. Control is the concern: the most-used code host also offers its own chat features. The team records it as a complementor that could become a rival, and links the finding to buyer power, since customers with deep integrations are less likely to switch.

## A missing complement

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a company makes an electric cargo bike for urban delivery businesses. Sales are slow despite good reviews.

**Walkthrough:**

Interviews with prospects show that the missing complement is local repair service: fleet operators will not buy vehicles they cannot get fixed within a day. The map rates repair service as highly necessary and unhealthy in most target cities. The finding goes to synthesis with options: train independent bike shops, partner with a service network or offer mobile repair. The team notes the parallel with ecosystems where a product is ready before its complements.

## A complementor with too much power

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a small app studio depends on a single app marketplace for nearly all distribution and payments.

**Walkthrough:**

The map rates the marketplace as necessary, healthy and controlled by one company. The power assessment notes that the marketplace sets fees and review rules and can promote competing apps. The team treats it as acting like a powerful supplier and records the link. Synthesis options include building a direct web channel for some customers and diversifying to a second marketplace.
