# Examples: Defining Goals for a GO Product Roadmap

## Turning a feature list into goals

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a team building a language-learning app has a draft roadmap of four quarters, each listing features: offline mode, a speaking tutor, streak rewards, and a family plan. Leadership asks what the roadmap is meant to achieve, and nobody can answer in one sentence.

**Walkthrough:**

The product manager asks why each feature matters. Offline mode matters because learners on commutes drop off when they lose signal, so the outcome is helping commuting learners keep practising. Streak rewards and the speaking tutor both serve the same underlying need, helping learners build a daily habit. The family plan is about revenue from households.

The team rewrites the roadmap as three compound goals: help new learners build a daily habit and grow the active base; help commuting learners keep practising and reduce early churn; help families learn together and open a new revenue stream. Each feature now sits under the goal it serves, and one feature that served none of them is moved back to the backlog as an idea.

## Using KPIs on a mature product

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: an established expense-reporting tool has a stable strategy and a loyal customer base. Its KPIs show engagement drifting down for three months and the bug count creeping up, while revenue is flat.

**Walkthrough:**

Deriving goals from the strategy produces nothing new, since the strategy has not changed. The product manager instead looks at the KPIs. Two candidates emerge: make submitting an expense simpler to recover engagement, and reduce technical debt in the approval module to cut the bug rate and speed up future changes.

Both goals are checked against the strategy's needs and business goals and pass. The team estimates the debt goal at about two months and the simplification goal at about a quarter. Because the bug rate is making the simplification work slower, the debt goal goes first. The roadmap now has two columns that address the actual problems the numbers show.

## Right-sizing an oversized goal

**Scenario:**

Illustrative scenario: a team building a clinic scheduling product writes a single goal for the next nine months: help clinics fill every appointment slot and make scheduling a paid add-on.

**Walkthrough:**

In the workshop the development team points out that the goal is too big to guide anyone. The group splits it. The first goal helps clinics reduce no-shows with reminders and grows the base of active clinics. The second helps clinics fill cancelled slots from a waitlist. The third turns scheduling into a paid add-on for clinics that already rely on it.

Each subgoal fits within roughly a quarter and can be measured on its own. The ordering follows a narrative: first prove value with reminders, then deepen it with the waitlist, then charge for it. The team records the rationale next to each goal so the next review can see why the order was chosen.
