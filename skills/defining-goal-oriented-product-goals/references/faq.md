# FAQ: Defining Goals for a GO Product Roadmap

## What is the difference between a goal and a feature?

A goal states the outcome or benefit the product should create, such as helping users build a habit or reducing development cost. A feature is a capability that might help achieve it. Pichler's test is to ask why a proposed goal matters: if the answer reveals a deeper benefit, the original statement was a feature in disguise. Features belong in the features row, under the goal they serve.

## Where should roadmap goals come from?

From the product strategy first. Pichler recommends breaking its user needs and business goals into smaller, specific steps. For mature products, where the strategy changes little, the product's KPIs are a better source of candidate goals, as long as those goals still support the strategy. Goals dictated by individual stakeholders tend to produce an incoherent product.

## How big should a goal be?

Pichler's guideline for a roadmap covering about a year is that goals should take no less than six weeks and no more than four months. Goals smaller than that resemble sprint goals, and bigger ones offer too little guidance. Quarterly goals are common but not required, so size each goal to the work it needs.

## Can I have more than one goal per timeframe?

Pichler recommends one goal at a time because it focuses the team, reduces resource conflicts, and makes progress easier to track. He treats an occasional second goal as acceptable but warns against making it the norm. If you regularly need several, consider shorter timeframes or look at why stakeholders cannot agree on one.

## Should technical work appear as a goal?

Yes, when it creates value. Pichler lists removing technical debt among his sample goals, framed by its benefit, such as reducing development cost or future-proofing the product. Writing it as an outcome makes the trade-off against user-facing goals explicit instead of hiding the work.
