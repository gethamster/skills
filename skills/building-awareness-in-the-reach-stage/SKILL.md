## Overview

Building awareness in the Reach stage is the work at the top of the [RACE Framework](../../methods/race-framework/METHOD.md) funnel. Dave Chaffey defines Reach as building awareness and visibility of your brand, products and services on other websites and in offline media, in order to drive visits to your own web presences, and as maximizing reach over time to create multiple interactions using paid, owned and earned media touchpoints ([Chaffey: RACE marketing model](https://www.davechaffey.com/digital-marketing-glossary/race-marketing-planning-model/)). This skill covers choosing the channels for that job, planning them, and judging whether they work.

Two ideas in that definition shape the skill. The first is "over time". Reach is a sustained presence built through repeated contact across several touchpoints, which is why a single campaign rarely fixes an awareness problem. The second is that the purpose of Reach is to start interactions. Visibility that never turns into a visit, a search for your brand or a first interaction does not feed the rest of RACE.

Channels in the Reach stage fall into three groups. Paid media is what you buy: search ads, social ads, display, sponsorships, print and broadcast. Owned media is what you control: your site, content, email list and social profiles. Earned media is what others give you: press coverage, reviews, shares and recommendations. Chaffey stresses inbound techniques such as search, social media, content and email marketing as vital for the cut-through Reach needs. Offline media belongs here too.

Reach also sits inside a longer debate about brand building versus short-term activation. In [The Long and The Short of It (2013)](https://ipa.co.uk/knowledge/ipa-blog/the-next-chapter-for-the-long-and-the-short-of-it), Les Binet and Peter Field found the optimum budget ratio was 60:40 between brand building and activation, according to the IPA. That finding does not map one to one onto RACE stages, but it is a useful check when a Reach plan is made entirely of activity designed to convert this week.

The output is a Reach plan: audience definition, channel mix across paid, owned and earned media, creative and content themes, budget and a timeline, and KPIs that measure both arrival and quality.

## How It Works

The Reach plan starts with the audience. Define who you need to reach in terms that channels can target: roles, interests, problems, the words they search for, and the places they already spend attention. Without that, channel choice comes down to habit.

Channel selection then weighs each option on four questions: does it reach the defined audience, can it deliver repeated contact over time, what does it cost for the quality of visitor it brings, and can it be measured. Organic search, content and earned coverage usually build slowly and compound. Paid channels can switch on quickly and stop as soon as spending stops. Most plans need both.

The Smart Insights campaign guide adds practical rules for the media plan. It recommends a visual timeline or Gantt chart showing when each communication goes live and who owns it, planning repeated communications and several different touchpoints rather than relying on a single channel, refreshing creative across a long campaign, and thinking about how offline media will drive people to digital channels ([Smart Insights: campaign plan](https://www.smartinsights.com/digital-marketing-strategy/organize-campaign-plan-race-framework/)). Budget belongs in the same plan, channel by channel.

Measurement has two layers. Arrival KPIs show whether Reach is happening: visits by channel, new users, impressions, share of search, and brand searches. Smart Insights lists brand searches and paid media impressions as typical awareness measures in its [SMART objectives guide](https://www.smartinsights.com/goal-setting-evaluation/goals-kpis/define-smart-marketing-objectives/). Quality KPIs show whether the visitors are the right ones: the share of each channel's visitors who go on to an Act interaction, such as viewing a product or signing up.

| Channel type | Examples | Strength | Watch for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Paid | Search ads, social ads, display, sponsorship | Fast, targetable, scalable | Stops when spend stops |
| Owned | Site content, SEO, email, social profiles | Compounds over time | Slow to build |
| Earned | Press, reviews, shares, referrals | Credible, low direct cost | Hard to control |
| Offline | Events, print, broadcast, outdoor | Broad reach | Harder to link to visits |

The quality layer is what connects Reach to the rest of RACE. A channel that delivers cheap visits with no interactions is a poor Reach channel for your business, however good its reach numbers look. Review Reach and Act numbers together, by channel, and move budget toward the channels whose visitors interact.

## Step-by-Step Guide

### Step 1: Set the Reach baseline

Pull recent numbers for each current Reach channel: visits, new users, impressions, cost, and the share of visitors who go on to an Act interaction. Note which channels you cannot measure. Record brand search volume as a rough indicator of awareness. This baseline is what the plan will be judged against.

### Step 2: Define the audience in channel terms

Describe the audience by role or situation, the problems they are trying to solve, the terms they search for, and where they spend attention online and offline. Use customer interviews, search data and your own analytics. Split the audience into segments only where they need different channels or messages. Write the definition down so every channel owner targets the same people.

### Step 3: Choose the channel mix

List candidate channels across paid, owned, earned and offline media, following the touchpoint types in [Chaffey's definition](https://www.davechaffey.com/digital-marketing-glossary/race-marketing-planning-model/). Score each on audience fit, ability to deliver repeated contact, cost per quality visitor and measurability. Pick a mix that combines channels that build over time with channels that deliver quickly. Drop channels that reach the wrong audience, even if they are cheap.

### Step 4: Plan content, creative and timing

For each channel, decide the messages and formats that will earn attention and lead toward an interaction. Put every activity on a timeline with an owner, as the [Smart Insights campaign guide](https://www.smartinsights.com/digital-marketing-strategy/organize-campaign-plan-race-framework/) recommends, and plan how creative will be refreshed over a long campaign. Make sure each piece points somewhere useful: a relevant page, a sign-up, a next piece of content.

### Step 5: Set Reach KPIs and tracking

Set arrival KPIs and quality KPIs for each channel, with targets. Tag campaign links consistently so visits can be attributed to channels. Make sure the Act interactions you use for the quality KPIs are tracked as events in analytics. Agree how often each KPI will be reviewed.

### Step 6: Launch and test

Launch the plan in stages where you can, starting with the channels you are most confident in. Test messages, formats and audiences within each channel, changing one thing at a time. Watch quality as well as volume in the first weeks, since a channel can grow visits while its visitors stop interacting.

### Step 7: Reallocate toward channels whose visitors interact

At each review, compare channels on cost per quality visitor, not only on visits. Move budget toward channels that bring visitors who go on to Act, and cut or fix those that do not. Feed what you learn about the audience back into the plan. Share findings with the Act owner, because the landing experience affects how Reach channels perform.

## Best Practices

- Judge Reach channels on the visitors they bring, measured by what those visitors do in Act, as well as on volume.
- Plan for repeated contact across several touchpoints. Chaffey describes Reach as maximizing reach over time with paid, owned and earned touchpoints ([Chaffey](https://www.davechaffey.com/digital-marketing-glossary/race-marketing-planning-model/)).
- Balance quick channels with compounding ones. Paid media switches on fast; search, content and earned coverage keep working after the budget stops.
- Keep some brand-building activity. Binet and Field's 60:40 finding on brand building versus activation is a reminder that a plan made only of short-term activation may miss longer-term effects ([IPA](https://ipa.co.uk/knowledge/ipa-blog/the-next-chapter-for-the-long-and-the-short-of-it)).
- Put the media plan on one timeline with owners, so gaps and overlaps are visible.
- Track brand searches over time as a simple signal that awareness is growing.

## Common Mistakes

- **Chasing volume**: Buying cheap traffic raises Reach numbers and lowers everything after it. Pair every volume KPI with a quality KPI.
- **One channel only**: Relying on a single channel leaves the plan exposed to price rises and rule changes. Build a mix.
- **Stopping and starting**: Reach built in short bursts fades between them. Plan a sustained presence with periodic campaigns on top.
- **Sending visitors nowhere**: Awareness content that has no next step wastes the visit. Link every Reach activity to an Act interaction.
- **Ignoring offline media**: Events, print and broadcast can drive visits too. Plan how they will send people to digital channels and measure it where you can.
